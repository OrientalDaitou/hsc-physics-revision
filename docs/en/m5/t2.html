Circular Motion: Forces

Explained from zero

This chapter is about the forces on objects moving in a circle. The core idea is only one sentence: net force towards the centre = mv²/r. The only hard part is finding which real force does this job in each situation.

Order for revising with students

  1. First spend 3 minutes on "Know these 4 first" below, especially splitting a force into a vertical part and a horizontal part.
  2. Go through 2.1 → 2.3 in order. For each situation, first let the student draw the free-body diagram and say which force points to the centre. Then open the animation to compare.
  3. Finish with the "Quick quiz", then look at "Common mistakes".
Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Velocity is a vector: it has a size (the speed) and a direction. If only the direction changes, the velocity has still changed.
  2. Newton's first and second laws: with no net force, an object moves in a straight line at a constant speed. Fnet = ma: the acceleration points the same way as the net force.
  3. Splitting a force: when a force is at angle θ to the vertical, its vertical part is F cos θ and its horizontal part is F sin θ. Horizontal part ÷ vertical part = sin θ/cos θ = tan θ.
  4. Units: km h−1 ÷ 3.6 = m s−1 (example: 72 km h−1 = 20 m s−1); rpm (turns each minute) ÷ 60 = f; g = 9.8 m s−2.
2.1

Uniform circular motion and centripetal force

Big ideaAn object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. The acceleration points to the centre. A net force towards the centre, the centripetal force, causes it.

  • Uniform circular motion: moving around a circle at a constant speed.
  • Velocity is a vector and its direction changes all the time. So the velocity changes all the time: the object accelerates. It is like driving around a circle: the number on the speedometer stays the same, but the front of the car keeps turning.
  • The velocity is always along the tangent. A tangent touches the circle at one point, at 90° to the radius.
  • Take two velocities a moment apart, v1 (earlier) and v2 (later), and draw them tail to tail. The change Δv between them points to the centre.
  • So the acceleration also points to the centre. It is called the centripetal acceleration ac. "Centripetal" means "centre-seeking".
  • One lap is the circumference 2πr, done in one period T: the time for one full turn, in s. So v = 2πr/T.
  • ac grows with v²: a faster object turns its velocity arrow more quickly, and the arrow is longer. Both get bigger, so v is squared.
  • At the same speed, a smaller r gives a bigger ac. A sharp bend is harder to turn than a wide one.
  • By Newton's second law, the net force (the total of all the forces) also points to the centre: Fnet = mac = mv²/r. This net force towards the centre is called the centripetal force Fc.

2.1

Frequency, angular velocity and "centrifugal force"

Big ideaThe centripetal force is not a new kind of force: real forces always do this job. The feeling of being thrown outwards on a turn is not a force pushing you. It is your body trying to go straight.

What Fc depends on

  • Fc ∝ m, Fc ∝ v², Fc ∝ 1/r. ∝ means "is proportional to".
  • If v becomes 2 times as big, Fc becomes 4 times as big (2² = 4). If v stays the same and r becomes 2 times as big, Fc becomes half as big.
  • You can also find ac from the period T: ac = 4π²r/T² (put v = 2πr/T into ac = v²/r). Note: if T stays the same, a bigger r gives a bigger ac. This does not contradict the point above: here v gets bigger too.

Frequency and angular velocity

  • Frequency f: turns each second, in Hz. f = 1/T (on the sheet).
  • Convert first: km h−1 ÷ 3.6 gives m s−1; rpm (revolutions per minute, turns each minute) ÷ 60 gives f.
  • Angular velocity ω: the angle turned each second. ω = Δθ/t (on the sheet), in rad s−1.
  • One full turn = 2π radians (rad) = 360°. So ω = 2π/T (not on the sheet).

Real forces do the job of the centripetal force

The centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is more like a "job" that real forces do. These are the common ones, and sometimes several forces add up to do it:

SituationWhat provides the centripetal force
Ball whirled on a stringTension
Car on a flat bendFriction
Banked track, rotorNormal force
Moon or satellite around the EarthGravity

There is no "centrifugal force"

  • No force pushes you outwards on a turn.
  • By inertia (the tendency to keep moving in a straight line) your body tries to go straight. The seat, seatbelt and door push you inwards, so you turn with the car.
  • When a car turns sharply, you feel "thrown" towards the door. Really, the car turns inwards while you still try to go straight. So the door comes up against you and pushes you inwards.
  • So "centrifugal force" is not a real force. Do not draw it on a free-body diagram.
  • If the string breaks, the inward force is gone. By Newton's first law, the ball moves in a straight line along the tangent, not straight outwards along the radius. You can cut the string in Animation 1 to see this.
2.2

Real situations: the forces that provide Fc · Strings

Big ideaIn every situation, find the real forces and split them into a part towards the centre and a vertical part. The part towards the centre adds up to mv²/r.

  • Tension FT: the pull of a string, rope or chain, along its length.
  • Normal force FN: the push of a surface on an object, at 90° to the surface.
  • Component: the part of a force that acts in one chosen direction.

Method: do this for every question

  1. Draw a free-body diagram: only this object, with one arrow for each real force. No Fc arrow.
  2. Split each force into two parts. With θ measured from the vertical: the vertical part is F cos θ and the horizontal part is F sin θ.
  3. The parts towards the centre add up to mv²/r. The vertical parts (horizontal circle) add up to 0.

Conical pendulum

  • Conical pendulum: a ball hanging on a string and moving in a horizontal circle. The string sweeps out a cone.
  • Only two forces act on the ball: FT and mg.
  • The centre of the circle is directly below the pivot (the fixed point that the string hangs from), not at the pivot. So the radius is r = L sin θ (L is the string length).
  • Towards the centre: FT sin θ = mv²/r.
  • Vertically: FT cos θ = mg, so FT = mg/cos θ > mg. The tension is always bigger than the weight.
  • It also uses the formula from the next section, tan θ = v²/(rg): the faster it spins, the bigger θ, and the higher the ball swings.
Conical pendulum

The centre of the circle is directly below the pivot: r = L sin θ.

Swing-ride chair

The chair hangs from a spinning frame: r = d + L sin θ, where d = the distance from the axis to the top of the chain.

Vertical circle: a swing

  • Vertical circle: an upright circle, such as a swing. It is not uniform: the speed keeps changing.
  • But at each point, the net force towards the centre is still mv²/r.
  • Lowest point of a swing: FT − mg = mv²/r. The tension is bigger than the weight, so you feel heavier.
  • Top of the circle, on a string: FT + mg = mv²/r.

You can also derive it: count forces towards the centre as positive; they add up to mv²/r. At the bottom the centre is above: FT points up, towards the centre, and mg points down, away from the centre, so you subtract. At the top both forces point down, both towards the centre, so you add.
Note: neither is on the sheet. The booklet's summary table puts them under Ch. 3, and Chapter 3 covers them in detail.

2.2

Tilted surfaces: bowls and banked tracks

Big ideaWhen a bend is built higher on the outside and lower on the inside, the road's normal force tilts towards the centre. At the design speed, its horizontal part is exactly enough to be the centripetal force, and no friction at all is needed.

  • Banked track: a bend that is higher on the outside and lower on the inside, like the bends of a cycling track.
  • Bank angle θ: the angle between the road and the horizontal.
  • FN is at 90° to the road, so it also tilts from the vertical by the same θ, towards the centre.
  • Design speed: at this speed, no friction is needed.

The three cases below are all seen from behind the car, with the centre of the bend to the left:

Exactly at the design speed

No friction is needed. There are only FN and mg, and the horizontal part of FN is exactly enough to be the centripetal force.

Slower than the design speed

The car tends to slide down, so friction acts up the slope.

Faster than the design speed

The car tends to slide up, so friction acts down the slope.

  • At the design speed: FN sin θ = mv²/r, FN cos θ = mg, so FN = mg/cos θ > mg.
  • Banking means less friction is needed (as long as the car is not going very slowly). So banked bends are safer when the road is wet and slippery. When the car goes very slowly, a banked road needs more friction: you can see this in Animation 3.
  • A marble circling inside a bowl: the analysis is exactly the same. FN points to the bowl's centre. r is the horizontal distance from the marble to the centre line, not the bowl's radius.

2.2

Friction: flat bends and the rotor ride

Big ideaOn a flat bend, friction is the centripetal force. Friction has a limit, so there is a fastest speed. In a rotor, the wall's normal force is the centripetal force and friction only holds the rider up, so there is a slowest speed.

  • Static friction Ff: friction that stops two surfaces sliding. It is only as big as needed, up to a limit: Ff ≤ μFN (not on the sheet).
  • Coefficient of friction μ: it has no unit and says how strongly the two surfaces grip. About 0.8 on a dry road, about 0.1 on ice.

Note: "≤" means static friction is only as big as needed, up to μFN. If more is needed, sliding starts.

Flat bend

  • The tyres push the road outwards. By Newton's third law, the road pushes the tyres inwards.
  • This friction is the centripetal force: Ff = mv²/r. Vertically: FN = mg.
  • Free-body diagram (seen from behind the car, centre to the left): FN points up, mg points down, Ff points left, towards the centre.
  • The road can give at most μmg. If the bend requires more, the car skids towards the outside. On ice μ is small, so it is very easy to skid.

Rotor

  • Rotor: a big drum spins and then the floor drops away. The riders stay on the wall and do not fall. It is like clothes staying on the side of a washing machine drum during the spin cycle.
  • The wall's normal force is horizontal, towards the axis. It is the centripetal force: FN = mv²/r.
  • Static friction acts up the wall and holds up the weight: Ff = mg.
  • Riders stay up only if mg ≤ μFN. So there is a slowest safe speed.
  • Faster: the friction limit grows, but the actual friction stays mg.
Flat bend

Friction = centripetal force. The faster the car, the more friction is needed. So there is a fastest speed vmax.

Rotor

FN = centripetal force, friction = mg. The faster the drum, the bigger the friction limit. So there is a slowest speed vmin.

When to use: vmax for a flat bend; vmin for a rotor (after the floor is taken away). Mass makes no difference.
Where it comes from (you can also derive it): at the limiting speed, friction needed = largest friction. Flat bend: mvmax²/r = μmg, so vmax² = μgr. Rotor: mg = μFN = μmvmin²/r, so vmin² = gr/μ.
Trap: μFN is the largest friction, not the actual friction.

2.3

Key experiments

Investigating Fc = mv²/r with a whirling stopper

Aim
To investigate how Fc depends on speed (Part A), radius (Part B) and mass (Part C). The syllabus requires this experiment.
Method
Hold a vertical tube in your hand, with a string through the tube. Tie a stopper (a rubber stopper, mass m) to the top end and whirl it in a horizontal circle. Hang a mass (mass M) from the bottom end. The hanging mass stays still, so the tension provides the centripetal force: Fc = Mg. A paper clip below the tube keeps the radius r = L fixed. Time 20 turns (this gives less reaction-time error). Do this three times and take the average. T = time ÷ 20; v = 2πL/T.
Results
Part A: Fc against v² is a straight line through the origin (0, 0). The gradient of the line of best fit (the straight line closest to all the points) agrees with m/r within 1 %. Parts B and C (expected from theory): v² against r and v² against 1/m should also be straight lines through the origin.
Conclusion
Part A: Fc ∝ v². This supports Fc = mv²/r. If the Part B and C graphs are also straight, then at a fixed speed Fc ∝ 1/r and Fc ∝ m. (Fc against 1/T² is also straight; Fc against v is a curve.)
Significance
It confirms Fc = mv²/r with a measurable force (a hanging weight). Engineers use the same model for bends, banked tracks, rides and satellite orbits.

The three kinds of variable in Part A:

  • Independent variable (changed on purpose): M.
  • Dependent variable (measured): the time for 20 turns.
  • Controlled variables (kept the same): m, L, and a horizontal circle.

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. Constant speed in a circle is still acceleration, because the direction changes. ac = v²/r points to the centre. Also v = 2πr/T, f = 1/T and ω = Δθ/t.
  2. Fc = mv²/r is the net force towards the centre, given by a real force: tension, normal force, friction, gravity. There is no centrifugal force.
  3. Draw only real forces. The parts towards the centre add up to mv²/r; the vertical parts (horizontal circle) add up to 0.
  4. Conical pendulum or banked road (design speed): tan θ = v²/(rg).

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. A ball moves in a circle at a constant speed. The size of its velocity never changes. Is it accelerating?

Yes, it is accelerating. Velocity is a vector and its direction keeps changing, so the velocity is changing. The acceleration ac points to the centre.

2. A ball is being whirled on a string, and the string suddenly breaks. Which way does the ball go?

Along the tangent at the moment the string breaks, in a straight line. The inward force is gone, so by Newton's first law the ball moves in a straight line. It does not fly straight outwards along the radius, because there is no outward force at all.

3. A 0.5 kg ball moves with radius 2 m and speed 4 m s−1. What are the centripetal acceleration and centripetal force? What if the speed becomes 8 m s−1?

ac = 8 m s−2, Fc = 4 N; with the speed doubled, Fc = 16 N. ac = v²/r = 4²/2 = 8 m s−2. Fc = mac = 0.5 × 8 = 4 N. If v becomes 2 times as big, Fc becomes 4 times as big: 0.5 × 8²/2 = 16 N.

4. Conical pendulum: a 2 kg ball, with the string at 60° to the vertical. What is the tension?

FT = 39.2 N. Vertical forces balance: FT cos 60° = mg, FT = 2 × 9.8 / 0.5 = 39.2 N. That is 2 times mg = 19.6 N. The tension is always bigger than mg.

5. A car on a banked track is going exactly at the design speed. Is friction needed? What if it goes faster than the design speed?

No friction is needed at the design speed; when faster, friction acts down the slope. At the design speed, the horizontal part of FN is exactly enough to be the centripetal force. When the car goes faster, it tends to slide up, so friction acts down the slope.

6. A flat bend has radius 50 m. On a dry road μ = 0.8. What is the fastest speed? What about on ice (μ = 0.1)?

About 19.8 m s−1 on a dry road, 7.0 m s−1 on ice. vmax = √(μgr) = √(0.8 × 9.8 × 50) = √392 ≈ 19.8 m s−1 (about 71 km h−1). On ice: √(0.1 × 9.8 × 50) = √49 = 7.0 m s−1.

7. A rotor has radius 2 m, and μ = 0.5 between the rider and the wall. What is the slowest speed? Does a heavier rider need it to spin faster?

About 6.3 m s−1; no, mass makes no difference. vmin = √(gr/μ) = √(9.8 × 2 / 0.5) = √39.2 ≈ 6.3 m s−1. In the derivation m cancels: the friction needed, mg, and the largest friction, μmv²/r, are both proportional to m.

8. In the whirling stopper experiment, what does the centripetal force equal? Why time 20 turns?

Fc = Mg (the weight of the hanging mass). The hanging mass stays still, so the tension in the string equals Mg, and this tension is the stopper's centripetal force. Timing 20 turns and dividing by 20 gives less reaction-time error.
