Projectile Motion

Explained from zero

This chapter is about how thrown things fly. There is only one secret: split the motion into two directions, horizontal and vertical. Work them out separately. They share only the time t. Once it is split, each direction is a straight-line motion you have already learned.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. A vector has a size and a direction. It is drawn as an arrow. An arrow at an angle can be split into a horizontal arrow plus a vertical arrow.
  2. In a right-angled triangle: cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse, sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse. Set your calculator to degree mode.
  3. Displacement s and velocity both have a sign. The sign is the direction. Speed has only a size, with no direction.
  4. Newton's second law: Fnet = ma. Fnet is the net force: all the forces added together. No net force means no acceleration, so the velocity does not change. Acceleration due to gravity is g = 9.8 m s−2, downwards: the velocity changes by 9.8 m s−1 every second.
1.1

The projectile model

Big ideaA thrown ball moves forwards and up and down at the same time. The two motions do not affect each other. The forward velocity stays the same. Gravity changes the up-and-down velocity by the same amount every second.

  • Projectile: an object that moves through the air after it is thrown or launched, with no engine pushing it.
  • Component: the part of a vector (an arrow quantity) that points in one direction.
  • The projectile model (a simple description we can calculate with) has two assumptions: zero air resistance; and g = 9.8 m s−2 is constant, straight down.

Why do the two directions not affect each other?

  • Gravity pulls only downwards: there is no horizontal force. No horizontal force means no horizontal acceleration (Newton's second law), so vx stays the same.
  • Vertically there is only gravity, so vy changes by 9.8 m s−1 every second.
  • The two motions are independent: they do not affect each other and share only the time t.
  • A comparison: imagine two shadows. The shadow on the ground only moves forwards at a steady speed. The shadow on the wall only moves up and down. The two shadows use the same stopwatch.
A: dropped

Let go at the table edge. It falls straight down.

B: thrown horizontally at the same moment

Thrown forwards from the same height at the same moment. Look every 0.1 s: A and B are at the same height, and they land at the same time.

B's forward velocity does not make it fall more slowly, and it does not make it fall more quickly. Animation 1 shows this.

  • The distance forwards is proportional to t. The distance fallen grows with t². So the path (the trajectory) is a parabola: a curve symmetrical about its top.
  • Signs: the origin is the launch point. Up and forwards are positive. So ay = −g = −9.8 m s−2 for the whole flight, even at the top.

1.1

The simplest case: a horizontal launch

Big ideaIn a horizontal launch uy = 0, so vertically the fall is like a drop from rest. This means the time in the air depends only on the height.

  • Launched horizontally off a table, a ledge or a cliff: the launch velocity is horizontal, so uy = 0.
  • Vertically, the fall is like a drop from rest. However fast you throw it forwards, this does not change how long the fall takes.
  • The time in the air depends only on the height. Four times the height doubles the time (because √4 = 2). At the same speed, it also doubles the range.

1.2

Maximum height, time of flight and range

Big ideaAt the top of the path the vertical velocity is zero for an instant. This gives the maximum height and, on level ground, the time of flight and the range.

  • Maximum height hmax: the greatest height above the launch point.
  • Time of flight tflight: the time from launch until landing.
  • Range R: the horizontal distance from the launch point to the landing point.
At the top

Only vy = 0. The ball still moves forwards at vx, so the velocity = vx, not 0.

Acceleration

It is g down at every point, never zero. The same is true at the top.

  • On level ground the path is symmetrical: the way down is like a mirror image of the way up. Time up = time down.

  • Double u (same angle): the times double; the height and the range become 4 times as big (because the formulas have u²).
1.2

Range and the 45° rule

Big ideaThe range needs two things: horizontal speed and time in the air. On level ground, 45° gives the best mix of the two, so the range is longest.

  • A low angle: large horizontal speed, but the ball lands quickly. A high angle: a long time in the air, but the ball moves forwards slowly.
  • 45° gives the longest range: when 2θ = 90°, sin 2θ = 1, its largest value. Then Rmax = u²/g.
  • This is true only on level ground with no air resistance.
  • Complementary angles: two angles that add to 90°, such as 30° and 60°. They give the same range. The steeper one goes higher and stays in the air longer.
  • A comparison: watering plants with a hose. Hold the hose flat and the water lands quickly. Point it at the sky and the water falls back at your feet. Hold it at an angle and the water goes far.
30°

Lower, and lands sooner. The range is the same as for 60°.

60°

Higher and longer in the air, but it moves forwards slowly. The range is the same as for 30°.

The range still comes from R = u² sin 2θ / g above. There is no new formula here. In Animation 3, first keep the launch height h at 0 and look at 30° and 60°. The next section raises the launch point.

1.2

Launching from a height

Big ideaWhen the launch point is above the landing point, sy = −h at the moment of landing. Solve a quadratic equation for t and keep the positive solution.

  • Launch height h: how far the launch point is above the landing point. So sy = −h at landing.

Four steps to find the time

  1. Write the equation for the vertical motion: −h = uyt + ½ayt², with ay = −9.8.
  2. Write it as 4.9t² − uyt − h = 0. This is a quadratic equation: it has a t² term, a t term and a number.
  3. A = 4.9, B = −uy, C = −h. Put them into t = (−B ± √(B² − 4AC)) / 2A.
  4. You get two roots (solutions of the equation). Keep the positive one. The negative one is before the launch, so it has no meaning. Then find the range R = uxt.
  • The level-ground tflight and R formulas do not apply here.
  • A target above the launch point gives two positive roots: one on the way up, one on the way down.
  • Higher launch point: longer time and longer range. But hmax (measured from the launch point) stays the same.
  • From a height, the best angle is less than 45°. The extra drop gives the ball more time in the air. So it is better to put more of the speed into ux. Try a larger launch height h in Animation 3.

1.2

Final velocity

Big ideaThe final velocity is the velocity just before landing. Give its size and its direction.

  • Horizontally: vx = ux.
  • Vertically: vy = uy + ayt, or vy² = uy² + 2aysy. vy is negative when the ball moves down.
  • Then combine the two parts: use Pythagoras' theorem for the speed and tan for the direction. α is the angle below the horizontal.
  • On level ground: sy = 0, so vy = −uy. Landing speed = launch speed and the angle = launch angle, but below the horizontal.

1.3

Energy in a flight

Big ideaWith no air resistance, kinetic plus potential energy stays the same. This gives the speed at any height directly, without the time or the angle.

  • On the way up, kinetic energy (K = ½mv²) turns into gravitational potential energy (ΔU = mgΔh). On the way down it turns back.
  • The total stays the same: this is conservation of energy. It is like a roller coaster: slower as it climbs, faster as it drops.
  • For a ball launched at an angle, K is smallest at the top, but it is not zero. The ball still moves forwards at ux, so Ktop = ½mux².
  • Kinetic energy gained = potential energy lost. Both sides have m, so it cancels.

1.3

Air resistance: where the model fails

Big ideaReal air pulls the ball backwards. So a real ball does not go as far or as high, and its path is no longer symmetrical.

  • Drag (air resistance) is an extra force opposite to the velocity. The faster the object, the larger the drag, and it grows quickly.
Model: no air resistance

The path is symmetrical. On level ground 45° goes farthest. Landing speed = launch speed. K + U stays the same.

Real: with air resistance

The range is shorter (drag slows the horizontal motion). The top is lower (on the way up, drag and gravity both pull down). Not symmetrical: the way down is steeper and takes longer. The ball lands slower. The best angle is below 45°.

  • The model works well for small, dense (heavy for their size), slow objects over short distances. Drag hardly changes a heavy object's motion. For example, a small steel ball.
  • The model works badly for light, large or very fast objects. For example, a feather, or a golf ball just after it is hit.

1.4

Predicting motion: uniform velocity and constant acceleration

Big ideaStraight-line motion has only two models: uniform velocity and constant acceleration. A projectile uses both at once: uniform velocity horizontally, constant acceleration vertically, with the same t.

  • Prediction: a result calculated from a model before it is measured.
Uniform velocity

The same speed in the same direction. Net force = 0 (Newton's first law). s = ut, v = u. The displacement–time graph is a straight line, gradient = velocity.

Constant acceleration

The velocity changes by the same amount every second. A constant net force (Fnet = ma), as in free fall (gravity only). The velocity–time graph is a straight line: gradient = acceleration, area under it = displacement.

Choosing the model: separately for each direction

Net force in this directionWhich model
No net forceUniform velocity
A constant net forceConstant acceleration
A changing force (for example, large drag)Neither is exact

Never use s = ut for a falling object. Its velocity keeps changing.

Two special cases

  • Straight up (ux = 0): time up = time down. The ball returns to your hand at its launch speed. Below the top it passes each height twice (two positive values of t).
  • From a moving vehicle: after release, the object keeps the vehicle's velocity. This is inertia. A ball tossed straight up from a cart moving at uniform velocity lands back in the cart. Seen from the ground, its path is a parabola. A parcel dropped from a plane at uniform velocity stays directly below the plane.
  • A comparison: on a smoothly moving bus, toss your keys upwards. The keys still land back in your hand. They do not fly to the back of the bus.

How to predict: three things

  1. One origin.
  2. One model for each object and each direction.
  3. One clock t. "At the same place" gives one equation in t. Solve it for t.
1.5

Key experiments

Range of a ball launched horizontally

Aim
To collect primary data (data you measure) to validate (test) R = u√(2h/g): R against u should be a straight line through the origin, with gradient √(2h/g).
Method
A steel ball leaves the ramp's horizontal end, so uy = 0. A light gate times the ball blocking its beam: u = diameter ÷ Δt. Carbon paper marks the landing. R is measured from the plumb line (a string with a weight on the end, hanging straight down). Change u (5 speeds, 5 trials each). Keep h and the ball the same. h is how far the end of the ramp is above the floor.
Results
R against u (h = 0.900 m) is a straight line very close to the origin, with gradient 0.423 s (model: 0.429 s). The value of g found from the gradient is about 3 % above 9.8 m s−2.
Conclusion
R is proportional to u: double u and R doubles. The data validate the model (for a dense, slow ball). Every R is about 2 % low: this is a systematic error (same direction each time), not a model failure.
Significance
The fall time is the same at every speed, so R grows in step with u. This shows that the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The R–u graph in Animation 1 shows this.

Other ways to validate the model

R–sin 2θ
Fixed u, level ground: a straight line through the origin. The largest R is at 45°; 30° and 60° give equal R.
R–√h
Fixed u, horizontal launch: a straight line through the origin.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. From the same height, A is dropped and B is thrown horizontally at the same moment. Which lands first?

They land at the same time. Both have uy = 0 and ay = −9.8 m s−2. The horizontal velocity does not affect the vertical motion.

2. A ball is launched at an angle. At the top, is its velocity 0? What about its acceleration?

The velocity is not 0: it equals vx. The acceleration is 9.8 m s−2 down. At the top only vy = 0. Gravity is always there, so the acceleration is never 0.

3. A ball is launched horizontally at 2 m s−1 from a table edge at h = 0.900 m. How long does it take to land? What is the range?

t = 0.429 s, R = 0.857 m. t = √(2h/g) = √(2 × 0.900 / 9.8) = 0.429 s. R = ut = 2 × 0.4286 = 0.857 m.

4. On level ground, a ball is launched at 20 m s−1, 30°. Find the vertical component, the time of flight, the maximum height and the range.

uy = 10 m s−1, tflight = 2.04 s, hmax = 5.10 m, R = 35.3 m. uy = 20 sin 30° = 10; tflight = 2 × 10 / 9.8 = 2.04 s; hmax = 10² / (2 × 9.8) = 5.10 m; R = 20² × sin 60° / 9.8 = 35.3 m (or ux × tflight = 17.3 × 2.04).

5. Same launch as question 4, but at 60°. Does the range change? Which one flies higher?

The range is the same, still 35.3 m; 60° goes higher. 30° and 60° are complementary angles, and sin 60° = sin 120°. For 60°, hmax = (20 sin 60°)² / 19.6 = 15.3 m, and the ball is in the air for 3.53 s.

6. A ball is launched horizontally at 3 m s−1 from a height of 4.9 m. How fast does it land? In what direction?

About 10.2 m s−1, about 73° below the horizontal. t = √(2 × 4.9 / 9.8) = 1 s; vy = −9.8 × 1 = −9.8 m s−1; v = √(3² + 9.8²) = 10.2 m s−1; tan α = 9.8 / 3, α = 73.0°. Energy also gives the speed: v = √(u² + 2gh) = √(9 + 96.04) = 10.2 m s−1, but energy cannot give the direction.

7. With air resistance, how does the path change compared with the model?

The range is shorter, the top is lower, the way down is steeper and takes longer, and the ball lands slower. Drag is opposite to the velocity, so it slows the horizontal motion. On the way up it also pulls down, together with gravity. The best angle is also less than 45°.

8. A plane at uniform velocity drops a parcel (no air resistance). When the parcel lands, where is it compared with the plane?

Directly below it. Because of inertia, the parcel keeps the plane's horizontal velocity after release. There is no horizontal force, so the parcel's vx always equals the plane's velocity.
