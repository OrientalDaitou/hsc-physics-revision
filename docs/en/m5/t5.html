Energy in Gravitational Fields

Explained from zero

This chapter is about energy in space: satellites, planets and rockets. Two things confuse people the most: why U is negative, and why a higher satellite is slower but has more energy. If you remember the three rules below, you can work all of these out yourself.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Kinetic energy K = ½mv² is never negative (K = 0 at rest). Work W = Fs cos θ: when a force is at 90° to the motion, that force does no work (M5 T3).
  2. F = GMm/r²: r is measured from the centre of the planet, r = rE + h. The farther away, the weaker the gravity (M5 T4).
  3. In a circular orbit, gravity is the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so v = √(GM/r) (M5 T4).
  4. Near the ground ΔU = mgΔh. Wby gravity = −ΔU: when gravity does positive work, U decreases (M5 T3).
5.1

Gravitational potential energy in a radial field

Big ideaFar from the ground, gravity gets weaker, so mgΔh works only near the surface. With U = 0 at infinity, U = −GMm/r is negative everywhere else.

  • Gravitational potential energy (U): energy stored because of where an object is in a gravitational field. Unit: J.
  • Radial field: a field whose lines all point straight at the planet's centre, like the spokes of a wheel.
  • Infinity: so far away that the planet's gravity is too small to matter.
  • mgΔh assumes that g stays constant. But far from the ground g gets smaller, so you cannot use it in space.

Why U is negative: three steps

  1. We choose U = 0 at infinity, where the pull of gravity is zero.
  2. Moving away needs work against gravity. So U increases as r increases.
  3. U reaches 0 only at infinity. So U is negative at every real distance.

The minus sign comes from our choice of zero. It is like calling the roof of a building 0 m: the height of every floor inside is a negative number, but going upstairs still takes you higher. Only changes in U can be measured.

How work and U are linked

  • Work done by gravity: Wby gravity = −ΔU. When an object moves outwards, gravity pulls inwards, so gravity does negative work and U increases.
  • When an engine pushes an object outwards at a steady speed, all of its work is stored as U: Wagainst = +ΔU.
  • Where U comes from: draw the graph of F = GMm/r² against r. From r1 to r2, the area under the graph = work against gravity = ΔU = GMm(1/r1 − 1/r2).
  • Let r1 = r and r2 = ∞, then use U(∞) = 0: 0 − U = GMm/r, so U = −GMm/r.

5.2

Kinetic, potential and total energy in orbit

Big ideaIn a circular orbit the total energy is U + K = −GMm/2r. A lower satellite moves faster, but it has less total energy.

  • Total mechanical energy (E) = kinetic energy + potential energy: E = U + K, unit: J.
  • K is always positive and U is always negative. In a circular orbit K is exactly half the size of U, so E is negative.
  • You can think of E as "how much more energy it needs to escape". E = −15.7 MJ means it still needs 15.7 MJ. The lower the satellite, the more it needs, even though it moves faster.

② Put in v² = GM/r: K = ½mv² = GMm/2r.

Trap: for an ellipse use K = ½mv². "E increases" means it gets less negative.

5.2

Energy in elliptical orbits (Kepler's laws)

Big ideaWith gravity only, E = K + U stays the same. When a planet gets closer to the Sun, U goes down, so K goes up and the planet moves faster.

First, a review of Kepler's laws (covered in M5 T4):

  1. Kepler's first law: each planet moves in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.
  2. Kepler's second law: a line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  3. Kepler's third law: r³/T² = GM/4π² (on the sheet).

The eccentricity (e) tells you how stretched an ellipse is: e = 0 is a circle, and the closer e is to 1, the flatter the ellipse.

Now we link these laws to energy.

Circle

Gravity is always at 90° to v. W = Fs cos 90° = 0, so gravity does no work and the speed stays the same.

Ellipse

Gravity is the only force, so E = K + U stays the same. When U goes down, K goes up. This is called conservation of mechanical energy.

  • Perihelion (closest point to the Sun): U lowest, K largest, fastest.
  • Aphelion (farthest point from the Sun): U highest, K smallest, slowest. This agrees with Kepler's second law.
  • Why it speeds up (Figure 5.2 in the booklet, anticlockwise): on the way in towards the Sun, part of the force of gravity F points along v. It is as if gravity pushes the planet from behind, so it goes faster and faster.
  • Analogy: a skateboard in a half-pipe. Going down, it gets faster and faster (U turns into K). Going up, it gets slower and slower (K turns into U). The total energy stays the same.

At any point of any path where gravity is the only force, you can write E = K + U = ½mv² − GMm/r. Use v and r at the same point.

5.3

Escape velocity

Big ideaThe escape velocity is the smallest launch speed that lets a projectile get away from a planet for ever. At exactly this speed, its total energy E = 0.

  • Escape velocity (vesc): that smallest launch speed. Any slower and the object is pulled back.
  • Projectile: it gets all its speed at the start, then moves with only gravity acting (no engine).
  • The total energy decides the path.

Newton's cannon

Imagine a very tall mountain, with its top above the air. A cannon on the top fires sideways at five speeds (Figure 5.3 in the booklet; the mountain is drawn far too tall, not to scale):

  1. Slower than orbital speed: falls back to the ground.
  2. v = √(GM/r): a circle.
  3. Between √(GM/r) and vesc: an ellipse.
  4. v = vesc: just escapes.
  5. v > vesc: escapes and is still moving when very far away.

Paths 1–3 have E < 0: bound. Path 4 has E = 0: it reaches infinity with K = 0 and U = 0. Path 5 has E > 0.

When to use: a projectile, from the surface or from an orbit.
You can also derive it: energy is conserved. In the smallest case the object reaches infinity with K = 0 and U = 0, so K + U = 0 at launch too.
① ½mv² − GMm/r = 0. Add GMm/r to both sides, then divide by m (m cancels): ½v² = GM/r.
② Multiply by 2, take the square root: vesc = √(2GM/r).
Trap: do not lose the 2. Without it you get the orbital speed √(GM/r).

  • m cancels, so a pebble and a spacecraft need the same escape speed (the heavier one needs more kinetic energy). From Earth's surface, vesc = 11.2 km s−1.
  • At the same r: vesc = √2 × vorbital.
  • A rocket need not reach vesc. Its engines keep adding energy, and vesc is smaller higher up. It escapes once K + U reaches 0.

5.4

Moving between orbits

Big ideaTo reach a higher orbit, the engines must add energy ΔE. But the satellite ends up slower: K falls by ΔE and U rises by 2ΔE. Air drag does the opposite.

  • A bigger orbit has a less negative E. So the engines supply ΔE = E2 − E1.
  • Every circular orbit has K = −E and U = 2E. So ΔK = −ΔE and ΔU = 2ΔE.
  • Think of it in equal parts: the engines give 1 part, K falls by 1 part, U rises by 2 parts. Of the 2 parts that U gains, 1 comes from the engines and 1 comes from K.

Trap: ΔE is not ΔU. The engines supply only half of the rise in U. The other half comes from the fall in K.

From the ground

  • On the launch pad K ≈ 0, so E = U = −GMm/rE.
  • The smallest energy to reach a circular orbit is ΔE = −GMm/(2r) + GMm/rE.
  • Geostationary orbit (GEO): above the equator, one turn per day. Used by TV and weather satellites.
  • Low Earth orbit (LEO): for example the space station and Starlink.

During a launch: the fuel's chemical energy becomes extra K and U (and heat and sound). After the engines stop, the rocket keeps going up because of its inertia. It coasts: K turns into U, and K + U stays constant.

The Hohmann transfer

A Hohmann transfer is a fuel-saving way to change orbit, using only two short engine firings. Each firing is called a burn. Perigee and apogee are the closest and farthest points of an orbit around Earth. Figure 5.4 in the booklet: from a 250 km orbit to GEO (not to scale).

  1. Burn 1 at perigee: push forwards (along the motion) into the elliptical transfer orbit. This half ellipse is called the Hohmann transfer orbit.
  2. Coast: gravity slows it. K turns into U, and E is constant.
  3. Burn 2 at apogee: push forwards again, into the big circle.

Both burns speed it up, but it ends slower: 7.77 → 3.08 km s−1. To move down, push backwards.

Orbital decay

Orbital decay is a slow fall to lower orbits. A LEO satellite moves through very thin air. Atmospheric drag (the push of this air against its motion) turns some mechanical energy into heat. Figure 5.5 in the booklet: the satellite spirals slowly inwards (the spacing is exaggerated).

  1. Drag removes energy, so E falls (more negative).
  2. E = −GMm/2r, so r falls. The orbit gets lower.
  3. E = −K, so K rises: the satellite speeds up.
  4. U = 2E, so U falls by twice the energy lost: half becomes heat, half becomes extra K.

Lower orbits meet denser air, so decay speeds up until the satellite burns up. Decay is caused by drag, not by "stronger gravity".

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. U = −GMm/r: zero at infinity, negative everywhere else. r = rE + altitude.
  2. Circular orbit: K = GMm/2r and E = U + K = −GMm/2r. So E = −K and U = 2E.

Common mistakes

Wrong: using mgΔh for the change in U in space too.
Right: mgΔh works only near the ground. Farther away g gets smaller, so use U = −GMm/r.
Wrong: using the altitude directly as r, or losing the minus sign of U.
Right: r = rE + altitude, measured from the centre. Keep the minus sign.
Wrong: using K = GMm/2r for an elliptical orbit too.
Right: it is for circular orbits only. For an ellipse use K = ½mv², with v and r at the same point.
Wrong: "E increases" means the number gets bigger if you ignore the minus sign, for example −15.7 MJ → −31.4 MJ.
Right: E is negative. "E increases" means it gets less negative, for example −31.4 MJ → −15.7 MJ.
Wrong: vesc = √(GM/r).
Right: vesc = √(2GM/r). Without the 2, it is the orbital speed.
Wrong: a heavier spacecraft has a larger escape velocity.
Right: m cancels, so the escape velocity is the same. The heavier one only needs more kinetic energy.
Wrong: a satellite that moves to a higher orbit goes faster (because energy was added).
Right: higher means slower. E has increased, but K has decreased.
Wrong: to reach a higher orbit, the engines must supply as much energy as ΔU.
Right: the engines supply only ΔE, which is half of ΔU. The other half comes from the fall in K.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. Why is a satellite's U negative?

Because we choose U = 0 at infinity. Moving outwards, U increases, and it rises to 0 only at infinity. So everywhere else it is less than 0. The minus sign comes from our choice of zero.

2. What is U for 1 kg on the ground? (G = 6.67 × 10−11 N m² kg−2, ME = 6.0 × 1024 kg, rE = 6.371 × 106 m)

About −62.8 MJ. U = −GMm/r = −6.67 × 10−11 × 6.0 × 1024 × 1 / (6.371 × 106) = −6.28 × 107 J. So it takes 62.8 MJ to send 1 kg from the ground to infinity. This is the same number as "62.8 MJ to escape" in the booklet.

3. A 1 kg satellite in a circular orbit at r = 2 rE has K = 15.7 MJ. What are its E and U?

E = −15.7 MJ, U = −31.4 MJ. Circular-orbit shortcut: E = −K = −15.7 MJ, U = 2E = −31.4 MJ. Check: U + K = −31.4 + 15.7 = −15.7 MJ.

4. A planet moves in an elliptical orbit. Where is it fastest? Is E larger there than at other points?

Fastest at perihelion; E is the same everywhere. Gravity is the only force, so E does not change. Perihelion is closest to the Sun, so U is lowest and K is largest.

5. At r0 = 1.00 × 107 m, what is the escape velocity? How many times as big as the orbital speed here is it?

8.95 km s−1, which is √2 ≈ 1.41 times 6.33 km s−1. GM = 6.67 × 10−11 × 6.0 × 1024 = 4.002 × 1014 m³ s−2. vesc = √(2 × 4.002 × 1014 / (1.00 × 107)) = 8.95 × 10³ m s−1. Orbital speed √(GM/r) = 6.33 × 10³ m s−1. These match the 8.95 and 6.33 in the booklet's experiment.

6. Do a pebble and a 1000 kg spacecraft have the same escape velocity from the ground?

Yes, both are 11.2 km s−1. m cancels in ½mv² − GMm/r = 0. The spacecraft only needs more kinetic energy.

7. A 1 kg satellite moves from a 250 km orbit to GEO. What is the smallest energy the engines must give? How much do K and U change?

ΔE = 25.5 MJ; K falls by 25.5 MJ, U rises by 51.0 MJ. The booklet: 32.6 MJ to a 250 km orbit, 58.1 MJ to GEO, so ΔE = 58.1 − 32.6 = 25.5 MJ. ΔK = −ΔE = −25.5 MJ, ΔU = 2ΔE = +51.0 MJ. So the satellite ends up slower (7.77 → 3.08 km s−1).

8. A satellite in a low orbit feels drag. Does it speed up or slow down?

It speeds up. Drag removes energy, so E falls (more negative) and r falls. E = −K, so K rises. U falls by twice the energy lost: half becomes heat, half becomes K.
