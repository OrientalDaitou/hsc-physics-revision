Circular Motion: Angles, Energy, Work and Torque

Explained from zero

This chapter continues circular motion and looks at three things: how big the angle turned is, how the energy changes, and how a force makes things turn. It looks like a lot of formulas, but it really uses only three rules. Once these three are clear, the rest is simple.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Circular motion from the last chapter: v = 2πr/T, ac = v²/r, Fc = mv²/r (all on the sheet).
  2. Centripetal force Fc is the net force towards the centre: add up the real forces in the direction towards the centre. It is not an extra force.
  3. Kinetic energy K = ½mv², and the change in gravitational potential energy ΔU = mgΔh (from Year 11, both on the sheet).
  4. Trigonometry: sin 90° = 1, cos 90° = 0, sin 0° = 0. A slanted force can be split into two parts at 90° to each other. Each part is called a component.
3.1

Angles and angular velocity

Big ideaEvery point on a spinning object turns through the same angle in the same time, so it has the same ω. Points further out move faster.

  • Radian (rad): a unit for angles. One turn = 2π rad = 360°, so 1 rad ≈ 57.3°.
  • Angular displacement (Δθ): the angle turned through, measured at the centre.
  • Angular velocity (ω): the angle turned each second. Unit: rad s−1.

How to change to rad

Starting unitTo radExample
Degrees× π/18090° = π/2 rad
Turns× 2π3 turns = 6π rad
rpm (turns per minute)× 2π/6060 rpm = 2π rad s−1

Same ω: further out is faster

  • Look at two points, A and B, on a spinning disc. They are on the same radius. B is 2 times as far from the centre as A.
  • When the disc turns, A and B turn through the same angle, so they have the same ω.
  • B's circle is bigger. For the same angle, B's arc is 2 times as long as A's, so B's speed is also 2 times A's.
  • Arc length: the distance along the edge of the circle, s = rθ. Here θ must be in rad.
  • Analogy: a merry-go-round. Horses on the inner ring and the outer ring take the same time for one turn. A horse on the outer ring must go round a bigger circle, so it moves faster.
The same for every point

ω, period T, frequency f: the whole object turns together, so they are the same for every point.

Bigger further out

Tangential speed (speed along the circle) v = ωr, and also ac = ω²r: with the same ω, the bigger r is, the bigger they are.

3.2

Work and energy in circular motion

Big ideaIn uniform circular motion the net force is at 90° to the motion. So it does no work, and the speed stays constant.

  • Work (W): energy transferred by a force when the object moves along the direction of the force (or of part of it). Unit: joule (J).
  • Total mechanical energy (E): kinetic energy plus potential energy, E = K + U.
WorkOn the formula sheet

Example: F = 10 N, s = 2 m, θ = 60°: W = 10 × 2 × cos 60° = 10 J.
Trap: Fc does no work, because θ = 90° and cos 90° = 0.

Going round a circle: how much work does the centripetal force do?

  • The centripetal force Fc always points to the centre. The velocity v is always along the tangent. They are at 90° at every instant.
  • Split one turn into many tiny steps Δs. For each tiny step: W = Fc Δs cos 90° = 0.
  • Work–energy theorem: the work done by the net force = the change in kinetic energy, Wnet = ΔK.
  • The work done by the net force is 0, so ΔK = 0. K = ½mv² stays constant, and so does the speed. Only the direction of the velocity changes.
  • Analogy: above your head, whirl a small ball on a string in a horizontal circle. The string pulls inwards all the time. It only changes the direction. It does not make the ball faster or slower.
  • If the object is speeding up, part of the net force must be along the path (tangential). This part does the work.

Note: in uniform circular motion Wnet = 0, so ΔK = 0: the speed stays constant.

Energy: a horizontal circle and a Ferris wheel

Horizontal circle / circular orbit

The height (or the radius of a satellite's orbit) does not change, so U is constant. K is constant too, so E = K + U is constant.

Ferris wheel

Steady speed, so K stays the same. But as a rider rises, U gets bigger, so E gets bigger. The seat does positive work on the rider. The energy comes from the motor.

Gravity's work is already counted in ΔU. So E changes only when forces other than gravity (a push, a motor, friction) do work.

Trap: do not count gravity twice. Its work is already in ΔU.

3.2

Vertical circles

Big ideaOn a vertical circle, gravity slows the ball down when it goes up, and speeds it up when it goes down. So it is slowest at the top and fastest at the bottom.

If only gravity does work (no motor, push or friction), the ball's speed changes round a vertical circle. This is non-uniform circular motion (moving in a circle with a changing speed).

  • The tension (FT) of the string points along the string, to the centre, at 90° to the motion. So it does no work.
  • Gravity always points down. Going up, part of gravity pulls backwards and the ball slows down. Going down, gravity speeds the ball up.
  • So the ball is slowest at the top and fastest at the bottom.
  • K + U stays the same: this is the conservation of mechanical energy.

Forces: write equations only at the very top and bottom

  • Their sum is Fc = mv²/r (on the sheet). Fc is not an extra force. Do not draw it on a free-body diagram.
  • At the top, mg points down, which is exactly towards the centre. So write +mg, not −mg.
  • At the bottom, FT points up, towards the centre (+). mg points down, away from the centre (−).

On a track: dip and crest

When a cart moves on a track, there is no string. The push of the track or seat is called the normal force (FN). It replaces FT. Apparent weight is the seat's push on you, FN: it is the weight you feel.

Dip

The low point of a valley, with the centre above. FN − mg = mv²/r, so FN is larger than mg: you feel heavier.

Crest

The top of a hill, with the centre below. mg − FN = mv²/r, so FN is smaller than mg: you feel lighter.

When does the string go slack?

  • The slower the ball is at the top, the looser the string is. When FT = 0, the string just goes slack (loose).
  • Then only gravity provides the centripetal force: mg = mv²/r, so v = √(gr). This is the smallest speed at the top. Any slower and the ball cannot get over the top: the string goes slack before the top, and the ball leaves the circle and falls inwards.
  • A cart going over a crest: if v > √(gr), the cart leaves the track.

3.3

Torque: what makes things turn

Big ideaA force can make an object turn about a point. Its turning effect is the torque: τ = r⊥F. If the torques balance, the rotation does not change.

  • Torque (τ): the turning effect of a force. Unit: newton metre (N m).
  • Pivot: the point or line that an object turns about, such as the hinge of a door.
  • Line of action: the line along which a force acts, drawn longer in both directions.
  • Perpendicular distance (r⊥): the shortest distance from the pivot to the line of action.

What makes torque bigger?

  • A bigger force.
  • Further from the pivot. A door handle is placed as far from the hinges as possible. Pushing the handle is easy. Pushing next to the hinges is hard.
  • Closer to 90° to the lever (the bar that turns). If you push at a slant, part of the force is wasted.

Two ways to find it, same result

A force F pulls on a spanner. θ is the angle between r and F.

(a) Perpendicular distance r⊥

Draw the line of action longer in both directions. From the pivot, draw a line to it at 90°. r⊥ = r sin θ, τ = r⊥F.

(b) Perpendicular force F sin θ

Split F into two components. F sin θ is perpendicular to the spanner and turns it. F cos θ is along the spanner: no turning. τ = r × F sin θ.

  • sin 90° = 1, so the torque is largest when the force is perpendicular to the spanner.
  • When the line of action passes through the pivot, r⊥ = 0, and there is no torque. If you push a door straight towards its hinges, the door does not move.

  • θ in W = Fs cos θ: the angle between the force and the displacement.
  • θ in τ = rF sin θ: the angle between r and F, with the calculator in degree mode.
3.3

How torque changes rotation

Big ideaA force changes straight-line motion. A torque changes turning motion. If the net torque (the result of all the torques together) is not 0, the rotation changes. If it is 0 (the torques balance), the rotation does not change.

net torque ≠ 0

The object starts to turn, speeds up or slows down. A bigger net torque changes the rotation faster (whether it is speeding up or slowing down).

net torque = 0

Balanced torques: the rotation does not change. A still object stays still, and a turning object keeps turning at the same rate.

  • Clockwise and anticlockwise torques cancel each other. What is left is the net torque.
  • About one pivot, the condition for balanced torques is: all the clockwise torques added up = all the anticlockwise torques added up.
  • Analogy: a seesaw. If the torques on the two sides are equal, the seesaw stays still. A heavier person sitting closer and a lighter person sitting further away can also balance.
Balanced torquesNot on the sheet: learn it
∑τclockwise = ∑τanticlockwise
∑τclockwise
sum of all clockwise torques (N m)
∑τanticlockwise
sum of all anticlockwise torques (N m)

You can also derive it: net torque = 0 means the clockwise and anticlockwise torques are equal and cancel each other.
Example: on a metre rule, the anticlockwise torque of the load is 0.59 N m. Pull straight down at r = 0.30 m: F = 0.59/0.30 = 1.97 N, and the two sides balance.
Trap: all torques must be found about the same pivot.

Torque in everyday life

  • Door handles are far from the hinges, and a long spanner is used on a tight nut. With a big r, the same force gives more torque.
  • A bicycle crank (the arm that joins the pedal to the axle): the torque is largest when the crank is horizontal and the foot pushes straight down. Then the force is at 90° to the crank.
3.4

Key experiments

Investigating torque: τ = r⊥F

Aim
To test τ = rF sin θ, and to see how an unbalanced torque changes the rotation.
Method
A metre rule is pivoted at its centre of mass (where its weight seems to act). A spring balance keeps it level against a fixed load. This is repeated at different r and angles. Then the torques are made unbalanced.
Results
Fr stayed at about 0.59 N m: F against 1/r is a straight line through the origin. At an angle, F sin θ stayed the same. A bigger net torque changed the rotation of the rule faster.
Conclusion
Only the part of F at 90° to the rule turns it: τ = rF sin θ.
Significance
It explains door handles, spanners and levers, and motor torque (Module 6). Animation 5 is this experiment.

Work and energy in circular motion

Aim
To relate the work done on an object moving in a circle to its total energy.
Method
A: a stopper on a string is whirled in a horizontal circle. A video gives K = ½mv² at 8 points. B: a ball swings down from rest through a height h. A light gate (a beam that times how long the ball blocks it) gives its speed.
Results
A: K stayed at 0.24 J all the way round, at the same height. B: K at the bottom was 97 % of the U lost (mgh), at every height.
Conclusion
A: E = K + U was constant: the tension is at 90° to the motion and does no work. B: gravity does the work. The missing 3 % is a systematic error (an error in the same direction in every reading): the light-gate beam is not a thin line, so every speed comes out slightly too low. Within this error, the K gained = the U lost.
Significance
Energy conservation explains pendulums, rollercoasters and satellites. The 8 measuring points and 0.24 J in Animation 2 are Experiment A.

Memorise these 5 before the exam

  1. One turn is 2π rad. ω = Δθ/t = 2π/T, v = ωr: every point has the same ω.
  2. Uniform circular motion: Fc is at 90° to v, so W = 0 and K is constant.
  3. Only forces other than gravity change E: Wother = ΔE = ΔK + ΔU.
  4. Vertical circle: use energy for the speed (top to bottom: drop 2r). Top: FT + mg = mv²/r; bottom: FT − mg = mv²/r.

Common mistakes

Wrong: one turn is 1 rad; or putting degrees directly into ω = Δθ/t.
Right: one turn is 2π rad. Change turns and degrees to rad first.
Wrong: using the diameter for r in v = ωr.
Right: r is the radius.
Wrong: the centripetal force does work on the object and makes it go faster and faster.
Right: Fc is at 90° to v, so W = 0. It only changes the direction, not the speed.
Wrong: using Wother = ΔK + ΔU and also counting gravity's work in Wother.
Right: gravity's work is already in ΔU. Do not count it twice.
Wrong: from top to bottom, the drop in height is r.
Right: the drop is the diameter, 2r: vbottom² = vtop² + 4gr.
Wrong: writing FT − mg = mv²/r at the top.
Right: at the top, mg points to the centre, so it is +mg: FT + mg = mv²/r.
Wrong: treating Fc as an extra force and drawing it on a free-body diagram.
Right: Fc is the result of adding up the real forces.
Wrong: measuring r as the whole length of the spanner, or from some other point.
Right: measure r from the pivot to where the force acts.
Wrong: the question gives the angle between the force and a line at 90° to the lever, and you use it directly as θ.
Right: θ is the angle between r and F. Use 90° minus the given angle.
Wrong: writing the unit of torque as J.
Right: the unit of torque is N m.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. A fan makes 30 turns each minute (30 rpm). What is ω in rad s−1? How long is the period?

ω = π ≈ 3.14 rad s−1, T = 2.0 s. ω = 30 × 2π/60 = π rad s−1. T = 2π/ω = 2π/π = 2.0 s (30 turns in one minute, so 2 s for each turn).

2. A disc has ω = 10 rad s−1. Point A is 0.10 m from the centre, and point B is 0.20 m from the centre. What is the speed of each?

vA = 1.0 m s−1, vB = 2.0 m s−1. They have the same ω, so use v = ωr: 10 × 0.10 = 1.0, 10 × 0.20 = 2.0. B is 2 times as far out, so it is 2 times as fast.

3. A stopper goes once round a horizontal circle at a steady speed. How much work does the tension of the string do?

0. The tension points to the centre and is always at 90° to the velocity, and cos 90° = 0. So K does not change, and the speed stays the same all the time.

4. A Ferris wheel lifts a rider at a steady speed. How do the rider's K, U and E change? Where does the energy come from?

K does not change, U gets bigger, E gets bigger. The speed does not change, so K does not change. The rider gets higher, so U gets bigger. The seat does positive work on the rider (Wother = ΔE). The energy comes from the motor.

5. A ball on a string of length r = 1.0 m goes round a vertical circle. What is the smallest speed at the top? What is the speed at the bottom in this case?

Top 3.13 m s−1, bottom 7.0 m s−1. Smallest speed v = √(gr) = √(9.8 × 1.0) = 3.13 m s−1. The drop is 2r, so vbottom² = vtop² + 4gr = 9.8 + 4 × 9.8 × 1.0 = 49, vbottom = 7.0 m s−1.

6. Continuing from question 5, the ball has mass m = 0.50 kg. What is the tension in the string at the bottom?

29.4 N. At the bottom: FT − mg = mv²/r. FT = 0.50 × 49/1.0 + 0.50 × 9.8 = 24.5 + 4.9 = 29.4 N.

7. When a rollercoaster goes through a dip, do you feel heavier or lighter? Why?

Heavier. The centre of a dip is above: FN − mg = mv²/r, so FN = mg + mv²/r, which is larger than mg. The weight you feel (apparent weight) is FN.

8. A 40 N force pulls on a spanner and acts 0.25 m from the pivot. The force is at 60° to the line perpendicular to the spanner. What is the torque?

5.0 N m. θ is the angle between r and F: θ = 90° − 60° = 30°. τ = rF sin θ = 0.25 × 40 × sin 30° = 5.0 N m.
