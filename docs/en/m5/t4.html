Gravity, Orbits and Kepler's Laws

Explained from zero

This chapter explains why things in the sky go around in circles: the Moon, satellites and planets. There seem to be many formulas, but they all come from two rules. First we make these two rules clear. Then you can derive the later formulas yourself.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Centripetal force Fc = mv²/r: to move in a circle, an object needs a net force pointing to the centre of the circle (Chapter 2, on the sheet).
  2. v = 2πr/T: the distance for one orbit is 2πr, and the time it takes is the period T (Chapter 2, on the sheet).
  3. Free fall: when gravity is the only force, the acceleration is g. It does not depend on the object's own mass.
  4. Units and calculator: km × 1000 → m; hours × 3600, days × 86 400 → s. Know how to find a square, a square root and a cube root (∛).
4.1

Newton's law of gravitation

Big ideaAll masses attract each other. The pull weakens quickly with distance.

  • Gravitational force: the pull between any two masses. Unit: newton (N).
  • The forces on the two objects are equal in size and opposite in direction. The Earth pulls on you with the same size of force as you pull on the Earth.
  • Inverse-square law: distance × 2, the force becomes ¼; distance × 3, the force becomes ⅑. This is because the formula has r².
  • This law is universal: it works everywhere, for apples, moons and planets alike. When you calculate, treat each object as a point or a sphere.
  • G is the universal gravitational constant, a fixed number: 6.67 × 10−11 N m² kg−2.

Trap: r is centre to centre, not surface to surface. Square all of r.

4.1

Gravitational field strength

Big ideag is the gravitational force on each 1 kg at a point. The further from the planet's centre, the smaller g is: distance × 2, g ÷ 4.

  • Gravitational field: a region where any mass feels a gravitational force.
  • Field strength (g): the gravitational force on each 1 kg, g = F/m. The unit is N kg−1, which is also m s−2. The two are the same thing.
  • In free fall (gravity is the only force), g is the object's acceleration.
  • A planet's field is a radial field: the field lines all point to the centre, like the spokes of a wheel. Further out, the lines are more spread out, so the field is weaker.
  • Near the ground, the field is nearly a uniform field: the same size and direction at every point, g = 9.8 m s−2.
  • Altitude (h): the height above the surface. Gravity formulas need the distance from the centre of the Earth: r = rE + h.
  • Data sheet values: rE = 6.371 × 106 m, ME = 6.0 × 1024 kg.

Factors that affect g

  • Only two: the planet's mass (g ∝ M) and the distance from its centre (g ∝ 1/r²). r × 2 ⇒ g ÷ 4.
  • The falling object's own mass cancels. So (if air resistance is negligible) all objects fall with the same acceleration.
  • Data sheet values give GME = 4.0 × 1014 m³ s−2 and a surface g = 9.86 m s−2. This is a little bigger than the sheet's 9.8, only because the data sheet's ME = 6.0 × 1024 kg is rounded. Normally use 9.8. You get 9.86 only when a question asks you to calculate g yourself.
  • Real Earth: g ≈ 9.78 m s−2 at the equator and 9.83 m s−2 at the poles. This is because the equator bulges a little, and the Earth's spin reduces the measured g at the equator. Mountains and dense rock also change g slightly.
  • g is a vector, pointing towards the mass. Add the fields of two bodies as vectors.
4.2

Satellites in circular orbits

Big ideaA satellite is always falling, but it moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away as fast as it falls. Gravity provides the centripetal force.

Newton's cannonball

Newton imagined a cannon on a very, very tall mountain, firing horizontally, with no air. (The mountain in Animation 2 below is also drawn far too tall, just so you can see clearly.)

  1. A: a slow ball soon falls to the ground.
  2. B: a faster ball lands further away. The ball is still always falling.
  3. C: at about 7.9 km s−1 near the ground, the ground curves away as fast as the ball falls. The ball never lands: it is in orbit.
  4. D: faster still, the orbit becomes an oval. Above 11.2 km s−1 the ball escapes: it flies away and does not come back.
  • So an orbit is projectile motion that never ends. A satellite is always in free fall.
  • Gravity points to the Earth's centre, which is also the centre of the circle. So gravity provides the centripetal force: F = Fc.
  • On the circular orbit C, gravity F and velocity v are always at 90°.
Orbital speed (circular orbit)Not on the sheet: learn it
v = √(GM / r)
v
orbital speed: the speed needed for a circular orbit (m s−1)
G

M
mass of the central body, not the satellite (kg)
r

When to use: any circular orbit around a planet, moon or star.
You can also derive it: GMm/r² = mv²/r (both on the sheet). Divide both sides by m, then multiply by r: v² = GM/r. Then take the square root.
Example: the ISS, r = 6.791 × 106 m: v = √(4.0 × 1014 / 6.791 × 106) ≈ 7.7 × 103 m s−1, which is about 7.7 km s−1.
Trap: M is the planet's mass, not the satellite's. r is not the altitude.

  • The satellite's mass cancels: objects at one height, however heavy, have the same speed and the same period (the orbit simulation in 4.5 shows this).
  • A higher orbit is slower: v ∝ 1/√r.
  • The satellite's acceleration ac = v²/r = GM/r² is exactly the g at that height.
  • Orbital velocity is along the tangent, at 90° to the radius.
  • Apparent weightlessness: feeling no weight in free fall.
Gravity: still there

The ISS is 420 km up. There g = 8.7 m s−2, only a little less than at the ground. Gravity has not gone. It acts exactly as the centripetal force.

Push from the floor: gone

The astronauts and the station fall together, so the floor does not push on them: the normal force = 0. So they feel no weight.

An analogy: the cable of a lift breaks, and you and the lift fall together. The scale under your feet does not have to hold you up, so it reads 0. But the Earth is still pulling you.

4.2

The period and Kepler's third law

Big ideaPut v = 2πr/T into v² = GM/r, and you get r³/T² = a constant. This constant depends only on the mass of the central body.

  1. Circular orbit: v² = GM/r (previous section).
  2. The sheet has v = 2πr/T. Put it in: 4π²r²/T² = GM/r.
  3. Multiply both sides by r, then divide by 4π²: r³/T² = GM/4π².
  • You can use it to find three things: the period T, the orbital radius r (then use h = r − rE to find the height), or the mass M of the central body.
  • r is the orbital radius. If the orbit is an ellipse, use the semi-major axis (covered in 4.3) as r.
  • A higher orbit has a longer period: when r becomes 2 times as big, T becomes 2√2 ≈ 2.8 times as big.

Example 2: one day, T = 86 400 s: r = ∛(4.0 × 1014 × 86 400² / 4π²) = 4.23 × 107 m, h = 4.23 × 107 − 6.371 × 106 ≈ 3.59 × 107 m, about 36 000 km. This is the geostationary orbit covered in 4.4.
Trap: the cube root gives r, not the height. You still need to subtract rE.

4.3

Kepler's laws of planetary motion

Big ideaKepler's three laws came from observed data. Newton's law of universal gravitation explains all three.

  • Kepler used Tycho Brahe's careful data. He published his first two laws in 1609 and the third in 1619.
  • They are empirical laws: they only describe how planets move. They do not explain why.
  • In 1687, Newton showed that a force with F ∝ 1/r² explains all three.

First law: ellipses

Kepler's first law: each planet moves in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.

  • Ellipse: a stretched circle. Its long axis has two fixed points called foci (singular: focus). The Sun is at one of them. The other focus is empty.
  • Planet orbits are nearly circles. The Earth's eccentricity (how much the ellipse is stretched) is only 0.017, so we normally treat its orbit as a circle.
  • Comets' orbits are very stretched.
  • The closest point to the Sun is perihelion, and the farthest point is aphelion. For an orbit around the Earth, they are perigee and apogee.
  • For an ellipse, r in the third law is the semi-major axis (half the longest diameter): a = (rp + ra)/2.

Second law: equal areas

Kepler's second law: a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. In the "Area" mode of Animation 4 below, each sector takes the same time (Δt1 = Δt2) and has the same area (A1 = A2).

Near the Sun

The line is short. To sweep out the same area, the planet must cover a longer arc in the same time. So it is fastest at perihelion.

Far from the Sun

The line is long. A short arc is enough to make the same area. So it is slowest at aphelion.

An analogy: two triangular pieces of cake of the same size. One is long and narrow. The other is short and wide. The short one has the longer outer arc.

With forces: why the planet speeds up and slows down

A force does work (gives an object energy) only if part of the force points along the motion.

  • Circle: gravity is always at 90° to v. No work is done, so the speed does not change.
  • Moving closer to the Sun: part of gravity points along the motion, so the planet speeds up.
  • Moving away from the Sun: part of gravity points against the motion, so the planet slows down.
  • On an ellipse, F ⟂ v at only two points: perihelion (fastest) and aphelion (slowest).

With energy

  • Only gravity acts, so the total energy (kinetic K plus potential U) stays constant.
  • U = −GMm/r: the smaller r is, the lower (more negative) U is. So U is lowest at perihelion, and K = ½mv² is largest at perihelion.
  • Circular orbit: r does not change, so U, K and the speed do not change.
  • At perihelion and aphelion only: rpvp = rava (from equal areas).

Third law: periods

  • Kepler's third law: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the orbital radius (semi-major axis): T² ∝ r³.
  • The constant GM/4π² depends only on the mass of the central body. All the Sun's planets share one value, and all the Earth's satellites share another.
  • For two orbits around the same central body: r1³/T1² = r2³/T2². You can use any units, as long as both sides use the same ones. For example, years and astronomical units (AU): 1 AU = the average Earth-Sun distance, 1.50 × 1011 m.

4.4

Low Earth orbits and geostationary orbits

Big ideaThe height of an orbit fixes its speed and period. Each height suits different jobs.

  • Low Earth orbit (LEO): 200 km to 2000 km up. There is still very thin air there, which causes orbital decay: a slow loss of height.
  • Polar orbit: a LEO that passes over both poles. The Earth turns beneath it, so it passes over every part of the Earth.
  • Geostationary orbit: the satellite always stays above one point on the equator. It needs three things:
  1. A period of one day (strictly 23 h 56 min).
  2. A circular orbit above the equator.
  3. Motion from west to east, the same direction as the Earth's spin.
  • With these three conditions, Kepler's third law allows only one radius: 4.23 × 107 m (6.6 Earth radii).
  • Geosynchronous orbit: also a one-day period, but the orbit may be tilted. Then the satellite drifts north and south in the sky, so it is not geostationary.

  • In both orbits, gravity provides the centripetal force.
4.5

Key experiments

Factors that affect g

Aim
To show that g at a body's surface is proportional to M/r² (M: mass of the body, r: radius of the body).
Method
Use secondary data (data measured by others) for the Moon, Mercury, Mars, Venus and the Earth. Plot g against M/r².
Results
A straight line through the origin, with gradient 6.67 × 10−11 N m² kg−2.
Conclusion
g ∝ M/r², and the gradient is G, as g = GM/r² predicts.
Significance
M = gr²/G gives a planet's mass. Cavendish found G in 1798 ("weighing the Earth").

Kepler's third law and the moons of Jupiter

Aim
To show that all of Jupiter's large moons share one value of r³/T², and to find Jupiter's mass.
Method
Use r and T (T in seconds) of four moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. Plot r³ against T².
Results
A straight line through the origin, with gradient 3.205 × 1015 m³ s−2.
Conclusion
r³ ∝ T². Gradient = GM/4π², so Jupiter's mass is 1.90 × 1027 kg.
Significance
Galileo (1610) was the first to see objects orbiting another planet. This supported the Sun-centred model (the Sun is at the centre).

Orbits in a simulation: mass, radius, speed and period

Aim
To find how a satellite's period and speed depend on its own mass and on its orbital radius.
Method
A computer orbit model (planet mass 6.0 × 1024 kg). The satellite is launched at 90° to the radius, at the speed that gives a circular path. 5 orbits are timed. First only the satellite's mass is changed (100 kg, 1000 kg, 5000 kg), then only r (7.0 × 106 to 4.0 × 107 m).
Results
The same period for every mass (T = 5817 s at r = 7.0 × 106 m). A bigger r gave a longer T and a lower v, but v²r = 4.00 × 1014 m³ s−2 at every radius.
Conclusion
The period does not depend on the satellite's mass. v²r = GM, so v ∝ 1/√r, as v = √(GM/r) predicts.
Significance
All objects at one height move together, whatever their mass: an astronaut can float beside the space station. Animation 3 is this experiment.

Common mistakes

Wrong: using the distance between the two surfaces as r, or squaring only part of it.
Right: r is centre to centre. Square all of r.
Wrong: using the height h directly as r; not changing km to m.
Right: r = rE + h, in m. For the ISS, using h = 420 km as r gives g ≈ 2.3 × 10³ m s−2, which is clearly wrong.
Wrong: using the satellite's mass as M in v = √(GM/r).
Right: M is the mass of the central body (the planet). The satellite's mass cancels.
Wrong: a heavier satellite must move faster to stay in the same orbit.
Right: at the same height, the speed and period are the same, whatever the satellite's mass.
Wrong: astronauts on the ISS float because there is no gravity there.
Right: g = 8.7 m s−2 there. The astronauts and the station fall together, so the normal force = 0.
Wrong: using T in hours or days; taking a square root to find r.
Right: first change T to seconds. Use a cube root to find r.
Wrong: the r from the cube root is the height.
Right: it is the distance from the Earth's centre. The height is h = r − rE.
Wrong: a planet moves around the Sun at a constant speed.
Right: on an elliptical orbit, it is fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion (equal areas).
Wrong: geosynchronous is the same as geostationary.
Right: geosynchronous only needs a period of one day, and may be tilted. Geostationary must also be above the equator and move from west to east.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. The distance between two objects becomes 3 times as big. What happens to the gravitational force between them?

It becomes 1/9 of the original. F ∝ 1/r². Distance × 3, r² × 9, so force ÷ 9.

2. The ISS is 420 km up. What is g there? (Use G, the Earth's mass and the Earth's radius from the data sheet.)

8.7 m s−2. First find r = 6.371 × 106 + 4.2 × 105 = 6.791 × 106 m. g = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10−11 × 6.0 × 1024 / (6.791 × 106)² = 8.7 m s−2. This is only a little less than the 9.8 at the ground.

3. If g = 8.7 m s−2 at the ISS, why do astronauts float?

Because the normal force = 0, not because gravity = 0. The astronauts and the station are in free fall together. They fall together, so the floor does not have to push on them. Gravity is still there, and it acts exactly as the centripetal force.

4. Continuing from Question 2, what are the ISS's orbital speed and period, roughly?

v ≈ 7.7 km s−1, T ≈ 5.56 × 10³ s (about 93 min). v = √(GM/r) = √(6.67 × 10−11 × 6.0 × 1024 / 6.791 × 106) ≈ 7.68 × 10³ m s−1. T = 2πr/v = 2π × 6.791 × 106 / 7.68 × 10³ ≈ 5.56 × 10³ s ≈ 93 min, which agrees with "about 90 min" in Table 4.1.

5. Two satellites are in the same circular orbit: one is 100 kg, the other is 5000 kg. Which one moves faster?

They move at the same speed, and have the same period. GMm/r² = mv²/r. The m (satellite's mass) on both sides cancels. v = √(GM/r) has only the Earth's mass M in it.

6. A geostationary satellite has a period of one day (86 400 s). What are its orbital radius and its height above the ground?

r = 4.23 × 107 m, h ≈ 3.59 × 107 m (about 36 000 km). r³ = GMT²/4π² = 6.67 × 10−11 × 6.0 × 1024 × 86 400² / 4π² ≈ 7.57 × 1022 m³. The cube root gives r = 4.23 × 107 m. h = r − rE = 4.23 × 107 − 6.371 × 106 ≈ 3.59 × 107 m. (Using the strict value 23 h 56 min = 86 160 s gives 4.22 × 107 m.)

7. A planet orbits the Sun with a semi-major axis of 4 AU. What is its period in years?

8 years. Both orbit the Sun, so compare with the Earth (1 AU, 1 year): r1³/T1² = r2³/T2², 1³/1² = 4³/T², T² = 64, T = 8 years. You can use AU and years. You do not need to change to m and s.

8. A comet's distance from the Sun at aphelion is 3 times its distance at perihelion. How many times faster is it at perihelion than at aphelion?

3 times. rpvp = rava, ra = 3rp, so vp = 3va. Near the Sun the line is short, so the comet must cover a longer arc in the same time (equal areas).
