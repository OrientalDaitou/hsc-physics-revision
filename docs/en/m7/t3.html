Light as a Particle: Black Bodies and the Photoelectric Effect

Explained from zero

This chapter is about two things the wave model cannot explain: how hot things give off light (black body curves), and how light knocks electrons out of a metal (the photoelectric effect). The answer is the same idea: light energy comes in packets, and one packet is E = hf. Remember the three rules below. All the later formulas come from these three. The photoelectric formulas are all just conservation of energy.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. The order of the EM spectrum: radio → microwaves → infrared → visible (red to violet) → UV (ultraviolet) → X-rays → gamma. The further along, the higher the f and the shorter the λ. In a vacuum c = fλ, with c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1 (Chapter 1).
  2. Temperature must be changed to kelvin (K): T(K) = T(°C) + 273. For example, 27 °C = 300 K.
  3. Work done by a voltage: W = qV. The size of the electron's charge is q = 1.602 × 10−19 C. An electron that moves through 1 V gains 1 electron volt (eV) of energy: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J. Kinetic energy: K = ½mv².
  4. The result from Chapter 2: interference and diffraction show that light is a wave. In the wave model, brighter light = bigger amplitude = more energy.
3.1

Black body radiation: the black body curve and Wien's law

Big ideaThe light a black body gives off depends only on its temperature. In 1900 Planck explained why the curve has this shape: energy is given out in packets, and one packet is E = hf.

  • All objects give off EM waves because of their temperature. This is called thermal radiation. The reason: the charges in an object vibrate, and vibrating charges give off EM waves. Things that are not very hot give off mostly infrared, which you cannot see.
  • Black body: an ideal object. It absorbs all the radiation that hits it. It is also a perfect emitter.
  • A hollow box with a tiny hole is almost a black body: light that goes in through the hole cannot get out. Stars are also almost black bodies.
  • Example: lava (about 1000 K to 1500 K) peaks in the infrared and glows dull red. The Sun (5800 K) looks white. Hotter stars look blue-white.
  • Wien (1893): λmax is inversely proportional to T. If T doubles, λmax halves.
  • Intensity: radiation energy each second on each square metre. This is the vertical axis of a black body curve. The horizontal axis is wavelength.

Four features of the black body curve

  1. One peak, at the peak wavelength λmax: the wavelength where the curve is highest. Steep rise on the short-wavelength side, long tail on the long side.
  2. A hotter body is higher at every wavelength. The two curves never cross.
  3. A hotter body peaks at a shorter wavelength.
  4. The curve depends only on temperature, not on the material.

3.1

The ultraviolet catastrophe · Planck's idea: energy comes in packets

Big ideaThe old physics predicted that the shorter the wavelength, the more light there is, all the way to infinity. But the real curve falls to 0. Planck said: energy can only be given in packets. A short-wavelength packet is too big, so it is rarely given out.

Where the old physics went wrong

  • Classical physics (physics before 1900): light is a wave, and energy can have any value.
  • Its prediction for the black body curve is called the Rayleigh–Jeans law. On the long-wavelength side, it fits the measured curve. The longer the wavelength, the closer the two lines come.
  • On the short-wavelength side, it rises without limit. Added up, the total energy would be infinite. But the real curve falls to 0.
  • This wrong prediction is called the ultraviolet catastrophe: the prediction, from physics before 1900, of infinite intensity at short wavelengths (the UV side).
  • Figure 3.2 in the booklet shows T = 5000 K: the classical prediction goes to infinity. The measured curve (Planck's formula fits it) has a peak, then comes back down.

Why it failed: a hot box holds more and more standing-wave patterns at shorter wavelengths. Classical physics gives each pattern the same share of energy. With more and more patterns, the total energy is infinite. It is like giving every child the same number of lollies: with endless children, you need endless lollies.

Planck's idea: energy comes in packets

  • Planck (1900): the vibrating charges in the walls are called oscillators. Their energy is quantised. It comes in steps: only 0, hf, 2hf, 3hf, …. It cannot stop between two steps.
  • So they give out or take in energy only in packets, and one packet is hf. One packet is called a quantum (plural: quanta). h is Planck's constant, 6.626 × 10−34 J s.
  • Classical physics is like a ramp: energy can stop at any height. Planck is like a staircase: you can only stand on a step.
Low f (long wavelength)

Each step hf is tiny, so tiny that the steps act like a ramp. So here, Planck agrees with classical physics.

High f (short wavelength)

One step hf is bigger than the heat energy an oscillator usually has. It can hardly climb the first step. Few quanta are given out, so the curve falls to 0.

  • Analogy: a game machine needs 10 tokens for each go. You usually have only two or three tokens, so you can hardly ever play. A short-wavelength packet hf is these "10 tokens".
  • Planck's formula fits the measured curve at every wavelength. He saw quanta as only a maths trick. But they began the quantum model of light.

3.2

The photoelectric effect: evidence against the wave model · Stopping voltage

Big ideaLight can knock electrons out of a metal. Experiments showed that three predictions of the wave model are wrong.

  • Photoelectric effect: electrons are released from a metal surface when light shines on it.
  • Hertz first saw the effect (1887): UV helped sparks jump a spark gap (a small air gap between two metal balls). Lenard measured it with a vacuum tube (1902).

The apparatus (Figure 3.3 in the booklet)

  • Light shines on a metal cathode and knocks out electrons, called photoelectrons. They cross the vacuum to the anode.
  • A microammeter (a meter for tiny currents) measures the photocurrent. Bigger current = more electrons each second, not faster ones.
  • The tube is a vacuum: electrons do not hit air molecules. The window is made of quartz, which lets UV in (glass absorbs UV).
  • The circuit has a power supply that can be connected the other way round, and a voltmeter (reversible supply and voltmeter). In Figure 3.3 in the booklet, the anode is negative. This is the connection used to find V0.
  • Electroscope demonstration: an electroscope has a thin gold leaf that stands out when it is charged. Put a negatively charged zinc plate on it. Shine UV on it, and the leaf falls: electrons leave the zinc. Shine very bright visible light on it, and the leaf does not move.

Stopping voltage

  • Make the anode negative. It pushes the electrons back.
  • Electrons leave with a range of speeds. The fastest have the maximum kinetic energy, Kmax.
  • Slowly raise the reverse voltage. At V0 even the fastest electrons stop just before the anode, so I = 0. V0 is called the stopping voltage: the smallest reverse voltage that stops every photoelectron, so the current is 0.
  • Analogy: rolling balls up a hill. The higher the hill (the reverse voltage), the faster a ball must go to get over it. V0 is the height at which even the fastest ball only just fails to get over.
  • V0 is a voltage (V); Kmax is an energy (J or eV). In eV, Kmax has the same number as V0 in volts. For example, V0 = 2.04 V, Kmax = 2.04 eV.
  • Speed of the fastest electrons: put Kmax, in J, into K = ½mv² (on the sheet), with m = the electron's mass.

3.2

What the wave model predicted

Big ideaIn the wave model, light energy depends on brightness, not frequency, and it arrives slowly and evenly, spread over the whole metal surface. Experiments show that three of the four predictions are wrong.

In the wave model, light energy depends on brightness (amplitude), not frequency. It arrives smoothly, spread over the whole surface (Table 3.1). It is like drizzle: it falls evenly on all the ground, and every spot slowly gets wet.

The wave model predictedExperiments showedRight?
Brighter light gives more electrons each second (bigger current).Brighter light gives a bigger current (when f ≥ f0).Right
Any frequency works if the light is bright enough.There is a threshold frequency f0: below it, no electrons, however bright.Wrong
Dim light: a delay while electrons build up energy.No delay: electrons leave at once (in less than 10−9 s).Wrong
Brighter light makes faster electrons (bigger V0).V0 does not depend on brightness. It rises in a straight line with f.Wrong

Threshold frequency: the lowest frequency of light that releases electrons from a metal.

Wave model: energy spread out

Energy depends on brightness and falls evenly over the whole surface. Each electron gets only a tiny share and must slowly build it up. Like drizzle.

Experiment: energy in packets

Energy is concentrated in packets, and each packet is hf. One packet hits one electron and gives all its energy at once. Like hail: one hailstone hits one spot.

Conclusion: light energy cannot arrive spread out like a wave. It must arrive in concentrated packets, and each packet is hf.

3.3

Einstein's photon model

Big ideaIn 1905 Einstein said that light itself is a stream of photons, each with energy hf. One photon gives all its energy to one electron, so conservation of energy gives Kmax = hf − ϕ.

  • Photon: one packet (quantum) of light, with energy E = hf.
  • Brighter light of one colour has more photons each second. Each photon still has the same energy hf.
  • One photon interacts with only one electron, and gives it all its energy at once. Energy from two photons never adds up. It is like a machine that takes only one coin: it needs 5 dollars, and you put in 1-dollar coins. Even a hundred of them will not work.
  • Each metal has its own work function ϕ: the least energy to remove an electron from the metal's surface. For example, caesium 2.1 eV, zinc 4.3 eV.
Einstein's photoelectric equationOn the formula sheet
Kmax = hf − ϕ
Kmax
maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons (J or eV)
h
Planck's constant, 6.626 × 10−34 J s (J s)
f
frequency of the light (Hz)
ϕ
work function of the metal (J or eV)

When to use: light falls on a metal and hf ≥ ϕ. If hf − ϕ is negative, no electrons are released.
Where it comes from: conservation of energy for one photon and the least-bound electron (the easiest to remove): energy in = energy to escape + energy left over. So hf = ϕ + Kmax, and Kmax = hf − ϕ. Deeper electrons need more than ϕ, so they leave slower.
Trap: use one unit for every term: all J or all eV. ϕ is lower-case phi; capital Φ on the sheet is magnetic flux.

The photon model explains every result in Table 3.1

  • Bigger current: more photons each second free more electrons each second.
  • Threshold: one photon must give at least ϕ, so hf ≥ ϕ.
  • No delay: each photon delivers its whole energy in one event.
  • Kmax depends only on f (and the metal), not on brightness: brightness changes the number of photons, not hf.

Only just enough to release an electron: the threshold

  • At the threshold f0 the fastest electron has nothing left over. Put Kmax = 0 in Kmax = hf − ϕ to get ϕ = hf0.
  • The threshold wavelength λ0 is the longest wavelength that releases electrons. Electrons leave only if f ≥ f0 (λ ≤ λ0), however bright the light.

How many photons each second

  • For a source of power P, the number of photons given out each second = energy each second ÷ energy of one photon: N/t = P/hf.
  • At the same power, red light gives more photons than blue: red light has a lower f, so each photon has less energy, and it takes more of them to make up the same energy.

Planck, Einstein and a new model of light

Planck (1900)

He quantised the oscillators in the walls. He saw this as a maths trick. It explained the black body curve.

Einstein (1905)

He quantised light itself: photons are real. This explained the photoelectric effect.

  • Both used E = hf.
  • Light still shows interference and diffraction. But it is given out and absorbed as photons, one at a time. This is called wave–particle duality: light acts as a wave in some experiments, as particles in others.
  • Note: photons are not Newton's corpuscles.
3.3

Photoelectric graphs

Big ideaKmax = hf − ϕ is a straight line: the gradient is h, it crosses the horizontal axis at f0, and if you extend it to the left, it crosses the vertical axis at −ϕ.

The Kmax–f graph (Figure 3.4 in the booklet)

  • It is the same as y = mx + c: y is Kmax, x is f, the gradient is h, and the intercept is −ϕ.
  • Gradient = h for every metal, so the lines are parallel. With Kmax in eV, gradient = 4.136 × 10−15 eV s.
  • f-intercept = f0: where the line crosses the horizontal axis.
  • Kmax-intercept = −ϕ: you must extrapolate: extend the line, dotted, to f = 0, read the intercept, then change the sign. The dotted part has no electrons. It is only an extension of the line.

The I–V graph (Figure 3.5 in the booklet, idealised)

The horizontal axis is the tube voltage V. The vertical axis is the photocurrent I. On the right the anode is positive. On the left the anode is negative (it pushes electrons back). The right-hand graph in Animation 3 shows this.

  • Anode positive: all electrons are collected. The flat part is called the saturation current: the largest photocurrent, when every released electron reaches the anode. Its height is set by the number of photons each second.
  • Anode negative: electrons are pushed back and the current gets smaller. At −V0 the current = 0.
  • In the graph, beam A has more photons each second than beam B (higher flat part) and a higher f (bigger V0).
  • Brighter light, same f: only the flat part rises.
  • Bigger ϕ, same light: smaller V0.
3.4

Key experiments

Measuring black body radiation (1899–1900)

Aim
To measure black body curves at several temperatures (Lummer and Pringsheim).
Method
Radiation leaves a small hole in a hot oven (this is the black body). A thermocouple (a thermometer) measures the oven's temperature. The radiation passes through a slit and a prism: long wavelengths bend least, short wavelengths bend most (Figure 3.6 in the booklet). A bolometer (a detector warmed by the radiation) is moved bit by bit along the spectrum. This is repeated at several temperatures.
Results
Curves like Figure 3.1. λmaxT ≈ 2.9 × 10−3 m K at every temperature.
Conclusion
Wien's law. Classical theory fitted only the long-wavelength side.
Significance
Planck's formula fitted the whole curve. This led him to quanta (1900).

The photoelectric effect: measuring h and ϕ

Aim
To find how V0 depends on frequency and brightness (Lenard 1902, Millikan 1914–1916, or a school photocell).
Method
In the circuit of Figure 3.3, slowly raise the reverse voltage until the current is just 0 (this is V0). Repeat for several frequencies and brightnesses.
Results
V0 rises in a straight line with f. No current below f0. Brightness changes only the current, not V0.
Conclusion
qV0 = hf − ϕ, so h = q × gradient; the two intercepts give f0 and ϕ. This supports the photon model.
Significance
Millikan (1914–1916) expected to prove Einstein wrong. Instead he confirmed the law and found h within about 1 % of today's value.

1. A piece of metal is heated to 727 °C. What is its λmax? Which part of the spectrum is the peak in?

2.90 × 10−6 m (about 2898 nm), in the infrared. First change to kelvin: 727 + 273 = 1000 K. λmax = 2.898 × 10−3 / 1000 = 2.898 × 10−6 m. As with lava, the peak is in the infrared, and it gives off only a little dull red light.

2. Why did classical physics predict the ultraviolet catastrophe? How did Planck solve it?

Classical physics gives each standing-wave pattern the same share of energy; Planck made energy come in packets. The shorter the wavelength, the more patterns, so the total energy is infinite. Planck: an oscillator can give out only hf at a time. At short wavelengths hf is bigger than the usual heat energy, so it is rarely given out, and the curve falls to 0.

3. Light with f = 10 × 1014 Hz shines on caesium (ϕ = 2.1 eV). What is the energy of one photon in eV? What are Kmax and V0?

hf ≈ 4.14 eV; Kmax ≈ 2.04 eV; V0 ≈ 2.04 V. E = 6.626 × 10−34 × 1.0 × 1015 = 6.626 × 10−19 J. Divide by 1.602 × 10−19 to get 4.136 eV. Kmax = 4.136 − 2.1 = 2.04 eV. In eV, V0 has the same number: 2.04 V.

4. Very bright visible light shines on a zinc plate and the gold leaf does not move. UV makes the leaf fall at once. Why?

What matters is the energy of each photon, not the brightness. Visible light has a low f, so each photon's hf is smaller than zinc's ϕ = 4.3 eV (for example, a 500 nm photon has only 2.48 eV). One photon gives its energy to only one electron, and two photons cannot add up, so however bright the light, no electrons are knocked out. UV has a high f, so hf is big enough. One photon can knock out one electron, and it leaves at once.

5. Same f, but the light is 2 times as bright. What happens to the saturation current, Kmax and V0?

Saturation current × 2; Kmax and V0 do not change. Brighter = twice as many photons each second = twice as many electrons each second. Each photon still has hf, so the energy of the fastest electrons does not change.

6. Zinc has ϕ = 4.3 eV. What is its threshold wavelength?

About 289 nm (UV). ϕ = 4.3 × 1.602 × 10−19 = 6.89 × 10−19 J. λ0 = hc/ϕ = 6.626 × 10−34 × 3.00 × 108 / (6.89 × 10−19) ≈ 2.89 × 10−7 m = 289 nm. Light with a longer wavelength than this cannot knock out electrons.

7. A 1 W light source gives out light with f = 5 × 1014 Hz. How many photons does it give out each second?

About 3.0 × 1018. One photon: hf = 6.626 × 10−34 × 5 × 1014 = 3.313 × 10−19 J. N/t = P/hf = 1 / (3.313 × 10−19) ≈ 3.0 × 1018 photons each second.

8. On a Kmax–f graph, why are the caesium and zinc lines parallel? Where do they cross the vertical axis?

Both have gradient h; they cross at −2.10 eV and −4.30 eV. Kmax = hf − ϕ, and the gradient h does not depend on the metal. The vertical-axis intercept = −ϕ. You must extrapolate the dotted line to f = 0 to read it.
