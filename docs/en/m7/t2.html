Light as a Wave: Diffraction, Interference and Polarisation

Explained from zero

Is light a particle or a wave? Newton said particles, Huygens said a wave. This chapter looks at how experiments decided. Remember the three rules below: the later formulas for interference, gratings and polarisation all use them.

Rule ①
Superposition

When two waves meet, their heights add (superposition). Crest meets crest → brighter. Crest meets trough → they cancel → dark. "Light + light = darkness": only waves can do this.

Rule ②
Bright fringes

When the extra path for light from neighbouring slits is exactly a whole number of wavelengths, the crests line up and a bright fringe appears. The double slit and the grating both use this rule.

Rule ③
Light is a transverse wave

Light's electric field vibrates at 90° to the direction of travel, so a polariser can "select a direction". When polarised light meets another polariser with its axis at θ to the previous one, the brightness changes as cos²θ.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Some words for waves: crest (the highest point), trough (the lowest point), wavelength λ (the distance from one crest to the next), amplitude (how high a crest is).
  2. Visible light has λ between 400 nm and 700 nm: violet is shortest, red is longest.
  3. Normal: a line at 90° to a surface. Angles of rays are always measured from the normal, not from the surface.
  4. Change units into m first: 1 nm = 10−9 m, 1 µm = 10−6 m, 1 mm = 10−3 m. Calculator in degrees. cos²θ means (cos θ)².
2.1

Newton's and Huygens' models of light

Big ideaAround 1700 Newton said light is a stream of tiny particles. Huygens said light is a wave. Experiments decided between these two models.

  • Newton (1704): light is a stream of tiny particles, called corpuscles ("little bodies"). They travel in straight lines, so the edges of shadows are sharp.
  • Huygens (1678): light is a wave. He thought it was a longitudinal wave, like sound.
  • Wavefront: a line joining points of a wave that are in step, such as all the crests. Drop a stone into water: the rings of crests are wavefronts.
  • Huygens' principle: every point on a wavefront sends out a small circular wave, called a wavelet. The line touching all the wavelets is the new wavefront.
  • Light seems not to bend around corners, and Newton had a huge reputation. So most scientists followed Newton for about 100 years.
  • Reflection: Newton said a repulsive force (a force that pushes things away from the surface) at the surface reverses the normal part of the velocity. Huygens said reflected wavelets build the reflected wavefront. Both give θi = θr (angle of incidence = angle of reflection), so reflection cannot decide.

2.1

Refraction

Big ideaLight entering water at an angle bends towards the normal. Both models can explain this, but they need opposite speeds: Newton needs light to be faster in water, Huygens needs it to be slower.

  • Refraction: the bending of light as it enters another material.
  • Both models explain why light bends towards the normal in water. But they need opposite speeds, so measuring the speed of light in water can show which one is wrong.
Newton (particles): faster

The water pulls each particle inwards along the normal. Split the velocity into two parts: the normal part (at 90° to the water surface) grows, and the parallel part (parallel to the water surface) does not change. So light bends towards the normal and is faster in water.

Huygens (waves): slower

The end of the wavefront that enters the water first slows first, while the rest is still moving fast in air. The whole wavefront swings towards the normal. So light is slower in water. It is like a row of people holding hands and walking at an angle into sand: the person who steps into the sand first slows first, and the whole row changes direction.

  • Foucault (1850) timed light with a fast-spinning mirror. The returning image shifted more with a tube of water in the path. So light is slower in water.
  • This disproved Newton's explanation of refraction. But on its own, this one experiment did not prove the wave model.
  • Refractive index (n): how many times slower light is in a material than in vacuum.

2.2

Diffraction

Big ideaWaves spread out as they pass through a gap or around an edge. This is diffraction. The narrower the gap (compared with the wavelength), the more the wave spreads.

  • Diffraction: a wave spreading as it passes through a gap or around an edge.
  • Gap much wider than λ: little spreading. Gap about λ, or narrower: strong spreading, far into the "shadow".
  • λ, frequency and speed do not change; only the direction does.

The single-slit pattern

  • The pattern of a single slit: a wide, bright central maximum in the middle, with much weaker bright bands on both sides and dark bands between them.
  • Dark bands: wavelets from different parts of the slit cancel here.
  • A narrower slit width (a) or a longer λ → a wider pattern. Red spreads more than violet.
  • It depends only on λ ÷ a. No single-slit formula is needed.
  • The booklet's figure: the same red laser shines through two single slits of different widths (the wide one is 2 times as wide as the narrow one), and the two patterns are given the same peak height. The narrow slit's pattern is wider.

Why it spreads

  • Huygens' explanation: only the points inside the gap send out wavelets. The wavelets at the edges spread sideways into the shadow.
  • Light's λ (400 nm to 700 nm) is over a million times smaller than a doorway, so the edges of shadows usually look sharp. To see light diffract, use a slit narrower than about 0.1 mm.
  • The wavelength of sound is about the size of a doorway. So when you stand behind the wall next to a classroom door (not in front of the doorway), you can still hear people talking inside. Light's wavelength is far too small, so light hardly bends around the edge of the door and you cannot see the people inside.

The two models

  • Particles cannot make dark bands: adding light cannot make a place darker.
  • Waves explain all of it: the spreading, the dark bands and why red spreads more.
  • In 1818 Fresnel's wave theory predicted a bright spot in the middle of a small disc's shadow. Arago did find this bright spot.
2.3

Interference: Young's double-slit experiment

Big ideaWhere two waves overlap, they add or cancel. This is interference. Only waves can cancel to make darkness, so Young's double slit is strong evidence that light is a wave.

  • Superposition: where waves meet, their displacements (heights) add.
  • Interference: the pattern made when overlapping waves add or cancel.
In phase

Crests line up. Crest meets crest and makes a bigger wave. This is constructive interference: bright.

180° out of phase

One wave is half a wavelength behind. Crest meets trough and they cancel. This is destructive interference: dark. "Light + light = darkness" only makes sense for waves.

Young's experiment (about 1801)

  • Young passed light through two narrow slits very close together. A far screen showed evenly spaced bright and dark bands, called fringes.
  • Why the waves overlap: light diffracts at each slit. After it spreads out, the two waves overlap.
  • Set-up (the booklet's Figure 2.3): light source → single slit → double slit (two slits S1 and S2, a distance d apart) → screen (a distance L from the double slit). The middle is a bright fringe (m = 0). Going outwards, you see dark, bright (m = 1), dark, bright (m = 2), and so on. The figure is not to scale: really L ≫ d (L is much bigger than d). The picture on the left of Animation 3, in the next section, shows this set-up (view from above).

The two slits must be coherent

  • The two slits must be coherent: same frequency and a fixed phase difference (a constant shift between the crests of the two waves). It is like two people walking with steps of the same size and the same speed: one can be half a step behind the other, but this gap never changes.
  • Young lit both slits from one pinhole. Today a single slit or a laser does this.
  • With two separate lamps, the phase changes at random, so the pattern washes out.
  • Monochromatic light (one wavelength) gives the clearest fringes.
2.3

Path difference and d sin θ = mλ

Big ideaLook at how much further one wave travels than the other. A whole number of wavelengths further → in phase → bright. A whole number of wavelengths plus a half further → 180° out of phase → dark.

  • Path difference (δ, unit: m): how much further one wave travels than the other.
  • Bright: δ = 0, λ, 2λ, … (in phase). Dark: δ = ½λ, 1½λ, … (180° out of phase).
  • The centre has δ = 0, so it is always bright.
  • The screen is far away, so the two rays from the slits to a point P on the screen are almost parallel. Then δ = d sin θ.
  • Look at the booklet's Figure 2.4 (a close-up of the double slit), or Animation 3 below: from S1, draw a line at 90° to the ray from S2. The point where they meet is called Q. The part from S2 to Q is the extra path for light from S2: δ = d sin θ.

The pattern

  • Each dark fringe is halfway between two bright fringes.
  • The single-slit pattern of each slit (the envelope) dims the outer fringes.
  • A longer λ (red), a further screen, or closer slits → the fringes are further apart.
  • White light: the central fringe is white (δ is 0 for every colour at the centre). Beside it are coloured fringes: blue inside, red outside.

Impact

  • Light can cancel light. Young also measured the wavelengths of colours.
  • But because of Newton's reputation, most scientists accepted the wave model only after Fresnel and Arago (1818–1819) and Foucault (1850).
2.4

Diffraction gratings

Big ideaA grating has many slits, very close together. So its bright lines are sharp, bright and far apart: ideal for measuring wavelengths.

  • Diffraction grating: a slide with many equally spaced, parallel slits, about 100 to 1000 per mm. The tracks of a CD also act like a grating.
  • It makes sharp, bright lines where d sin θ = mλ. It also splits white light into a spectrum (a band of all the colours, in order of wavelength).
  • Sharp, bright lines: the waves from all the slits are in step only at the exact angle where d sin θ = mλ. Just off that angle, they cancel. It is like a big crowd clapping together: the sound is loud only when the claps match exactly.
  • Large angles: d is tiny, so the lines are far apart and easy to measure. Use d sin θ = mλ and y = L tan θ, not Δy = λL/d.
  • White light: the central line (m = 0) is white. Each order is a spectrum: violet nearest the centre, red furthest out. This is the opposite of a prism: a prism bends violet most.

2.5

Polarisation and Malus' law

Big ideaIn light, the electric field vibrates at 90° to the direction of travel. Only such transverse waves can be polarised, so polarisation tells us what kind of wave light is.

  • Light is a transverse wave: its electric and magnetic fields vibrate at 90° to the direction of travel. In a longitudinal wave (such as sound) the vibration is along the direction of travel.
  • Analogy: a rope shaken up and down passes a vertical gap in a fence, but not a horizontal one. Only transverse waves have a sideways vibration to select.
  • Sunlight is unpolarised light: the electric field vibrates in every direction across the beam, at random.
  • A polariser passes only the part of the vibration along its transmission axis. The light then becomes plane-polarised light: its electric field vibrates in one direction only.
  • A second filter, the analyser, passes only the part along its own axis.
  • Intensity (I): the power of light falling on each square metre. Unit: W m−2.

Looking along the beam, step by step (the booklet's Figure 2.5)

  1. Unpolarised light, intensity I0: the vibration is in every direction.
  2. Through the polariser (vertical axis): only the vertical vibration is left, with intensity ½I0. No direction is special, so on average half the energy is along the axis. Whatever angle the polariser is turned to, it is always half.
  3. Through the analyser (axis turned by θ): of the amplitude A, only the part A cos θ along the new axis passes. Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, so ½I0 cos²θ is left.

Two graphs

I against θ

A cos² curve: greatest at 0° and 180°, and 0 at 90°.

I against cos²θ

A straight line through the origin, gradient = Imax. The experiment in the booklet's Figure 2.6: the points from 0° to 90° and from 105° to 180° all lie on this line or very close to it, gradient = 12.0 W m−2.

Three filters

  • Two filters at 90° (crossed): all the light is blocked.
  • Add a 45° filter between them: now light passes! This is because each filter re-polarises the light along its own axis.
  • Uses: glare from water is partly polarised horizontally. Polarising sunglasses have a vertical transmission axis, so they block much of the glare.

Why polarisation matters for the model of light

  • Light is a transverse wave, so Huygens' longitudinal model had to change (Young 1817, Fresnel 1821).
  • Newton's particles had no natural explanation. He could only guess that rays have "sides".
  • Maxwell's theory (1865) predicts a transverse electromagnetic wave, which fits polarisation and Malus' law exactly. So polarisation was highly significant: with interference, it completed the classical model of light as a transverse electromagnetic wave.
2.6

Key experiments

Diffraction and interference with lasers

Aim
(A) How diffraction depends on slit width and wavelength. (B) Measure a red laser's wavelength (label: 650 nm) from d sin θ = mλ.
Method
(A) Red and green lasers through single slits 0.080 mm, 0.040 mm and 0.020 mm wide.
(B) Double slit (d = 0.250 mm): Δy = distance across 10 fringe spacings ÷ 10 (measuring 10 and averaging gives a smaller error), for L from 1.00 m to 3.00 m. Grating (300 lines per mm): y = half the distance between the two first-order spots.
Safety
Never look into the beam of a laser.
Results
(A) Halving the slit width made the central maximum 2 times as wide; red was wider than green.
(B) Δy against L: a straight line through the origin, gradient = λ/d (so λ = gradient × d). Double slit: 648 nm; grating: 649 nm.
Conclusion
Light diffracts and interferes like a wave. Both values are within about 0.3 % of 650 nm.
Significance
Particles cannot cancel to make dark fringes. This is strong evidence that light is a wave.

Testing Malus' law

Aim
To test I = Imax cos²θ for plane-polarised light.
Method
Lamp, fixed polariser, analyser on an angle scale, light sensor; in a dark room. Turn the analyser in 15° steps from the brightest position (θ = 0°) to 180°.
Results
I against cos²θ: a straight line through the origin, gradient Imax = 12.0 W m−2 (the booklet's Figure 2.6).
Conclusion
I ∝ cos²θ: Malus' law is supported.
Significance
Light can be polarised, so it is a transverse wave, as Maxwell predicted.

Wrong: Foucault's experiment proved that light is a wave.
Right: it only disproved Newton's explanation of refraction. On its own, it did not prove the wave model.
Wrong: after light passes through a narrow slit, its wavelength has changed.
Right: in diffraction, λ, frequency and speed do not change; only the direction does.
Wrong: putting nm, µm or mm straight into d sin θ = mλ.
Right: change into m first: 650 nm = 650 × 10−9 m, 0.250 mm = 0.250 × 10−3 m. Calculator in degrees.
Wrong: in Δy = λL/d, d is the width of one slit.
Right: d is the slit separation: the distance between the centres of the two slits.
Wrong: gratings also use Δy = λL/d.
Right: grating angles are large, so use d sin θ = mλ and y = L tan θ. Δy = λL/d is only for below about 10°.
Wrong: a grating is like a prism: it bends violet most.
Right: in each order of a grating, violet is nearest the centre and red is furthest out. A prism is the opposite: it bends violet most.
Wrong: putting m = 0 into the dark-fringe formula.
Right: in d sin θ = (m − ½)λ, m starts at 1.
Wrong: highest order = d/λ rounded to the nearest whole number.
Right: round down, because sin θ cannot be more than 1.
Wrong: cos²θ means cos(θ²).
Right: cos²θ means (cos θ)²: find cos θ first, then square it.
Wrong: in Malus' law, θ is measured from the vertical.
Right: θ is measured from the axis of the previous filter.

1. When light enters water, it bends towards the normal. Did Newton predict that light is faster or slower in water? Did Huygens? Who was right?

Newton: faster; Huygens: slower. Foucault (1850) measured slower. Newton thought the water pulls the particles inwards, so the speed gets bigger. Huygens thought the end of the wavefront that enters the water first slows first. The experiment shows that Newton's explanation of refraction was wrong.

2. The speed of light in a material is 2.00 × 108 m s−1. What is the refractive index of this material?

n = 1.50. n = c/v = 3.00 × 108 ÷ (2.00 × 108) = 1.50. It has no unit. n is more than 1, so light is slower in the material than in vacuum.

3. Why are the "dark bands" in single-slit and double-slit patterns evidence that light is a wave?

Particles added to particles can only make it brighter, not dark. Only waves can have "crest meets trough" and cancel, so "light + light = darkness" only makes sense for waves.

4. A double slit has d = 0.250 mm, a red laser has λ = 650 nm, and the screen is at L = 1.00 m. What is the fringe spacing Δy?

Δy = 2.60 × 10−3 m = 2.60 mm. First change the units: d = 0.250 × 10−3 m, λ = 650 × 10−9 m. Δy = λL/d = 650 × 10−9 × 1.00 ÷ (0.250 × 10−3) = 2.60 × 10−3 m. The angle is only about 0.15°, which is below 10°, so you can use this formula.

5. Two speakers in phase send out sound with λ = 2 m. Point P is 5 m from one speaker and 8 m from the other. Is the sound at P loud or quiet?

Very quiet (destructive). δ = |8 − 5| = 3 m, δ/λ = 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5. This is a whole number plus a half, so the two waves are 180° out of phase and cancel.

6. A grating has 300 lines per mm and λ = 650 nm. What is the angle of the first-order bright line? What is the highest order you can see?

θ1 ≈ 11.2°; highest m = 5. N = 300 × 1000 = 3.00 × 105 lines per metre, d = 1/N = 3.33 × 10−6 m. Dividing by d is the same as multiplying by N: sin θ1 = λ/d = λN = 650 × 10−9 × 3.00 × 105 = 0.195, θ1 = 11.2°. d/λ = 1 ÷ (Nλ) = 1 ÷ 0.195 = 5.13, rounded down gives 5.

7. In the Malus experiment, Imax = 12.0 W m−2. When the analyser is turned to 60°, what should I be?

I = 3.0 W m−2. cos 60° = 0.5, cos² 60° = 0.25. I = 12.0 × 0.25 = 3.0 W m−2.

8. Unpolarised light I0 goes through two filters at 90°: completely dark. Add a 45° filter between them. How much is left at the end?

⅛I0. First filter: ½I0. The middle filter is 45° from the first: ½I0 × cos² 45° = ½I0 × ½ = ¼I0. The last filter is another 45° from the middle one: ¼I0 × ½ = ⅛I0. Each filter re-polarises the light along its own axis.
