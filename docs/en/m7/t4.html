Light and Special Relativity

Explained from zero

This chapter starts from one strange fact: however fast you run, you measure the same speed for light. From this fact we get: moving clocks run slow, moving objects get shorter, nothing with mass can ever catch light, and mass can turn into energy. Remember the three rules below. The whole chapter just uses them.

  1. Constant speed: s = vt, distance = speed × time. You need this in the muon section.

4.1

Frames of reference and Einstein's postulates

Big ideaMotion is always measured from a viewpoint. Einstein said that no steady viewpoint is special, and that everyone measures the same speed for light in a vacuum.

  • Frame of reference: a viewpoint, with its own rulers and clocks. A platform is one frame. A train is another frame.
  • Inertial frame: a frame that is not accelerating: at rest or moving in a straight line at constant speed. Note: what stays constant is the velocity (this includes the direction).
  • Non-inertial frame: a frame that speeds up, slows down or turns. The Earth's surface is usually treated as an inertial frame.
  • Event: something that happens at one place and one instant, such as a flash.
  • Postulate: a starting statement that a theory takes as true. Rules ① and ② above are Einstein's two postulates.
  • Galilean relativity: the laws of motion are the same in every inertial frame, and everyday velocities can simply be added. Throwing a ball on a train moving at constant velocity is the same as throwing it on the ground. Einstein's postulate 1 extends it to all of physics.

The speed of light is "relative to" what?

  • Maxwell's equations give one speed for light: c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1. But they do not say relative to what.
  • At the time, light was thought to need a medium (a substance that carries a wave, as air carries sound). So people assumed that space was filled with something invisible, called the luminiferous aether.
  • The Earth moves through space at about 30 km s−1. If there were an aether, an aether wind would blow across the Earth, and light's speed should change with direction: faster with the wind, slower against it.
  • Michelson and Morley (1887) tried to measure this change, and they found no change at all. This is called a null result (no effect found).
  • The instrument they used is called an interferometer. Light leaves a source, and a half-silvered mirror splits it into two beams at right angles. Each beam goes along an arm to a mirror and comes back. The two beams recombine and enter a telescope, making fringes (bright and dark bands). If the speed of light changes, the fringes shift. The details of the experiment are in 4.5. First look at Animation 1.

4.1

Einstein's two postulates (1905)

Big ideaAll of special relativity rests on just two postulates. Learn their exact wording, word for word.

Special relativity: the theory of how space and time behave for people in inertial frames.

  1. All inertial frames of reference are equivalent.
    The laws of physics, including those of light, are the same in every inertial frame. No experiment can show which inertial frame is "really" at rest.
  2. The speed of light in a vacuum is an absolute constant.
    Every inertial observer measures c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1, however fast the source or the observer moves.
  • By postulate 1, Maxwell's equations hold in every inertial frame, so every frame calculates the same c.
  • Velocities do not add for light: when a car is moving, its headlight beam still travels at c, not c + v.
  • Two people moving relative to each other measure the same beam of light, and both get c. There is only one way this can be true: they measure different distances, different times (or both). 4.2 explains this.
  • c is constant only in a vacuum. Light slows down in glass and water.

4.2

Time dilation and length contraction · Thought experiment 1: lightning and a fast train

Big ideaEveryone measures the same speed for light, so people moving relative to each other must disagree about time and length. The first example: two events happen at the same time for one person, but not for another.

  • Thought experiment: an imagined experiment, worked through step by step with logic. It can explore speeds near c. Nobody could test such speeds in 1905.
  • It is not evidence. It only shows what the theory predicts. Real experiments are needed to test it.

Lightning and a fast train (Figure 4.1 in the booklet)

  1. The train moves to the right at speed v. Peter sits exactly in the middle of the train. Olivia stands on the platform, exactly halfway between A and B.
  2. Seen from the platform: lightning strikes A and B at the same time, exactly at the two ends of the train (the rear and the front).
  3. Olivia: both flashes reach her together. So for her the two strikes are simultaneous (at the same time).
  4. Peter: the train carries him towards the front flash, so the front flash reaches him first.
  5. Peter thinks: I am in the middle of the train, and I am at rest. Both flashes travel at c over the same distance. The front flash arrives first, so for me, the front strike really happened first. His frame is just as valid as Olivia's (postulate 1).
Platform frame (Olivia)

The two strikes happen at the same time. Peter moves towards the front flash, so he sees the front flash first.

Train frame (Peter)

Peter does not move. Both flashes travel at c over the same distance. The front flash arrives first, so the front strike happened first.

This is the relativity of simultaneity: events at different places that are simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in a frame moving along the line between them. Both people are right.

4.2

Thought experiment 2: the light clock

Big ideaA moving clock runs slow. To someone watching it move, the light in the clock travels on a slanted path, which is longer. But the speed of light is still c, so each tick takes longer.

  • Light clock: two mirrors, with a beam of light bouncing up and down between them. Each time the light goes there and back, the clock counts one tick.
  • (a) The clock is at rest: in one round trip the light travels 2d, so one tick takes t0 = 2d/c.
  • (b) The clock moves to the right at speed v (you stand still and watch it): the light must travel on a slanted path to catch the mirror, which has already moved forward. The slanted path is longer.
  • But the speed of light is still c (postulate 2). The path is longer and the speed has not changed, so the time is longer: each tick takes longer. This is time dilation.

Working it out with Pythagoras (Figure 4.2 in the booklet)

  1. Look at the right-angled triangle in (b). The longest side: the distance the light travels in half a tick, ct/2. The vertical side: the distance between the two mirrors, d. The horizontal side: the distance the clock moves in half a tick, vt/2.
  2. Pythagoras: (ct/2)² = d² + (vt/2)².
  3. Rearrange: t = 2d/√(c² − v²) = (2d/c)/√(1 − v²/c²).
  4. Put in t0 = 2d/c: t = t0/√(1 − v²/c²).
  • The number under the root is smaller than 1, so t is always bigger than t0. When v = 0 the two are the same.
  • The factor 1/√(1 − v²/c²) has a name: the Lorentz factor, written γ. For example, at v = 0.6c: √(1 − 0.6²) = 0.8, γ = 1.25, so one tick of the moving clock is 1.25 times as long.

4.2

Proper time and proper length · Muons: both effects in action

Big ideaA moving object is shorter along its motion. Muons use both "clocks run slow" and "lengths get shorter": look from the other frame and the result is exactly the same.

Whose measurement is the proper one?

  • Proper time t0: time between two events, measured by a clock present at both events, in whatever frame: the muon's own clock for its life, the ship's clock for a trip. Others measure a longer t.
  • Proper length l0: length measured in the frame where the object is at rest.
  • Length contraction: a moving object is measured shorter. It is shorter only along the direction of motion, not across it.
  • For the distance from the Earth to a star: l0 is measured in the Earth–star frame (in this frame, neither the Earth nor the star moves).
  • Both effects work both ways: A sees B's clock run slow, and B sees A's clock run slow too. Your own clock and ruler always seem normal to you.
  • At everyday speeds the effects are very small (at v = 0.1c, t is still only about 0.5 % longer than t0). They become large only near c.

Muons: both effects together (Figure 4.3 in the booklet)

  • Cosmic rays (fast protons from space) make muons 10–15 km up in the air. A muon is like a heavy electron.
  • Muons are unstable: after a short time they decay into other particles. At rest, their mean lifetime is 2.2 μs.
  • Without relativity: using s = vt, even flying at nearly the speed of light, a muon travels only about 660 m. Yet many muons reach the ground.
(a) Earth frame (seen from the ground)

The muon's clock runs slow, so it lives long enough to cross the whole layer of air l0. This uses: the longer time t + proper length.

(b) muon frame (as the muon sees it)

The muon is at rest, and the ground rushes up. It lives only t0, but the layer of air is contracted to l, which is short enough. This uses: proper time + the shorter length.

  • Both frames agree: the muon reaches the ground. Never mix dilated (longer) time and contracted (shorter) length in one frame.

Evidence

  • Frisch and Smith (1963): counted muons with a speed of about 0.995c. On a mountain 1.9 km up, 563 per hour; at sea level, 408 per hour. Without time dilation, only about 30 would survive.
  • The counts give a dilation factor of about 9. At 0.995c the factor should be 10. It is a little less because the muons slow down in the air.
  • CERN (1977): muons were stored in a ring, going around at 0.999418c. They lived 29.3 times longer than at rest, as time dilation predicts.

4.3

Relativistic momentum and the speed limit

Big ideaNear c, momentum grows much faster than m0v and heads towards infinity. So nothing with mass can ever reach the speed of light.

  • Near c, p = mv fails: with it, momentum would be conserved in one inertial frame but not in another. This goes against postulate 1.
  • You must use the relativistic momentum pv. It uses the rest mass m0: the mass measured when the object is at rest.
  • As v → c ("gets closer to c"), the root → 0, so pv → ∞ (Figure 4.4 in the booklet).
  • A finite force for a finite time gives only a finite Δp = FnetΔt. Pushing momentum to infinity cannot be done. So v never reaches c.
  • In other words: you keep pushing with the same force. Momentum grows by the same amount each second, but the speed grows by less and less, and it always stays a little below c.
  • Below about 0.1c, pv ≈ m0v, and the two lines almost overlap.
  • A photon has no rest mass and always travels at c.

Evidence and applications

  • Bertozzi (1964): electrons were accelerated to energies of up to 15 MeV. They got closer and closer to c but never passed it.
  • LHC (Large Hadron Collider, CERN's biggest accelerator) gives protons 7000 times their rest energy (the energy an object has because it has mass, see 4.4). But they stay below c.
  • A magnet bends a moving charge into a circle: r = p/(qB) (from qvB = mv²/r). Not on the sheet: learn it.
  • Synchrotron (a ring-shaped accelerator): the radius r of the ring is fixed, so the magnetic field B must rise with pv, not m0v. It is a daily test of relativity.
  • The HSC formula keeps m0 fixed: it is the momentum that grows, not the mass.

4.4

Mass–energy equivalence: E = mc²

Big ideaMass is a very concentrated form of energy. When energy is released, a little mass is lost: energy released = mass lost × c².

  • Any mass m has a rest energy E = mc².
  • c² = 9.00 × 1016 m² s−2, which is huge. So a tiny mass = a huge energy. 1 g of mass is equivalent to about a day's output of a large power station.
  • Every process that releases energy loses mass. A sealed box gets lighter only as the energy escapes.
  • It also works in reverse: collision energy can make new particles.
  • Mass defect Δm = mass before − mass after: the mass that disappears.

Two routes: do not mix them

Mass in u

u is the atomic mass unit. Multiply by 931.5 to get MeV.

Mass in kg

Use E = mc² to get J. 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J.

Annihilation: all the mass becomes light (Figure 4.5 in the booklet)

  • Antiparticle: a particle with the same mass as another but the opposite charge.
  • Positron (e+): the antiparticle of the electron. It has the same mass and a positive charge.
  • Annihilation: a particle meets its antiparticle, and both become photons.
  • An electron meets a positron (both almost at rest): all the mass 2me becomes two equal gamma-ray photons in opposite directions.
  • Why two, in opposite directions? The total momentum before is 0, so the total momentum after must also be 0. Two photons with equal momentum in opposite directions add up to exactly 0.
  • Hospital PET scanners (a type of medical scan) detect these pairs of photons.

4.5

Key experiments

Michelson and Morley (1887): looking for the aether

Aim
To detect the Earth's motion through the aether.
Method
They used an interferometer (Figure 4.6 in the booklet). Light leaves a source, and a half-silvered mirror (half the light passes through, half is reflected) splits it into two beams at right angles. Each beam goes along an arm to a mirror and comes back. The two beams recombine to make interference fringes (bright and dark bands) in a telescope. The whole apparatus floats on mercury and is slowly rotated. Measurements are made at different times of day and year. A change in light's speed would shift the fringes. See Animation 1 (in 4.1).
Results
Predicted shift: about 0.4 of a fringe. Seen: less than 0.02 of a fringe (null result).
Conclusion
No aether wind: light has the same speed in all directions on the moving Earth.
Significance
No evidence for an aether. The result agrees with both postulates.

Hafele–Keating atomic clocks (1971)

Aim
To test time dilation with very accurate atomic clocks flown around the world.
Method
Four caesium atomic clocks flew around the world: first eastward, then westward. Then they were compared with ground clocks. Seen from the Earth's centre, the eastward clock moves fastest (the Earth itself also spins east), so it runs slowest.
Results
Eastward clocks lost 59 ns (predicted 40 ns); westward clocks gained 273 ns (predicted 275 ns). The predictions include a gravity effect (general relativity, not in the HSC course).
Conclusion
Measured and predicted times agree within the uncertainties: moving clocks run slow, even at aeroplane speeds.
Significance
Direct evidence for time dilation with real clocks at everyday speeds. GPS satellites need similar corrections: speed and gravity together, about 38 μs a day.

Evidence from cosmology: slow-motion supernovae

Aim
To test whether events in very distant galaxies are measured to run slow.
Method
Every Type Ia supernova (exploding star) fades in nearly the same way. Hundreds were timed as they faded, and the redshift z of each was measured: how much the light was stretched.
Results
Distant ones fade more slowly, by the factor 1 + z (at z = 1, twice as long).
Conclusion
Cosmological time dilation, mainly from the expansion of the Universe (general relativity).
Significance
Evidence for time dilation from cosmological studies: time is not absolute, even across the Universe.

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. Postulate 1: All inertial frames of reference are equivalent. Postulate 2: The speed of light in a vacuum is an absolute constant.
  2. Simultaneity is relative. Moving clocks run slow (t > t0); moving objects are shorter along the motion (l < l0). Muons show both.

  6. Evidence: Michelson–Morley, muons, atomic clocks, accelerators, supernovae.

Wrong: Light travels at c everywhere.
Right: It travels at c only in a vacuum. It slows down in glass and water.
Wrong: Thought experiments prove relativity.
Right: A thought experiment only shows what the theory predicts. It is not evidence. Real experiments (muons, atomic clocks ...) only support the theory. They do not prove it.
Wrong: At v = 0.9c, calculating √(1 − 0.9) = 0.32.
Right: Square v/c first: √(1 − 0.9²) = 0.44.
Wrong: Getting t < t0 or l > l0.
Right: Always t > t0 and l < l0. If you get the opposite, you have put the proper value in the wrong place.
Wrong: Mixing dilated (longer) time and contracted (shorter) length in one frame.
Right: Earth frame: the longer time t + l0. Muon frame: t0 + the shorter l. Use only one effect in each frame.
Wrong: A moving object gets shorter in every direction.
Right: It gets shorter only along the direction of motion, not across it.
Wrong: Mass grows near c; a photon also uses the pv formula.
Right: In the HSC formula m0 stays fixed, and it is the momentum that grows. A photon has no rest mass, so it cannot use this formula.
Wrong: Putting 0.6 straight into v on the top line of the pv formula.
Right: v is in m s−1: 0.6 × 3.00 × 108.
Wrong: Multiplying by c² when the mass is in u; or using the whole object's mass to find the energy released.
Right: With u, multiply by 931.5 (to get MeV). Use E = mc² only with kg (to get J). Use only the lost mass Δm, and square c.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. Write out Einstein's two postulates (exact wording).

1. All inertial frames of reference are equivalent. 2. The speed of light in a vacuum is an absolute constant. The first: the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame. The second: everyone measures the same speed of light in a vacuum, c.

2. Peter on the train says the front lightning struck first. Olivia on the platform says both struck at the same time. Who is right?

Both are right. Every inertial frame is just as valid (postulate 1). Two events at different places that are simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in a frame moving along the line between them: this is the relativity of simultaneity.

3. A spaceship flies past the Earth at 0.6c. A clock on the ship shows 8.0 s has passed. How long does a person on Earth measure? The ship measures its own length as 100 m. What length does the person on Earth measure?

10 s; 80 m. √(1 − 0.6²) = √0.64 = 0.8. t = 8.0 / 0.8 = 10 s (t > t0). l = 100 × 0.8 = 80 m (l < l0).

4. v = 0.9c. A student calculates √(1 − 0.9) = 0.32. What is wrong? How many times t0 is one tick of the moving clock?

0.9 must be squared first; about 2.3 times. √(1 − 0.9²) = √0.19 ≈ 0.436 (about 0.44). t = t0 / 0.436 ≈ 2.29 t0, about 2.3 times.

5. A muon at rest lives 2.2 μs on average. At 0.995c (the factor is 10), seen from the ground, how far can it fly on average?

About 6.6 km. Seen from the ground, the muon's clock runs 10 times slower, so it lives 2.2 × 10 = 22 μs on average. s = vt = 0.995 × 3.00 × 108 × 22 × 10−6 ≈ 6.6 × 103 m. Without relativity, only about 660 m.

6. In the muon's own frame, it lives only 2.2 μs. Why can it still reach the ground?

Because the layer of air is contracted. In the muon frame, the muon is at rest and the ground rushes up at 0.995c. The length of the layer of air is l = l0√(1 − v²/c²), only about 1/10 of the original. Here you use proper time + the shorter length. You cannot also use the longer time.

7. A constant force keeps pushing a particle. Why can it never reach c?

Reaching c would need infinite momentum. As v → c, pv → ∞. A finite force for a finite time gives only a finite Δp = FnetΔt. So v can only get closer and closer to c.

8. The Sun loses 4 million tonnes (4 × 109 kg) of mass each second. What is its power?

About 3.6 × 1026 W. P = (Δm/Δt) × c² = 4 × 109 × 9.00 × 1016 = 3.6 × 1026 W.
