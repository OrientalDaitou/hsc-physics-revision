Properties of the Nucleus

Explained from zero

This chapter is about the nucleus: why it decays, how fast it decays, and why fission and fusion can release so much energy. It looks like a lot, but it really uses only three rules. Understand these three first. Everything after that just uses them.

Strong nuclear force vs repulsion

In a nuclear reaction, the total number of nucleons does not change, and the total charge does not change. So on the two sides of the equation, the top numbers (A) add up to the same total, and the bottom numbers (Z) add up to the same total.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. The structure of the atom: in the middle is a very small nucleus. Inside it are the proton (charge +e) and the neutron (no charge). Outside it is the electron (charge −e). The size of a nucleus is measured in fm: 1 fm = 10−15 m.
  2. The force between charges: like charges push each other apart, and the closer they are, the harder they push (Coulomb's law, M8 T2). A charged particle in a magnetic field feels a force F = qvB sin θ. Find its direction with the right-hand palm rule. Positive and negative charges bend in opposite directions. The smaller the momentum, the more it bends (r = mv/(qB)).
  3. E = mc² (M8 T1): mass and energy can change into each other. In nuclear physics, energy is often given in MeV (million electron volts). The data sheet has: 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c². u is the atomic mass unit.
  4. e and ln on the calculator: this e is a number, about 2.718 (not the charge of the electron). ex and ln undo each other: ln(ex) = x. This chapter uses ln, not log.
4.1

Why do nuclei decay?

Big ideaSome nuclei have the wrong mix of protons and neutrons, or are too big. They change by themselves into more stable nuclei and give out α, β or γ radiation.

  • Nucleon: a proton or a neutron (a particle in the nucleus).
  • Notation AZX: A = mass number = protons + neutrons. Z = atomic number = number of protons. Number of neutrons: N = A − Z.
  • Example: 23592U has 92 protons and 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different N. For example, hydrogen-1, hydrogen-2 and hydrogen-3 are all hydrogen (Z = 1), with 0, 1 and 2 neutrons.

A tug of war in the nucleus

  • Protons are all positive and push each other apart. When two protons are 1 fm (= 10−15 m) apart, the repulsion is about 230 N.
  • Strong nuclear force: a very strong, very short-range attraction between nucleons. Its pull on each nucleon is stronger than the repulsion. But it only reaches about 3 fm.
  • So a big nucleus has a problem. Each proton is held only by the few nucleons next to it, but it is pushed by all the protons in the nucleus. The bigger the nucleus, the faster the repulsion grows compared with the attraction.
  • Neutrons have no charge. Extra neutrons add attraction but no repulsion.
  • Analogy: a group of people hold hands in a circle. Each person can only hold the people next to them (the strong nuclear force is very short-range). Some people (protons) dislike each other and want to push each other away from any distance (the repulsion reaches far). The more people there are, the more people dislike each other, and the more easily the circle breaks up. Add a few people who only hold hands and do not push (neutrons), and the circle is a little more stable.

The band of stability

  • Plot the stable nuclei on a graph: the vertical axis is N, the horizontal axis is Z. They crowd into a thin strip called the band of stability.
  • Light nuclei: N ≈ Z. Heavy nuclei: they need some extra neutrons to help hold them together.
  • No nucleus with Z > 82 is stable.

Decay happens by itself

  • Radioactive decay is spontaneous: nothing has to touch the nucleus. It changes by itself.
  • The nucleus before the decay is called the parent nucleus. The nucleus after the decay is called the daughter nucleus. The daughter is closer to the band of stability than the parent.
  • α and β decay change Z, so the element changes. This is called transmutation (one element turns into another).
  • The radiation given out is all ionising radiation: it knocks electrons out of atoms, which turns the atoms into ions.

4.1

Alpha, beta and gamma decay and their radiation

Big ideaα is a helium-4 nucleus, β is an electron or a positron, and γ is a photon. Their charges and masses are different, so how far they penetrate and how they bend in a magnetic field are also different.

  • The β particle is not already in the nucleus. It is created at the moment of the decay. β−: a neutron turns into a proton, and an electron is created at the same time (looking closer: a down quark becomes an up quark. The quark is covered in T5).
  • Positron: the electron's antiparticle, charge +e.
  • Neutrino: neutral, with almost no mass. β− gives out an antineutrino νe, and β+ gives out a neutrino νe.
  • Gamma ray: a very high-energy photon. It comes from an excited nucleus (symbol *).

4.2

Half-life and activity

Big ideaThe moment when one nucleus decays is completely random. But with a huge number of nuclei together, half are left after each equal period of time. This period is called the half-life.

  • Half-life t½: the time for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.
  • Decay constant λ: the chance per second that one nucleus decays. Unit: s−1.
  • Activity A: the number of decays each second. Unit: becquerel (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay each second.

One at a time it is random; a huge number follow a clear pattern

  • Decay is random: you cannot predict when one nucleus will decay.
  • Each nucleus has the same chance each second. How "old" the nucleus is, the temperature, what it is chemically joined to and the size of the sample do not change this chance.
  • Analogy: 600 people all throw a die. You cannot say who will throw a 6. But each time, about 100 people throw a 6.
  • So the amount of the parent halves every half-life (Figure 4.2 in the booklet, which is the N–t graph in Animation 3): N0 → N0/2 → N0/4 → N0/8 … What halves is what is left, so after two half-lives N0/4 is left, not 0.
  • A Geiger counter measures a count rate (the number of counts each second or each minute). After you subtract the background radiation (radiation always around us), the count rate is proportional to the activity.

4.3

Mass defect, binding energy and energy released

Big ideaA nucleus has less mass than its separate protons and neutrons. In any nuclear reaction where the total mass goes down, the lost mass appears as energy (E = mc²).

  • Mass defect Δm: the mass "missing" when nucleons join to make a nucleus.
  • Binding energy EB: the energy needed to pull a nucleus apart into separate nucleons.
  • When free nucleons join, energy is given out, so mass is lost. A nucleus is lighter than its parts. Example, helium-4: Δm = 0.030 377 u, or 28.3 MeV.
  • Binding energy is the energy that must be supplied to split a nucleus. It is not energy "stored" in the nucleus. Analogy: two magnets give out energy with a "snap" when they come together. To pull them apart, you must do work.

Energy released in decays and artificial transmutations

  • Three things are conserved: mass-energy, A and charge.
  • Less mass after the reaction: the lost mass becomes kinetic energy and γ rays. More mass after the reaction: energy must be supplied from outside.
Alpha decay

Momentum is 0 before and after the decay, so the daughter moves backwards. This is called recoil, like a gun kicking back when it fires. The α is light and gets most of the energy (about 98 %). So α energies are fixed.

Beta decay

Each decay releases the same energy, but β energies are spread: some are bigger, some are smaller. Pauli (1930) said: a neutrino carries off the rest of the energy. The neutrino was first detected in 1956.

  • Artificial transmutation: a change forced by firing a particle at a nucleus, which turns it into a different nucleus.
  • Rutherford (1919, the first): 147N + 42He → 178O + 11H.
  • Cockcroft and Walton (1932): 73Li + 11H → 2 42He, about 17 MeV released, as the mass defect predicted. This was the first direct test of E = mc².
4.3

Binding energy per nucleon

Big ideaThe bigger the binding energy per nucleon, the more tightly bound the nucleons are in the nucleus. When a nucleus moves up the curve (towards iron-56), mass goes down and energy is released.

  • Binding energy per nucleon = EB/A: the average binding energy of each nucleon. Bigger means more tightly bound.
  • Figure 4.3 in the booklet (the curve in Animation 4) shows binding energy per nucleon against mass number A. Some points to remember:

  • Why up = less mass: each nucleon is more tightly bound, so more energy is given out when they join, and more mass is lost.
  • There are two ways to move up: light nuclei join (fusion), going up from the left; a heavy nucleus splits (fission), going up from the right. Section 4.4 covers both.

4.4

Fission and chain reactions

Big ideaSplitting a heavy nucleus (fission) and joining light nuclei (fusion) both make nucleons more tightly bound. The products have less mass, so energy is released. This section covers fission first.

  • Chain reaction: neutrons from one fission cause more fissions.
  • Critical mass: the smallest mass of fuel that can keep a chain reaction going.

How U-235 splits (Hahn and Strassmann, 1938)

  1. It absorbs a slow thermal neutron. A neutron has no charge, so it is not repelled by the nucleus. U-235 becomes an excited U-236 and stretches into a peanut shape.
  2. The short-range strong nuclear force cannot hold the far ends. Repulsion pushes them apart: two fission fragments (their kinetic energy finally becomes heat), and 2 or 3 fast neutrons.
23592U + 10n → 23692U* → 14054Xe + 9438Sr + 2 10n

This is one possible pair of fragments. Check the balance: A: 235 + 1 = 140 + 94 + 2 = 236; Z: 92 = 54 + 38.

  • Energy: U-235 has 7.6 MeV per nucleon; the fragments have 8.3 to 8.6 MeV. More tightly bound → less mass → about 200 MeV released per fission (this number is not on the data sheet; the question gives it when it is needed).
  • The fragments are radioactive. They are the nuclear waste.

Chain reactions

The 2 or 3 new neutrons can cause more fissions. What matters is how many neutrons from each fission cause a new fission, on average (Figure 4.4 in the booklet, Animation 5).

  • More than one: fissions multiply (1, 3, 9, 27, …). This is an uncontrolled chain reaction, which is a nuclear explosion.
  • Exactly one: a steady rate. This is a controlled chain reaction, which runs a reactor. Fermi's team first did this in 1942.
  • Less than one: the chain reaction dies out.
  • Analogy: passing on a message. If each person tells 3 people, soon the whole city knows. If each person tells only 1 person, there is always just one person talking. If it is less than 1, the message soon stops.
  • A small lump of fuel loses many neutrons through its surface. Below the critical mass (subcritical): the chain reaction dies out. Above the critical mass (supercritical): it grows faster and faster.
  • Natural uranium is only 0.7 % U-235. Reactor fuel is enriched to 3 to 5 % U-235; bombs need about 90 %.
Reactor

The moderator (water or graphite) makes neutrons slower, and slow neutrons split U-235 more easily. Control rods (boron or cadmium) absorb neutrons, so each fission causes exactly one new fission. The coolant carries the heat away to make steam.

Bomb

Highly enriched fuel is pushed together suddenly and becomes supercritical. Nothing absorbs the extra neutrons, so all the energy comes out in less than a second.

4.4

Nuclear fusion

Big ideaWhen light nuclei join, the nucleons become more tightly bound, the mass goes down and energy is released. The hard part is that both nuclei are positive, so they must be very hot before they can collide.

  • Why energy is released: helium-4 (7.1 MeV per nucleon) is far more tightly bound than hydrogen-2 (1.1 MeV), so the products have less mass.
  • Why it is hard: both nuclei are positive and repel. They must get within about 3 fm for the strong nuclear force to act. So the gas must be very hot and very dense: the hotter it is, the faster the nuclei move, and the more easily they get through the repulsion. The Sun's core is about 1.5 × 107 K; reactors on Earth need over 108 K.
  • The Sun: 4 11H → 42He + 2 0+1e + 2 νe, 26.7 MeV released in total (including positron annihilation).
  • Future reactors: 21H + 31H → 42He + 10n (17.6 MeV). The fuel is deuterium (hydrogen-2: 1 proton, 1 neutron) and tritium (hydrogen-3: 1 proton, 2 neutrons).
  • Fusing nuclei heavier than iron would take in energy (moving down the curve).

4.5

Key experiments

Dice model of half-life

Method
Throw 200 dice and remove the dice that show 6. Throw the rest again, and keep repeating.
Results
The number left falls like Figure 4.2 in the booklet: half are left after about 3.8 throws, whatever the starting number.
Why
Each die has a 1/6 chance of a 6 on each throw, just as each nucleus has the same chance (λ) of decaying each second. After each throw, 5/6 are left: (5/6)n = ½ → n = ln 2 / ln(6/5) ≈ 3.8. The "Dice" mode of Animation 3 is this experiment.

Penetrating power of α, β and γ

Aim
To compare how well α, β and γ pass through materials (penetrating power).
Method
First measure the background count (the counts with no source). Put each sealed source (Am-241: α; Sr-90: β; Co-60: γ) a fixed distance from a Geiger–Müller tube. Take counts with no absorber, then with paper, 5 mm of aluminium and 10 mm of lead.
Results
Paper stops α; 5 mm of aluminium stops β; 10 mm of lead only halves the γ count.
Conclusion
Penetrating power γ > β > α; ionising power is the reverse.
Significance
This guides shielding and uses: α in smoke alarms, γ in cancer treatment. The "Penetration" half of Animation 2 is this experiment.

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. The short-range strong nuclear force works against the repulsion between protons: big nuclei, or the wrong mix of N and Z, are unstable.
  2. α: A − 4, Z − 2. β−: n → p. β+: p → n. γ: energy only.
  3. Ionising power α > β > γ; penetrating power is the reverse.
  4. Nt = N0e−λt, λ = ln 2/t½, A = λN (for Bq, λ in s−1).
  5. E = Δm c², or Δm (u) × 931.5 MeV. Fission and fusion both move up the curve, towards iron-56: less mass, energy released.
  6. α energies are fixed; β energies are spread (because of the neutrino). Reactor: exactly one neutron per fission causes a new fission; the moderator slows neutrons, control rods absorb neutrons.

Common mistakes

Wrong: The β particle is an electron that was already in the nucleus and escapes during the decay.
Right: The β particle is created at the moment of the decay: one n turns into a p, and an electron is created at the same time.
Wrong: α is the most ionising, so it is also the most penetrating.
Right: It is the reverse. α is the most ionising and uses up its energy fastest, so one sheet of paper stops it. Penetration: γ > β > α.
Wrong: Each half-life takes away half of the original amount, so after two half-lives nothing is left.
Right: What halves is what is left: N0 → N0/2 → N0/4.
Wrong: Heating it, or making it into a different compound, can make it decay a little faster.
Right: Temperature, chemistry, age and sample size do not change λ or the half-life.
Wrong: The half-life is given in hours, so put λ in h−1 straight into A = λN and say the unit is Bq.
Right: For Bq, λ must be in s−1. λ and t must always be in the same time unit.
Wrong: Using log on the calculator; taking λ to be a wavelength.
Right: Use ln. This λ is the decay constant.
Wrong: In one question, using u × 931.5 at one point and kg × c² at another, and rounding along the way.
Right: One route only: u × 931.5 gives MeV; kg × c² gives J. Keep every decimal place.
Wrong: Using the atomic mass from the table directly when finding a mass defect with mp and mn.
Right: Atomic masses include electrons, so first subtract Z × 0.000 549 u. In a reaction the electrons cancel, so do not subtract them; in β+ decay also subtract 2me.
Wrong: Binding energy is energy stored in the nucleus that can be released.
Right: Binding energy is the energy that must be supplied to split a nucleus. The bigger the binding energy per nucleon, the more tightly bound the nucleus is.
Wrong: Control rods slow neutrons down.
Right: The moderator slows neutrons down; control rods absorb neutrons.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. A nucleus is above the band of stability (too many neutrons). Which decay is it most likely to go through? How do A and Z change?

β− decay: A same, Z + 1. A neutron turns into a proton: one neutron fewer, one proton more, so the nucleus moves closer to the band of stability.

2. 23592U goes through one α decay. What are the A and Z of the daughter nucleus? How many neutrons does it have?

A = 231, Z = 90, with 141 neutrons. α takes away 42He. Top: 235 − 4 = 231. Bottom: 92 − 2 = 90. Neutrons: N = A − Z = 231 − 90 = 141. Check: 231 + 4 = 235, 90 + 2 = 92, the two sides are equal.

3. In the experiment, what did paper, 5 mm of aluminium and 10 mm of lead each stop? Why is α the easiest to stop?

Paper stops α; 5 mm of aluminium stops β; 10 mm of lead only halves the γ count. α is heavy and slow, with charge +2e. It ionises many atoms along its path and uses up its energy fast. The stronger the ionising, the weaker the penetration.

4. A sample has 1000 undecayed nuclei, and the half-life is 10 s. How many are left after 30 s? What is the activity at that time?

125; about 8.66 Bq. n = 30/10 = 3, N = 1000 × (½)³ = 125. λ = ln 2/10 = 0.0693 s−1. A = λN = 0.0693 × 125 ≈ 8.66 Bq. Using e−λt gives the same: 1000 × e−0.0693 × 30 = 125.

5. For the same sample (half-life 10 s), how long does it take to fall from 1000 to 100?

About 33.2 s. t = (1/λ) ln(N0/Nt) = (1/0.0693) × ln(1000/100) = 14.43 × 2.303 ≈ 33.2 s. Check: after 30 s (3 half-lives) 125 are left, after 40 s 62.5 are left, and 100 is in between, so it is right.

6. The mass defect of helium-4 is 0.030 377 u. What is its binding energy in MeV? What is it per nucleon?

About 28.3 MeV; about 7.1 MeV per nucleon. EB = 0.030 377 × 931.5 ≈ 28.3 MeV. He-4 has 4 nucleons: 28.3 / 4 ≈ 7.1 MeV, which is the He-4 value on the curve.

7. Fission is splitting and fusion is joining. Why do both release energy?

Both move up the binding energy per nucleon curve (towards iron-56). Up = nucleons more tightly bound = less mass, and the lost mass becomes energy. Fusing nuclei heavier than iron moves down the curve and takes in energy.

8. What do the moderator and the control rods each do in a reactor?

The moderator slows neutrons down; control rods absorb neutrons. Slow neutrons split U-235 more easily. Control rods absorb the extra neutrons, so each fission causes exactly one new fission. This is a controlled chain reaction.
