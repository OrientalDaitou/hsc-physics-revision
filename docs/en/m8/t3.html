The Quantum Atom

Explained from zero

This chapter is about the electrons in an atom: Bohr's energy levels, hydrogen's spectrum, de Broglie's matter waves and Schrödinger's model. There are many names, but it all rests on just two rules: an electron can have only certain energies; an electron is also a wave. Once you understand these two, everything after them is just using them.

Rule ①
Energy is quantised

An electron in an atom can have only a few particular energies, called energy levels. In one jump it emits or absorbs just one photon. The photon's energy equals the difference between the two levels, called the gap.

Rule ②

Light can act like particles, and particles can act like waves. This is called wave–particle duality. When the electron's wave fits around an orbit to make a standing wave, that orbit is an allowed orbit.

3.1

From Rutherford's atom to Bohr's energy levels

Big ideaClassical physics says Rutherford's atom should collapse. Bohr added some quantum rules: the electron has only certain energies. It gives out or takes in light only when it "jumps".

  • Classical physics: physics before quantum ideas, that is, Newton's laws and Maxwell's electromagnetism.
  • Quantised: only certain values are allowed. It is like stairs: you can stand on a step, but not between two steps.
  • Stationary state: an allowed orbit. On it the electron does not radiate, which means it does not give out EM waves.

Why Rutherford's atom fails

Nuclear model (1911): a very small, dense, positive nucleus in the middle, with electrons moving around outside it. The nucleus explained the gold-foil experiment and is still accepted today. But the model has three problems:

  • It should collapse. The orbiting electron accelerates → by Maxwell, it gives out EM waves → it loses energy → it spirals into the nucleus in about 10−11 s. But real atoms are stable: they last for billions of years.
  • Wrong spectrum. As it falls in, it turns faster and faster, so its light would have every frequency: a continuous spectrum. But real hydrogen gives a line spectrum: only a few sharp wavelengths. It also absorbs exactly these wavelengths (Figure 1.1).
  • It does not say what the nucleus is made of, or how the electrons are arranged.

To see how it collapses: Animation 4 (in 3.4), Rutherford mode.

Bohr's four postulates (1913)

Bohr kept the nucleus and added some postulates (rules assumed without proof). Books number them differently, so learn them by what they say, not by their numbers.

  1. Electrons move in circular orbits, held by the electrostatic (Coulomb) attraction to the nucleus.
  2. Only certain orbits are allowed. In these stationary states the electron does not radiate, so its energy stays constant.
  3. A jump between two states emits or absorbs one photon. The photon's energy = the gap between the two levels: hf = Ei − Ef (emission; for absorption it is Ef − Ei). i = initial (start), f = final (end). This is conservation of energy: the energy the atom loses is the energy of the photon.
  4. Angular momentum (mvr, the amount of turning motion) is quantised: mvr = nh/(2π), n = 1, 2, 3, … (not on the sheet). 3.3 explains why.

3.1

Energy levels, ionisation and photon energy

Big ideaThe electron can stand on only a few "steps", called energy levels. Only the difference between two levels (the gap) can become one photon, or be filled by one photon. Only ionisation does not need to be exact: the photon energy just has to be ≥ the ionisation energy.

nEnergyName
n = ∞0 eVthe electron is just free and leaves the atom
n = 6−0.38 eVexcited state
n = 5−0.54 eVexcited state
n = 4−0.85 eVexcited state
n = 3−1.51 eVexcited state
n = 2−3.40 eVexcited state
n = 1−13.6 eVground state

Energy levels of hydrogen (the booklet, Figure 3.1). The booklet draws them as a ladder, with the spacing not to scale.

  • The allowed energies are called energy levels, named by n, like the rungs of a ladder. n = 1 is the ground state; higher levels are excited states. A jump between two levels is a transition.
  • Energies are negative. 0 eV = a just-free electron (n = ∞). An electron held by the atom has less energy than 0. It is like a ball in a well: the top of the well is 0, the further down, the more negative, and you must give the ball energy for it to climb out.
  • Hydrogen: En = −13.6 eV / n² (not on the sheet).
  • Only certain gaps exist, so only certain photon energies come out: this is the line spectrum.
  • Absorption: the photon's energy must exactly equal a gap. Even a small difference will not work. The atom does not "absorb a part".
  • Ionisation: the electron reaches n = ∞ and leaves the atom. The ionisation energy from n = 1 is 13.6 eV. Any extra energy becomes the electron's kinetic energy.

3.2

Hydrogen's line spectrum and the Rydberg equation

Big ideaEach line in hydrogen's spectrum is one kind of jump between two energy levels. The Rydberg equation gives the wavelength of every line.

SeriesJumps toWhere
Lyman seriesn = 1UV, 91 nm to 122 nm
Balmer seriesn = 2four visible lines, then UV
Paschen seriesn = 3IR, 820 nm to 1875 nm
  • Each element's lines are like a fingerprint: no other element has the same lines. Astronomers use them to find hydrogen in stars.
  • Balmer and Rydberg first found formulas for the lines. Bohr explained why hydrogen has these lines.
  • A bigger drop (bigger gap) → more photon energy → shorter λ. So the Lyman lines, which fall back to n = 1, are all in the UV, and the Paschen lines, which fall back to n = 3, are all in the IR.

The four visible Balmer lines

Then come UV lines, more and more crowded, down to the series limit (a jump from n = ∞), 365 nm. These four lines are also in Figure 1.1 in M8 T1. There they are written to 0.1 nm (656.3, 486.2, 434.1, 410.2 nm). The only difference is the rounding.

How the lines link to the levels

  1. Put En = −13.6 eV/n² into hc/λ = Ei − Ef.
  2. Rearrange: hc/λ = 13.6 eV × (1/nf² − 1/ni²). Divide both sides by hc, and this is the Rydberg equation.
  3. Here R = 13.6 eV / (hc). Work it out: 13.6 × 1.602 × 10−19 / (6.626 × 10−34 × 3.00 × 108) ≈ 1.096 × 107 m−1 (calculated), almost the same as R = 1.097 × 107 m−1 (13.6 is given to only 3 figures).

When to use: hydrogen lines only. Emission: ni > nf. In absorption the electron jumps up, but when you calculate the wavelength the smaller number still goes first in the bracket: 1/λ = R(1/nlow² − 1/nhigh²). For example, 2 → 6 absorption uses 1/2² − 1/6²; otherwise you get a negative number. Series limit: ni = ∞ (1/∞² = 0).
Example: 4 → 2: 1/λ = 1.097 × 107 × (1/2² − 1/4²) = 1.097 × 107 × 0.1875 = 2.057 × 106 m−1, λ = 1/(2.057 × 106) = 4.86 × 10−7 m = 486 nm.
Trap: square each n first. The equation gives 1/λ: take the reciprocal at the end to get λ. R is not resistance.

3.3

De Broglie's matter waves

Big ideaLight waves can act like particles. De Broglie (1924) proposed the reverse: particles can act like waves. Electron diffraction confirmed it.

  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both show wave and particle behaviour.
  • Photons carry momentum (p, the amount of motion): p = h/λ (not on the sheet).
  • De Broglie's hypothesis (an idea to be tested): particles follow the same rule, with p = mv. So λ = h/mv. This wave is called a matter wave.
  • Diffraction: a wave spreads out after it passes through a gap. It is clear only when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. Only waves diffract. Sound can go around a doorway into a corridor: that is diffraction.
  • A ball's mv is large, so its λ is far too small to notice. An electron at lab speeds has λ ≈ 10−10 m, about the same as the spacing of atoms in a crystal. So crystals diffract electrons.
  • Evidence (1927): Davisson and Germer, and G. P. Thomson (see 3.5). The evidence is that λ from the pattern matched h/mv.

3.3

Standing waves explain Bohr's orbits

Big ideaTreat the electron as a wave: an orbit is allowed only if one lap fits a whole number of wavelengths exactly. Then the wave joins itself and becomes a standing wave, which carries no energy away.

  • An allowed orbit fits a whole number of wavelengths in one lap: 2πr = nλ (not on the sheet). The wave goes round once and joins itself exactly: a standing wave.
  • A standing wave has some points that never move, called nodes. The wave only moves up and down in place. It does not travel forward.
  • A standing wave does not travel, so it carries no energy away: this is "no radiation". In other orbits the wave does not line up from lap to lap, and it cancels itself.
  • The booklet, Figure 3.2 (not to scale): n = 3, 4, 5: a whole number of waves fits exactly, so they are allowed. 3.4 waves: two laps drawn, with lap 2 out of step with lap 1, so it is not allowed.

Getting Bohr's rule from standing waves

  1. An allowed orbit: 2πr = nλ.
  2. Put in λ = h/(mv): 2πr = nh/(mv).
  3. Multiply both sides by mv, then divide by 2π: mvr = nh/(2π). Bohr's angular momentum postulate is explained. Before, it was only an assumption.

3.4

Bohr's limits and Schrödinger's quantum model

Big ideaBohr's model works only for atoms with one electron (like hydrogen). Schrödinger (1926) treated the electron fully as a wave. His model gives probabilities, not orbits. This is the basis of today's model of the atom.

What Bohr got right: stable atoms (by assumption); hydrogen's line spectrum can be calculated (the Rydberg equation).

Five limitations of Bohr's model

  1. It works only for hydrogen and one-electron ions (such as He+).
  2. It cannot explain why some lines are brighter than others (their intensity).
  3. It cannot explain fine structure: one line is really several very close lines. It also cannot explain the Zeeman effect (lines split in a magnetic field) or the Stark effect (lines split in an electric field).
  4. Its rules are assumed, not explained. It mixes classical orbits with quantum rules.
  5. It gives the electron an exact path. But the uncertainty principle (explained below) forbids this.

What Schrödinger added

  • A wave equation for the electron in 3-D, built on de Broglie's matter waves. Its solutions are wave functions, written ψ.
  • Max Born (1926): ψ² gives the probability of finding the electron at each place.
  • Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927): exact position and exact momentum cannot both be known. So there are no exact orbits.
  • An orbital (1s, 2p, …) is a 3-D "cloud" showing where one electron is likely to be. It is not a path. In 1s the most likely distance is the Bohr radius, 5.29 × 10−11 m, often written a0.
  • Energy levels and quantum numbers come out of the maths by themselves. They are not assumed.
  • It predicts how bright the lines are, and the simplest Zeeman splitting. Add electron spin (built-in turning of the electron) and the Pauli exclusion principle (no two electrons in an atom are in the same state): then it explains the periodic table. Add relativity (Dirac, 1928): then it explains fine structure.
orbit · Bohr

An exact line. The electron moves along it, like a train on a track.

orbital · Schrödinger

A cloud of probability, with no path. Where the cloud is denser (ψ² is larger), the electron is more likely to be found.

3.5

Key experiments

Investigating the Balmer series of hydrogen (student experiment)

Aim
To measure hydrogen's visible lines and compare them with the Rydberg equation.
Method
A hydrogen discharge tube (glowing gas) shines into a grating spectrometer. Inside it is a diffraction grating (slit spacing d), with a scale to measure angles. Measure each line's first-order angle θ, and get λ from λ = d sin θ. Then plot 1/λ against (1/2² − 1/ni²).
Results
Four lines (red, cyan, blue-violet, violet), each within 1% of the Rydberg value. The graph is a straight line through the origin, gradient 1.10 × 107 m−1 ≈ R.
Why
With nf = 2, the Rydberg equation is just 1/λ = R × (1/2² − 1/ni²), which is like y = R × x. So the graph goes through the origin and its gradient is R.
Conclusion
Each line is a jump down to n = 2 (from n = 3 to 6): energy levels are quantised.
Significance
These are the lines Bohr's model was built to explain. Astronomers use them to find hydrogen in stars.

Electron diffraction (1927): Davisson–Germer and G. P. Thomson

Aim
To test de Broglie's matter waves.
Method
Davisson and Germer fired electrons, accelerated through 54 V, at a nickel crystal in a vacuum. A detector counted them at each angle. G. P. Thomson fired fast electrons through a thin metal film.
Results
Davisson–Germer: a peak at 50° that moved when the voltage changed. λ = 1.65 × 10−10 m from the pattern, h/mv = 1.67 × 10−10 m. Thomson: rings appeared on the screen; the higher the voltage, the smaller the rings.
Why
The higher the voltage, the faster the electrons, the shorter λ = h/mv and the less they spread out. So the rings get smaller and the peak moves.
Conclusion
Electrons diffract, so they act as waves. λ matched h/mv.
Significance
This was the first direct evidence of matter waves. Electron microscopes use the same idea.

Common mistakes

Wrong: a photon's energy = the energy of one level, such as −3.40 eV.
Right: a photon's energy = the difference between two levels (the gap), such as E3 − E2 = 1.89 eV.
Wrong: putting eV and nm straight into a formula.
Right: eV × 1.602 × 10−19 gives J; nm × 10−9 gives m.
Wrong: En = 13.6 eV/n.
Right: En = −13.6 eV/n²: there is a minus sign, and n must be squared.
Wrong: if a photon's energy is a bit bigger than a gap, the atom absorbs a part of it.
Right: for absorption, the photon's energy must exactly equal a gap. Only in ionisation does the extra energy become the electron's kinetic energy.
Wrong: the Rydberg equation gives λ directly.
Right: it gives 1/λ, so take the reciprocal at the end. Square each n first. R is not resistance.
Wrong: using E = hc/λ for an electron's wavelength, and λ = h/mv for a photon.
Right: hc/λ is only for light; particles with mass use λ = h/mv.
Wrong: the faster the electron, the longer its de Broglie wavelength.
Right: the faster it is, the shorter λ is and the less it diffracts. This λ is also not the decay constant.
Wrong: an allowed orbit is r = nλ.
Right: 2πr = nλ: 2πr is the length of one full lap.
Wrong: an orbital is a path the electron moves along.
Right: an orbital is a cloud of probability. An orbit with an exact path is Bohr's idea, and the uncertainty principle forbids it.

1. By classical physics, why should Rutherford's atom collapse?

The orbiting electron accelerates, so it keeps giving out EM waves and losing energy. By Maxwell, an accelerating charge radiates. The electron has less and less energy, and in about 10−11 s it spirals into the nucleus. But real atoms last for billions of years.

2. An electron in a hydrogen atom falls from n = 3 to n = 2. What is the energy of the emitted photon in eV? In J? What is its frequency?

1.89 eV; 3.03 × 10−19 J; 4.57 × 1014 Hz. Gap = −1.51 − (−3.40) = 1.89 eV. In J: 1.89 × 1.602 × 10−19 = 3.03 × 10−19 J. f = E/h = 3.03 × 10−19 / (6.626 × 10−34) = 4.57 × 1014 Hz.

3. A hydrogen atom in the ground state meets an 11.00 eV photon. Does it absorb it? What about a 15.00 eV photon?

11.00 eV: not absorbed, it passes straight through. 15.00 eV: the electron is knocked out (ionisation) and takes away 1.40 eV of kinetic energy. The gaps from n = 1 are only 10.20, 12.09, 12.75 … eV. 11.00 eV does not equal any of them, and the atom does not absorb a part. 15.00 eV ≥ 13.6 eV, which is enough to ionise; the extra 15.00 − 13.6 = 1.40 eV becomes the electron's kinetic energy.

4. Use the Rydberg equation to find the wavelength of the second Balmer line (4 → 2).

486 nm. 1/λ = 1.097 × 107 × (1/2² − 1/4²) = 1.097 × 107 × (0.25 − 0.0625) = 1.097 × 107 × 0.1875 = 2.057 × 106 m−1. Take the reciprocal: λ = 4.86 × 10−7 m = 486 nm (cyan).

5. Why are the Lyman series lines all in the UV, and the Paschen series lines all in the IR?

Falling back to n = 1 means the biggest gaps, and falling back to n = 3 means small gaps. Lyman gaps are at least 10.20 eV, so the photon energy is large and λ is short (91–122 nm): UV. Paschen gaps are small, so the photon energy is small and λ is long (820–1875 nm): IR.

6. In the Davisson–Germer experiment, electrons accelerated through 54 V have a speed of about 4.36 × 106 m s−1. What is their de Broglie wavelength?

1.67 × 10−10 m. λ = h/mv = 6.626 × 10−34 / (9.109 × 10−31 × 4.36 × 106) = 1.67 × 10−10 m. This is exactly the h/mv in the booklet, and it matches the 1.65 × 10−10 m measured from the pattern. (The speed is a calculated value, from v = √(2qV/m) in Chapter 2.)

7. Why must an allowed orbit fit a whole number of wavelengths in one lap?

Only a whole number of waves can join up and become a standing wave. A standing wave does not travel and carries no energy away, so the electron does not radiate. If it is not a whole number, the wave does not line up from lap to lap and cancels itself, so this orbit does not exist. From 2πr = nλ and λ = h/mv you can also get Bohr's mvr = nh/(2π).

8. What is the difference between Bohr's orbit and Schrödinger's orbital?

An orbit is an exact path; an orbital is a cloud of probability. Schrödinger treated the electron as a wave ψ, and ψ² gives the probability of finding the electron at each place. The uncertainty principle says exact position and exact momentum cannot both be known, so an exact path is not possible.
