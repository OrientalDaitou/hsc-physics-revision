Deep Inside the Atom

Explained from zero

This chapter looks further inside the nucleus: protons and neutrons are also made of smaller particles. The theory of all the fundamental particles and forces is called the Standard Model. Scientists test it with particle accelerators. There are many new names, but underneath there are only three rules. Understand these three first and the rest is simple.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Charge is measured in units of e: e = 1.602 × 10−19 C. A proton is +e, an electron is −e, and a neutron has no charge. The total charge is the same before and after a reaction (conservation of charge). You also need to add and subtract fractions: +⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1.
  2. Rutherford's gold foil experiment (T2): a few alpha particles bounced at large angles, which showed that the positive charge is concentrated in a very small nucleus. SLAC in 5.1 is the same idea, but with electrons fired at a proton.
  3. The two fields of Module 6: a charge moving through a voltage V gains energy qV. The magnetic force is F = qvB sin θ, and its direction comes from the right-hand palm rule. It is always perpendicular to v, so it only turns the particle. This gives circular motion: Fc = mv²/r.
  4. Two formulas from earlier: λ = h/(mv) (T3: the faster the particle, the shorter the wavelength); E = mc² (T1: energy can become mass).
5.1

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental

Big ideaA fundamental particle has no smaller parts. The electron is fundamental, but protons and neutrons are not: they are made of quarks.

There are three pieces of evidence.

Evidence ①: deep inelastic scattering at SLAC

  • Deep inelastic scattering: at SLAC (an accelerator in the USA), very fast electrons are fired at protons (see the experiment in 5.4).
  • The electrons hit so hard that they broke the proton apart. This is what "inelastic" means: after the collision, things are not the same as before.
  • Result: far more electrons bounced at large angles than a "smooth proton" allows (Figure 5.1).
  • Conclusion: the proton has tiny, point-like charges (like dots). These are quarks.
  • This is the same logic as Rutherford's gold foil experiment: large-angle bounces → the charge is concentrated in very small points.
Model A: smooth proton

The positive charge is spread evenly through the whole proton. Prediction: electrons only deflect a little.

Model B: tiny charges inside

There are a few point-like charges inside (u = up, d = down). SLAC saw far more large-angle bounces.

Figure 5.1: Two models of the proton. Large-angle bounces show there are point-like charges inside: quarks.

Evidence ②: beta decay

  • n → p + e− + νe (νe is an antineutrino): a neutron (udd) changes into a proton (uud). Just one down quark changes into an up quark.
  • This is easy to explain if the neutron has parts. So it supports the quark model.
  • But it is not proof on its own: the muon is fundamental, but it also decays.

Evidence ③: the particle zoo

  • In the 1950s–60s, scientists found hundreds of new particles, so many that it was like a zoo. This is called the particle zoo.
  • In 1964 Gell-Mann and Zweig built them all from just three quarks (up, down, strange).
  • Gell-Mann's pattern (1962) also predicted a new particle, the Ω− (sss). It was found in 1964, as predicted.

Animation 1: electrons are fired at a proton one by one. First compare the two models, then press "Cover proton inside" and, like SLAC, look only at where the electrons go. In the animation, the colour of a quark only shows whether its charge is positive or negative.

5.2

The Standard Model: particles and forces

Big ideaThe Standard Model is the theory of the fundamental particles and forces: 6 quarks, 6 leptons and the bosons that carry the forces (Figure 5.2).

Four key words

  • Quark: a fundamental matter particle (a fermion) that does feel the strong force.
  • Lepton: a fundamental matter particle that does not feel the strong force. The electron is a lepton.
  • Hadron: a particle made of quarks, such as a proton.
  • Fermion / boson: its spin (a particle's built-in turning) is a half-number (such as ½) / a whole number (such as 0 for the Higgs boson).

Rules for quarks and leptons

  • The 6 types of quark are called 6 flavours. They are in 3 generations, each heavier than the one before.
  • The only quarks in ordinary matter are u and d: protons and neutrons are both built from them.
  • Colour charge: the strong force's "charge". It is not real colour, only a name. The colours of the quarks in the animations also only show positive or negative charge. They are not colour charge.
  • Confinement: a quark is never found alone, only inside hadrons.
  • An antiquark has a bar on top and the opposite charge: ū is −⅔e, d̄ is +⅓e.
  • Neutrinos have a tiny mass, no electric charge and no colour charge. So only the weak force and gravity act on them.

5.2

The four fundamental forces

Big ideaThe strong, EM and weak forces are all carried by bosons (the weak force has three: W+, W−, Z0). The graviton for gravity has never been found. Of the four forces, strong is the strongest and gravity is the weakest. Only the weak force can change one type of quark into another.

  • Exchange particle: a boson that carries a force. It is like two skaters throwing a ball to each other: one throws, the other catches, and the two are pushed apart.

  • Strong: a little of the pull between quarks leaks out of protons and neutrons. This small force holds the nucleus together, even though protons repel. This is the strong nuclear force of T4.
  • Weak: the only force that changes a quark's flavour. W and Z bosons are very heavy (about 86 and 97 proton masses), so they live very briefly and their range is tiny.
  • Beta-minus (β−): d → u + W−, then W− → e− + νe. Check the charge: −⅓ = +⅔ − 1.
  • Beta-plus (β+, only inside a nucleus): u → d + W+, then W+ → e+ + νe. Charge: +⅔ = −⅓ + 1.
  • Higgs field: it gives most fundamental particles mass. The Higgs field is like the surface of water, and its "ripple" is the Higgs boson.

5.2

Hadrons: baryons and mesons

Big ideaQuarks only come in groups: a group of three quarks is a baryon, and one quark plus one antiquark is a meson. The charge of each is the sum of its quark charges.

Baryon = 3 quarks

Proton (uud), neutron (udd). Three half-number spins add to a half-number, so baryons are fermions.

Meson = quark + antiquark

For example the pion π+ (ud̄). Two half-number spins add to a whole number, so mesons are bosons. All mesons are unstable.

5.3

Particle accelerators · The linear accelerator (linac)

Big ideaElectric fields speed charged particles up; magnetic fields steer them. Collisions of fast particles test the Standard Model.

Why accelerate? Two reasons

  1. To see finer details: a wave only shows details bigger than its wavelength. More momentum gives a shorter wavelength λ = h/(mv) (near c, use pv). SLAC's λ ≈ 6 × 10−17 m was 27 times smaller than a proton. A thick pen cannot draw a thin line: the wavelength is how thick the "pen tip" is.
  2. To make new particles: kinetic energy can become new mass (E = mc²). A particle–antiparticle pair (a particle and its antiparticle) needs at least 2mc² of energy.

The electric field pushes, the magnetic field turns

  • An electric field speeds a charge up: each gap adds W = qV. After n gaps from rest: K = nqV.
  • Energy is often given in eV (electron volt): 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J. A charge e moving through 1 V gains 1 eV.
  • The magnetic force F = qv⊥B = qvB sin θ is always at 90° to v. It only turns the particle and does no work, so the speed does not change.
  • Only charged particles can be accelerated. Electric and magnetic fields cannot push a neutral particle (such as a neutron).

The linear accelerator (linac)

A linac (linear accelerator) is a straight row of hollow metal tubes, called drift tubes. The tubes are joined in turn to the two ends of an AC supply: one tube to one end, the next tube to the other end (Figure 5.3; Animation 3 is drawn from it). On the far left is the proton source.

  1. In a tube: there is no electric field inside a metal tube, so the particle moves at constant speed (it coasts).
  2. In a gap: the gap between two tubes has an electric field. It gives the particle a push, and the particle gets faster.
  3. Swap: while the particle is in a tube, the AC swaps the + and − of every tube. So at the next gap the field still pushes it forward. Figure 5.3 shows one instant: half a cycle later, every + and − swaps.
  • The particle keeps getting faster, so each tube is longer than the one before. In this way the time in every tube is exactly half an AC cycle, and the timing stays right.
  • Analogy: pushing a swing. Push at the right moment each time and it swings higher and higher. Push at the wrong moment and you slow it down.
  • Limit: length. SLAC's linac is 3.2 km long.
  • Use: making X-rays in hospitals to treat cancer.

Animation 3: the proton coasts in the tubes and is pushed in the gaps. What comes out of the last tube is a stream of very fast protons, called a beam. Try turning the AC swap off, or making the tubes the same length.

5.3

The cyclotron

Big ideaThe magnetic field makes the ion go round. Every half turn it crosses the gap and the electric field pushes it. It gets faster and faster, the circles get bigger and bigger, and its path is a spiral going outward.

  • A cyclotron has two hollow D-shaped metal boxes, called dees, in a uniform magnetic field. The AC is connected across the gap between the two dees (Figure 5.4; Animation 4 is drawn from it: seen from above, with B into the page).
  • The particle being accelerated is an ion (a charged atom). It starts from the ion source in the centre.
  • In the gap: the electric field speeds the ion up.
  • In a dee: there is no electric field, only B. B bends it in a half circle. The speed stays the same, but the direction keeps changing, so the ion is also accelerating in a dee (it has an acceleration towards the centre, like the uniform circular motion in M5 T2). Accelerate means the velocity changes: a change in size or in direction both count.
  • Faster means a bigger circle, so the path spirals outward. A positive ion goes anticlockwise, and at the outer edge it flies off along the tangent (exit).
  • The AC flips while the ion is in a dee, so every gap crossing is a push forward.
  • Here is the surprise: the radius gets bigger, but the speed gets bigger too, so the time for a half turn does not change. So the AC needs only one fixed frequency.
  • Limit: near c, each turn takes longer, so the ion falls out of step with the AC.
  • Uses: making isotopes for PET (medical) scans; tumour therapy.

5.3

The synchrotron and the Large Hadron Collider

Big ideaA synchrotron keeps the particles going round on the same ring: the faster the particles, the stronger the magnetic field becomes. When two beams meet head-on, all the kinetic energy can be used to make new particles.

  • A cyclotron's magnet must cover the whole spiral. The faster the particles, the bigger the spiral, so the magnet must be bigger.
  • Synchrotron: the particles stay on one ring of fixed radius. Before they enter this ring, they go through a linac and some smaller rings.
  • RF cavity (a box with a fast-changing electric field): it pushes the particles once per lap, in time. "Synchro" means "in time".
  • The particles' momentum keeps getting bigger and B rises with it, so r = pv/(qB) stays fixed.
  • Collider: two beams meet head-on and the total momentum is zero, so all the kinetic energy can make new particles. If a beam hits a target that is not moving, what comes out must keep flying forward, so part of the kinetic energy cannot be used.
  • Large Hadron Collider (LHC, at CERN): 27 km ring, 8 T magnets, 13.6 TeV (13.6 × 1012 eV) per collision.

5.3

What accelerators have shown · Why our understanding is still incomplete

Big ideaTheory predicts a particle first, then an accelerator goes and finds it: the Standard Model has been confirmed again and again. But it is still incomplete: there are some big things it cannot explain.

  • A detector tracks what comes out. The direction a track curves tells us if the charge is positive or negative. How much it curves tells us the size of the momentum (the straighter the track, the larger the momentum).
  • Masses are in GeV/c² (1 GeV = 109 eV; rest energy in GeV). This is because E = mc², so m = E/c².
  • Each row of Table 5.3 is "theory predicts first → an accelerator confirms it". It can also go the other way: experiment can lead theory (particle zoo → quarks).

Why it is still incomplete

  • Gravity is not in the Standard Model; the graviton has never been found.
  • Dark matter (known only by its gravity) and dark energy (the unknown cause of the speeding-up expansion of the Universe) are together about 95% of the Universe. No Standard Model particle explains them.
  • Antimatter: the Big Bang should have made equal amounts of matter and antimatter, but the Universe is almost all matter.
  • Neutrino mass: in the first Standard Model, neutrinos had no mass. Since 1998, we know they have a tiny mass.
  • Unknowns: the reason for exactly three generations; about 19 numbers can only be measured.
5.4

Key experiments

Deep inelastic scattering at SLAC (1967–1973)

Aim
To find out whether the proton has parts inside it.
Method
SLAC's 3.2 km linac fired electrons (up to 20 GeV) at liquid hydrogen (its nuclei are single protons). Magnetic spectrometers (a magnet that bends the electrons, and detectors) measured each scattered electron's energy and angle.
Results
Far more large-angle scattering than a "smooth proton" allows.
Conclusion
Protons and neutrons contain point-like charged parts: quarks.
Significance
This was the first direct evidence of quarks (Nobel Prize 1990). See Animation 1.

Discovery of the Higgs boson at the LHC (2012)

Aim
To find the Higgs boson predicted in 1964, and measure its mass.
Method
Protons collided at 7 TeV to 8 TeV in total. The two detectors, ATLAS and CMS, kept only the collisions that gave "two photons" or "four leptons". From these collisions, they found the mass of the particle that made the photons or leptons.
Results
A small bump at 125 GeV/c² above the background, in both detectors. The chance of a random bump was below 1 in 3.5 million ("5 sigma").
Conclusion
A new boson of about 125 GeV/c² exists: the Higgs boson.
Significance
It was the last missing Standard Model particle (Nobel Prize 2013 for Englert and Higgs, who predicted it). The detector view in Animation 5 is illustrative, not a real Higgs event: it only shows how the curve of a track gives the charge and momentum.

Memorise these 7 before the exam

  1. SLAC's large-angle scattering showed quarks; beta decay and the Ω− support it.
  2. Positron, muon, pions, antiproton, neutrino: each had a new mass, charge or behaviour.
  3. Quarks u, c, t: +⅔e; d, s, b: −⅓e. Baryon = 3 quarks; meson = quark + antiquark.

Common mistakes

Wrong: beta decay proves that the neutron is made of quarks.
Right: it only supports this. The muon is fundamental and also decays, so "it decays" is not proof on its own.
Wrong: colour charge is the real colour of a quark.
Right: it is only a name for the strong force's "charge". It is not real colour.
Wrong: an antiquark has the same charge as its quark, for example d̄ is −⅓e.
Right: the sign flips: d̄ is +⅓e, ū is −⅔e.
Wrong: the charge of a hadron comes out as +⅓e.
Right: the total is always a whole number of e. If you get a fraction, a sign is wrong somewhere.
Wrong: β− decay releases a neutrino νe.
Right: β−: W− → e− + νe (antineutrino); β+: W+ → e+ + νe.
Wrong: gravity is also in the Standard Model, carried by the graviton.
Right: gravity is not in the Standard Model; the graviton has never been found.
Wrong: in a cyclotron, the magnetic field makes the particle faster.
Right: the magnetic force is perpendicular to v, does no work and only turns the particle. The electric field in the gap makes it faster. But turning also counts as accelerating (the direction is changing), so the ion is accelerating both in a dee and in the gap.
Wrong: r = mv/(qB) gives the diameter; T is written as m/(qB).
Right: r is the radius. T = 2πm/(qB): keep the 2π.
Wrong: in a cyclotron, the further out the particle goes, the longer one turn takes, so the AC frequency must change.
Right: well below c, T does not depend on speed, so the AC needs only one fixed frequency. Only near c does the ion fall out of step.
Wrong: a neutron can also be accelerated in an accelerator.
Right: only charged particles can be accelerated.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. At SLAC, many electrons bounced at large angles. What does this show about the inside of the proton?

It has tiny point-like charges inside: quarks. If the positive charge were spread evenly, electrons would only deflect a little. When the charge is concentrated in small points, an electron can pass very close to one. The force is then large, and only this gives a large-angle bounce.

2. Can beta decay alone prove that the neutron is made of quarks?

No, it only supports this. n → p + e− + νe is easy to explain as "one d changes into u". But the muon is fundamental and it also decays, so "it decays" cannot prove that there are parts inside.

3. Work out the charges: what are the charges of Ω− (sss) and π+ (ud̄)? Is each one a baryon or a meson?

Ω−: −1 (baryon); π+: +1 (meson). s is −⅓: 3 × (−⅓) = −1, which matches the "−" in its name; 3 quarks, so it is a baryon. π+: u is +⅔, and d̄ has the sign flipped, so it is +⅓: +⅔ + ⅓ = +1; one quark plus one antiquark, so it is a meson.

4. How do you write β− decay at the quark level? How does the charge balance? Which force is it?

d → u + W−, then W− → e− + νe; the weak force. Charge: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1), so the two sides are equal. Only the weak force can change a quark's flavour.

5. A proton starts from rest in a linac and passes 20 gaps, each 50 kV. What is its kinetic energy in eV? In J? What is its approximate speed?

K = 1.0 × 106 eV = 1.602 × 10−13 J; v ≈ 1.38 × 107 m s−1. K = nqV = 20 × e × 50 000 V = 1 000 000 eV. Multiply by 1.602 × 10−19 to get 1.602 × 10−13 J. Then use K = ½mv²: v = √(2K/m) = √(2 × 1.602 × 10−13 / 1.673 × 10−27) ≈ 1.38 × 107 m s−1. This is about 0.046c, which is below 0.1c, so you can use ½mv².

6. In a cyclotron, B = 0.5 T, and a proton has speed 1.0 × 107 m s−1. What is the radius of this half circle? How long does one full circle take?

r ≈ 0.209 m; T ≈ 1.31 × 10−7 s. r = mv/(qB) = 1.673 × 10−27 × 1.0 × 107 / (1.602 × 10−19 × 0.5) ≈ 0.209 m. T = 2πm/(qB) = 2π × 1.673 × 10−27 / (1.602 × 10−19 × 0.5) ≈ 1.31 × 10−7 s. There is no v in T: when the proton gets faster, T does not change.

7. In a synchrotron, the protons get faster and faster. Why must the magnetic field B also get stronger?

To keep the radius r fixed. r = pv/(qB). When pv gets bigger, if B does not change, r gets bigger and the beam hits the pipe wall. If B rises together with pv, r stays the same.

8. Why do we say the Standard Model is still incomplete? Give two reasons.

Any two of these: gravity is not in it (the graviton has not been found); dark matter and dark energy are about 95% of the Universe, and it cannot explain them; the Universe is almost all matter, not equal amounts of matter and antimatter; neutrinos have a tiny mass; we do not know why there are three generations, and about 19 numbers can only be measured.
