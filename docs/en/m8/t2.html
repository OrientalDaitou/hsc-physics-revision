Structure of the Atom

Explained from zero

This chapter covers five experiments. Step by step, they found what is inside the atom: first the electron, then the nucleus, and last the neutron. Every experiment uses the three rules below. Once you know these three, the rest is just remembering: who did it, in what year, and what they saw.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Electric field: between two parallel plates, E = V/d, and it points from the + plate to the − plate. The electric force is F = qE: a positive charge moves along E, and a negative charge (such as an electron) moves against E, which means towards the + plate.
  2. Magnetic field: a moving charge in a magnetic field feels a force F = qvB sin θ. For the direction, use the right-hand rule: fingers = B, thumb = v, the direction the palm pushes = the force on a positive charge. For an electron it is exactly the opposite. This force is always at 90° to v. When v and B are at 90° to each other, the particle moves in a circle: qvB = mv²/r.
  3. Kinetic energy and momentum: K = ½mv², momentum p = mv. In a collision with no outside force, the total momentum stays the same.
  4. Force between charges: like charges push each other away. The closer they are, the harder they push (Coulomb's law, F ∝ 1/r²).
2.1

Discovering the electron: cathode rays and Thomson

Big ideaElectric and magnetic fields both bend cathode rays, so they are negative particles.

  • Cathode ray: the invisible ray from the cathode (−) towards the anode (+) in a sealed glass tube with almost no air.
  • The two ends of the tube are connected to a high voltage (DC). It pulls charged particles out of the cathode and speeds them up.
  • The tube has a very low pressure, so almost no gas particles are in the way.
  • The glass glows where the rays hit. This is fluorescence (giving out light as soon as energy hits it).
  • At the time, there was a debate: are the rays waves (mostly scientists in Germany thought this) or particles (mostly in Britain)?

The evidence, one item at a time

What was seenWho · yearWhat it supports

A Maltese cross (a metal cross) in the beam leaves a sharp shadow behind it: the rays travel in straight lines—Fits both models
The rays pass through thin metal foilHertz, 1892Waves
A paddle wheel in the beam rolls away from the cathode (towards the anode)—Particles: at the time, this was taken as evidence that the rays carry momentum. Today we know the beam mainly turns it by heating the blades.
The rays are far slower than lightThomson, 1894Particles

The decisive evidence: fields bend charged particles, but not EM waves such as light (light has no charge). Fields bend cathode rays, so they are not waves. They are streams of negatively charged particles.

2.1

Thomson's charge-to-mass experiment (1897)

Big ideaThomson measured the q/m of this particle and found that it is far lighter than an atom: it is the electron.

  • Charge-to-mass ratio q/m: a particle's charge divided by its mass. Unit: C kg−1.
  • The anodes have small holes, called collimators. The rays can only pass through the small holes, so they become a thin, straight electron beam.
  • The beam passes through crossed fields: E and B at 90° to each other and to the beam (Figure 2.1 in the booklet).

First, the directions

  • Electric field: the + plate is on top and the − plate is below, so E points down. The electron is negative, so it is pushed towards the + plate: the electric force is up.
  • Magnetic field: a pair of coils outside the tube makes the magnetic field, with B into the page (⊗). Change the current in the coils, and B changes. The right-hand rule pushes a positive charge up, so electrons are pushed down.
  • One force pushes up and the other pushes down. With the right sizes, the two forces cancel.

Measuring q/m in two steps

  1. Step 1: adjust E and B until the beam goes straight. The two forces are now the same size: qE = qvB, so v = E/B. Only this one speed goes straight, so this set-up is called a velocity selector.
  2. Step 2: switch off E. Only the magnetic force is left. It is always at 90° to v, so the beam follows an arc of radius r: qvB = mv²/r.
  • The bright spot on the fluorescent screen: at the top with E only, in the middle when the two fields balance, and at the bottom with B only.
  • What about gravity? An electron's weight is over 1014 times smaller than qE, so ignore it.
Thomson's tube

First balance E and B to find v. Then keep only B and measure the radius r of the arc.

School e/m tube

Electron gun = a hot cathode + an anode. It speeds electrons from rest through a voltage V: qV = ½mv². A uniform B bends the beam into a full circle. A little gas is left in the tube and glows along the circle, so you can see it.

Why this result mattered

  • The measured q/m was about 1800 times the hydrogen ion's. For the same size of charge, the mass is only about 1/1800 of a hydrogen atom's. It is smaller than an atom: a subatomic particle.
  • q/m was the same for every metal and every gas. So this particle is in all atoms. It is the electron.
  • Atoms are neutral (no overall charge). They hold negative electrons, so they must also hold positive charge. Where is that positive charge? Section 2.3 answers this.

2.2

Millikan's oil drop experiment

Big ideaEvery oil drop's charge was a whole number times 1.6 × 10−19 C. So charge comes in packets: one packet is the charge of one electron.

  • Quantised: can only have certain separate values, with nothing in between. Like stairs: you can stand on a step, but not between two steps.
  • Elementary charge e: the smallest charge found on its own, 1.602 × 10−19 C. It is the size of an electron's charge.

The apparatus (Figure 2.2 in the booklet)

  • An atomiser sprays the oil into very small drops. Some drops fall through a small hole in the middle of the top plate, into the space between the two plates.
  • The + plate is on top and the − plate is below, a distance d apart. The voltage V can be changed (adjustable V).
  • A light shines on the drops from one side, and you watch them through a microscope from the other side. An X-ray source can change the charge on a drop.
  • The electric field points from the + plate to the − plate, down. So it pushes a negative drop up.

Two forces on the drop

Fg = mg

Weight, down.

FE = qE

Electric force, up. Adjust V until it exactly equals mg, and the drop hangs still: qE = mg.

  • First measure the mass: with the field off, the drop falls at its terminal velocity (steady speed, when air resistance balances weight). Heavier drops fall faster, so the falling speed gives m.
  • Oil is used instead of water because water evaporates, and the drop's mass would keep changing.

Why charge comes in packets

  • A drop can only gain or lose whole electrons. So the common "step" between the measured charges is e. The smallest measured charge may be 2e, but every charge is a whole number times e.
  • Note: Millikan measured drops, not one single electron.
  • Electron mass: Millikan's e divided by Thomson's e/me gives me = 9.11 × 10−31 kg, about 1/1836 of a proton's mass. The data sheet gives 9.109 × 10−31 kg. The only difference is rounding.

2.3

The nuclear atom: Geiger–Marsden and Rutherford

Big ideaMost alphas went straight through gold foil, but a very few bounced back. So an atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus in the middle.

  • Plum pudding model: Thomson's model (1904). Positive charge is spread through the whole atom, with electrons dotted through it like the plums in a pudding. There is no nucleus.
  • Alpha particle α: a helium nucleus, 2 protons and 2 neutrons, charge +2e.

Two models of the atom (Figure 2.3 in the booklet)

(a) Plum pudding model

Thomson, 1904. Positive charge is spread through the whole atom, with electrons set in it.

(b) Nuclear model

Rutherford, 1911. A tiny nucleus (+) in the middle, electrons moving around it outside, and almost everything else empty. In the booklet's figure, the nucleus is drawn far too big.

What they did (1909, Figure 2.4 in the booklet, top view)

  • Geiger and Marsden fired alphas at gold foil in a vacuum. The foil was very thin, only about 1000 atoms thick.
  • The alpha source was inside a block of lead, so it gave out only a thin beam of alphas.
  • A zinc sulfide screen went around the foil in a ring. When an alpha hit the screen, the screen gave a small flash, called a scintillation.
  • A microscope could move in a full circle around the foil. At each angle, the flashes were counted.

What did the plum pudding model predict?

  • The positive charge is spread out, so its push is weak.
  • Electrons are about 7300 times lighter than an alpha. They cannot stop it, just as a table tennis ball cannot stop a bowling ball.
  • So the prediction was: all alphas should pass straight through.

What was really seen (Table 2.1 in the booklet)

What was seenRutherford's explanation (1911)
Most alphas went straight through.The atom is mostly empty space.
Some were deflected by small angles.They passed fairly close to a positive nucleus.
About 1 in 8000 were deflected by more than 90°.They headed almost straight at a tiny nucleus that holds nearly all the mass, and it pushed them back. This is rare because the nucleus is tiny.
2.3

Rutherford's nuclear model (1911)

Big ideaIn the middle of the atom is a tiny nucleus that holds all the positive charge and nearly all the mass. This model got the nucleus right, but its picture of the electrons is incomplete.

  • Nuclear model (1911): a tiny nucleus holds all the positive charge and more than 99.9% of the mass.
  • The nucleus is about 10 000 to 100 000 times smaller across than the atom. For example: if an atom were as big as a football field, the nucleus would be only as big as a pea in the middle.
  • Electrons move around the nucleus, far away. The rest is empty space.
  • The alphas never touch the nucleus: the electric force pushes them away. By Coulomb's law, only tightly packed charge can turn a fast alpha back.

Strengths

It explains all three gold foil results, and why atoms are neutral. In 1913, counts at many angles, with different metals and different foil thicknesses, matched the predictions of Coulomb's law.

Limitations

① An orbiting electron accelerates. Classical physics says it should radiate, lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. ② It cannot explain line spectra. ③ It does not say what the nucleus is made of, or why a nucleus has about 2 times the mass of its protons.

  • Judgement: strongly supported for the nucleus, but incomplete for the electrons.
  • Why this set-up: gold can be made very thin and has a large nuclear charge (+79e), so the push is strong. The vacuum stops air from scattering the alphas.
2.4

Chadwick and the neutron

Big ideaA nucleus has about 2 times the mass of its protons. Chadwick (1932) used conservation of energy and momentum to show that the missing mass is neutrons.

  • Neutron 10n: a neutral particle in the nucleus with about the mass of a proton.
  • Elastic collision: a collision in which the total kinetic energy stays the same.

Finding it, step by step

  1. 1919: Rutherford used alphas to knock hydrogen nuclei out of nitrogen. In 1920 he named these nuclei protons (charge +e).
  2. Missing mass: helium has 2 protons but about 4 times hydrogen's mass. So he predicted (1920) that the nucleus also holds a neutral particle.
  3. 1930: alphas on beryllium gave a very penetrating radiation. Electric and magnetic fields did not bend it, so it has no charge. Bothe and Becker thought it was gamma rays.
  4. 1932: this radiation knocks fast protons out of paraffin wax (Curie and Joliot). Chadwick also used nitrogen as a target (Figure 2.5 in the booklet).
  5. How it was measured: an ionisation chamber measured the recoil particles that were knocked out. A charged particle ionises the gas inside, making a tiny current.

Chadwick's apparatus (Figure 2.5 in the booklet)

  1. polonium: the alpha source. It gives out alpha particles.
  2. beryllium: hit by alphas, it gives out neutral radiation.
  3. paraffin wax: it has lots of H (hydrogen). The radiation knocks protons out of it.
  4. ionisation chamber: the detector. It measures the energy of the protons.
Neutron hits a proton head-on

The masses are the same. The neutron stops, and the proton gets almost all the energy. Like billiards: the white ball hits an identical ball head-on, the white ball stops, and the other ball flies off.

Neutron hits nitrogen head-on

A nitrogen nucleus is 14 times heavier. The neutron bounces back, and the nitrogen nucleus gets only about a quarter of the energy. Like a tennis ball hitting a basketball at rest (a basketball is about 10 times heavier): the tennis ball bounces back, and the basketball slowly rolls forward.

Using energy to rule out gamma rays

  • The fastest protons had about 5.7 MeV (MeV: mega-electron volt, a unit of energy).
  • A photon has no mass. It has little momentum for its energy. So to knock out a 5.7 MeV proton, a photon would need about 50 MeV.
  • The beryllium reaction could give at most about 14 MeV. That is not enough, so this radiation is not gamma rays.

Using momentum and kinetic energy to find the mass

  • The particle's mass m and speed u are both unknown. Starting from them, the two conservation equations below give a target's recoil speed.
  • Chadwick used two targets: hydrogen (about 1 atomic mass unit, written 1 u) and nitrogen (about 14 u).
  • Dividing the two results cancels the unknown speed u. Only the mass is left: m ≈ 1.15 u, about a proton's mass.
  • Note the two u's: italic u is a speed; upright u is the atomic mass unit, 1 u = 1.661 × 10−27 kg (data sheet).
  • Data sheet: mn = 1.675 × 10−27 kg, mp = 1.673 × 10−27 kg. A neutron is only a tiny bit heavier than a proton.

The nuclear reaction and "indirect evidence"

42He + 94Be → 126C + 10n
  • In AZX: A = protons + neutrons (mass number); Z = protons (atomic number).
  • The total of A is the same on both sides (4 + 9 = 12 + 1 = 13), and so is the total of Z (2 + 4 = 6 + 0 = 6).
  • The neutron has no charge and leaves no track. We cannot see it. We can only work it out from the protons and nitrogen nuclei it knocks out. This is called indirect evidence (evidence from effects only).
2.5

Key experiments

To model an experiment is to draw and label its set-up, or to use a simulation (such as the animations on this page), or to use an analogy. One analogy: roll marbles under a board and see how they come out. This reveals the shape of the object hidden under the board.

Early cathode-ray experiments (1870s–1890s)

Aim
To find whether cathode rays are waves or particles.
Method
Objects (a Maltese cross, a paddle wheel) were put in the beam. Charged plates or a magnet were brought near the tube.
Results
Sharp shadows; fields bent the beam in the same direction as negative charges.
Conclusion and significance
The rays travel in straight lines and carry negative charge: they are negative particles. This led to Thomson's work.

Thomson's charge-to-mass experiment (1897)

Aim
To measure q/m for cathode-ray particles.
Method
E and B were adjusted until the beam went straight (v = E/B). Then E was switched off; the radius r of the arc gave q/m = E/(B²r). This was repeated with other metals and gases.
Results
About 1011 C kg−1 for every metal and every gas (today's value: 1.759 × 1011 C kg−1).
Conclusion and significance
The same particle is in all matter: it is negative and far lighter than atoms. This is the electron. It was the first subatomic particle, and it led to the plum pudding model.

Millikan's oil drop experiment (1909–1913)

Aim
To find whether charge comes in a smallest unit, and measure it.
Method
With the field off, a drop's falling speed gave its mass. Then V was adjusted until the drop hung still: q = mgd/V. X-rays changed its charge; many drops were measured.
Results
Every charge was a whole number times 1.592 × 10−19 C (today's value: 1.602 × 10−19 C).
Conclusion and significance
Charge is quantised; the smallest unit e is the electron's charge. With Thomson's q/m, it gave the electron's mass.

The Geiger–Marsden experiment (1909)

Aim
To test the plum pudding model.
Method
In a vacuum, a narrow beam of alphas hit thin gold foil. At each angle, flashes on a zinc sulfide screen were counted through a microscope.
Results
Most went straight through; some were deflected by small angles; about 1 in 8000 were deflected by more than 90°.
Conclusion and significance
A tiny, dense, positive nucleus in the middle of the atom; the atom is mostly empty space. This was the discovery of the nucleus, and the basis of Bohr's model and of nuclear physics.

Chadwick's discovery of the neutron (1932)

Aim
To identify the neutral radiation from beryllium hit by alphas.
Method
The radiation hit paraffin wax, then nitrogen. The recoil energies of the protons and nitrogen nuclei were measured. Conservation of energy and momentum were applied.
Results
Protons up to 5.7 MeV; nitrogen nuclei recoiled slowly; no single gamma-ray energy fits both results.
Conclusion and significance
Neutral particles with about the proton's mass: neutrons. This completed the picture of the atom and explained the missing mass. Neutrons were later used to split nuclei.

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. Fields bend cathode rays but not light, so cathode rays are negative particles.
  2. Thomson: v = E/B, then q/m = E/(B²r) (e/m tube: 2V/(B²r²)). q/m is the same for every metal and every gas: electrons are in all atoms. These are not on the sheet.
  3. Millikan: a still drop has qE = mg, so q = mgd/V. Every charge is q = ne: charge is quantised.
  4. Gold foil: most alphas pass straight through (the atom is mostly empty space); about 1 in 8000 are deflected by more than 90° (a tiny, dense, positive nucleus).
  5. Rutherford's model has strong evidence for the nucleus, but it cannot explain line spectra, or why orbiting electrons do not spiral in.
  6. Chadwick: a gamma ray would need more energy than the reaction gives. Momentum and kinetic energy, with two targets, give a neutral particle with about a proton's mass.

Common mistakes

Wrong: v = B/E.
Right: v = E/B: E over B.
Wrong: using r in cm, or using the diameter read from the scale as r.
Right: r in metres; if you read the diameter, divide it by 2.
Wrong: using q = mgd/V while the drop is still moving.
Right: it works only for a still drop (qE = mg). d must also be in metres.
Wrong: calculating with the −1.602 × 10−19 C printed on the sheet, and getting a negative n.
Right: use the size of e, 1.602 × 10−19 C, and work out the direction separately.
Wrong: Millikan measured the charge of one electron.
Right: he measured oil drops. Every drop's charge is a whole number times e, so e is the common "step".
Wrong: an alpha that bounces back has hit the nucleus.
Right: alphas never touch the nucleus. The electric force (Coulomb's law) pushes them back.
Wrong: a turning paddle wheel proves that the rays carry momentum.
Right: that is what people thought at the time. Today we know the beam mainly turns it by heating the blades.
Wrong: Chadwick saw the neutron's track.
Right: a neutron has no charge and leaves no track. He worked it out from the protons and nitrogen nuclei it knocked out: indirect evidence.
Wrong: kinetic energy is conserved in all collisions.
Right: with no outside force, momentum is always conserved; kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision.

1. Why is "fields bend cathode rays but not light" the decisive evidence?

Because it shows that cathode rays have charge and are not an EM wave. Light has no charge, so fields cannot bend it. Cathode rays are pushed towards the + plate, so they are negatively charged particles.

2. In Thomson's experiment, the beam goes straight when E = 2.0 × 104 V m−1 and B = 1.0 × 10−3 T. What is the speed? After E is switched off, the radius of the arc is r = 0.114 m. What is q/m?

v = 2.0 × 107 m s−1; q/m ≈ 1.75 × 1011 C kg−1. v = E/B = 2.0 × 104 / 1.0 × 10−3 = 2.0 × 107 m s−1. q/m = E/(B²r) = 2.0 × 104 / (1.0 × 10−6 × 0.114) ≈ 1.75 × 1011 C kg−1, very close to today's value of 1.759 × 1011.

3. An oil drop has mass m = 1.0 × 10−15 kg, and the plates are d = 5.0 mm apart. It hangs still when the voltage is set to V = 153 V. What is its charge? How many extra electrons does it have?

q ≈ 3.20 × 10−19 C, n = 2. First change d to metres: 5.0 × 10−3 m. q = mgd/V = 1.0 × 10−15 × 9.8 × 5.0 × 10−3 / 153 ≈ 3.20 × 10−19 C. n = q/e = 3.20 / 1.602 ≈ 2.

4. Use Millikan's e = 1.602 × 10−19 C and Thomson's q/m = 1.759 × 1011 C kg−1 to calculate the electron's mass.

About 9.11 × 10−31 kg. me = e ÷ (e/me) = 1.602 × 10−19 / 1.759 × 1011 ≈ 9.11 × 10−31 kg, about 1/1836 of a proton's mass.

5. Gold foil: what does "most alphas pass straight through" show? What does "about 1 in 8000 are deflected by more than 90°" show?

The atom is mostly empty space; in the middle is a tiny, dense, positive nucleus. Passing straight through shows that there is almost nothing in the way. Being pushed back shows that there is a very concentrated positive charge with nearly all the mass. This is rare because the nucleus is tiny.

6. What are the limitations of Rutherford's model?

Three. ① An orbiting electron accelerates. By classical physics, it should radiate, lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. ② It cannot explain line spectra. ③ It does not say what the nucleus is made of, or why a nucleus has about 2 times the mass of its protons.

7. Why did Chadwick say the radiation from beryllium was not gamma rays?

There is not enough energy. To knock out a 5.7 MeV proton, a gamma-ray photon would need about 50 MeV (a photon has no mass and little momentum for its energy). But the beryllium reaction can give at most about 14 MeV.

8. Check whether A and Z balance on the two sides of the reaction 42He + 94Be → 126C + 10n. Why do we say the neutron was found through "indirect evidence"?

They balance: A is 13 on both sides, and Z is 6 on both sides. A: 4 + 9 = 13, 12 + 1 = 13. Z: 2 + 4 = 6, 6 + 0 = 6. A neutron has no charge and leaves no track, so we cannot see it. We can only work it out from the protons and nitrogen nuclei it knocks out, so this is indirect evidence.
