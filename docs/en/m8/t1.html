Origins of the Elements: the Big Bang and the Stars

Explained from zero

This chapter is about where the elements come from. The Big Bang made hydrogen and helium, and stars then fused them into heavier elements. There is a lot of content, but it rests on only three rules. Understand them first: every section after that just uses them.

1.1

From radiation to matter: the Big Bang

Big ideaAbout 13.8 billion years ago the Universe was very hot and dense. As space expanded, it cooled, and energy turned into matter (E = mc²).

Pair production

A photon turns into a particle and its antiparticle. The two particles in a pair have the same mass and opposite charge.

Annihilation

A particle meets its antiparticle. Both disappear and turn into photons.

  • The Big Bang was not an explosion into empty space. It is space itself stretching, everywhere at once, so there is no centre. Expanding space cools.
  • E = mc² (on the sheet): mass and energy are two forms of the same thing. E is in J, m is in kg.
  • Early photons had very high energy. When one photon has E ≥ 2mc², it can make a pair of particles, such as an electron e− and a positron e+. This must happen near a nucleus, or in a collision with a second photon (then the energy of the two photons together must reach 2mc²).
  • Why 2m: a pair is two particles. Analogy: 2mc² is the "price of a pair of particles", and you can buy it only if you have enough money. A proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron (a number from 2.2 in the booklet), so a proton pair costs much more.
  • As the Universe got colder, photons had less and less energy. Proton pairs stopped first (they need more energy). Electron pairs stopped a few seconds after the Big Bang.
  • Matter and antimatter annihilated each other. But 1 particle in every billion was left: all the matter today is this tiny remainder.

In order of time

  1. Inflation: very, very fast expansion, 10−36 to 10−32 s.
  2. Quarks (tiny particles) join into protons and neutrons: 10−6 s.
  3. Helium nuclei form: 3–20 minutes, 109 K.
  4. Atoms form and light is set free: 380 000 years, 3000 K.
  5. First stars: 200 million years.
  6. Today: 2.7 K.

This making of nuclei is called Big Bang nucleosynthesis. By mass, it left about 75 % hydrogen and 25 % helium, and a trace of lithium. Heavier elements were made later, in stars (see 1.5).

1.2

Evidence for an expanding Universe

Big ideaThe further away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away (its light is redshifted). So space is expanding, and long ago the Universe was small, hot and dense.

Step 1: spectral lines and redshift give the speed

  • Each element has its own set of spectral lines (dark lines in starlight), like a fingerprint.
  • A source moving away from us stretches its waves. All lines shift to longer wavelengths (the red end): this is called redshift. When a source moves towards us, the lines shift to shorter wavelengths: this is called blueshift.
  • A bigger shift means a faster speed.
  • Slipher (from 1912) found that most galaxies were redshifted. They are moving away from us.

Step 2: Cepheids give the distance

  • A Cepheid variable is a type of star that brightens and dims in a regular cycle.
  • Leavitt (1912): the time for one cycle → its luminosity (true brightness). Compare this with how bright it looks → how far away it is.
  • Analogy: you know a light bulb is 100 W. How dim it looks tells you how far away it is.

Step 3: Hubble put the two together

  • Hubble used Cepheids to show that Andromeda is another galaxy (1924).
  • In 1929 he found v ∝ d: twice as far, twice as fast. This is called Hubble's law.
  • Plot v against d. The gradient is the Hubble constant H0, about 70 km s−1 Mpc−1. Mpc is a megaparsec, a very large unit of distance.

You can also derive it: the source sends out one wave every period T, so λ0 = cT. In this time the source moves away a distance vT, so the wave is stretched by Δλ = vT. Divide: Δλ/λ0 = v/c. At constant speed, t = d/v = d/(H0d) = 1/H0.

Trap: before you work out t, first change H0 from km s−1 Mpc−1 into s−1: H0 = 70 is 2.27 × 10−18 s−1.
Note: H0, the number of metres in 1 Mpc and the laboratory wavelengths of spectral lines are not on the data sheet. The question will give them.

1.2

What the expansion means; more evidence for the Big Bang

Big ideaThe Universe looks the same from everywhere. Every galaxy sees the others moving away, so space itself is stretching, and there is no centre.

  • Cosmological principle: on a large scale, the Universe looks the same from everywhere.
  • So every galaxy sees the others moving away. This shows that space itself is stretching, and Earth is not the centre.
  • Analogy: baking raisin bread. As the dough rises, each raisin sees the other raisins moving away. Raisins further away move away faster. No raisin in the dough is the centre.
  • Run the film backwards: everything is squeezed together, and was once hot and dense. Going back at today's speeds, t ≈ 1/H0 = 1.4 × 1010 years, very close to the 13.8 billion years in 1.1. 1/H0 is only a rough estimate (it treats the speed as constant).
Evidence 1: Hubble's law

Redshift gives the speed, Cepheids give the distance. v ∝ d: space is expanding.

Evidence 2: CMB and 25 % helium

Faint microwaves come from every direction; the oldest stars have about 25 % helium. Both match what the Big Bang predicts.

  • Cosmic microwave background (CMB): found in 1965. It is faint microwave radiation from every direction.
  • It is the light set free at 380 000 years. Expanding space stretched it about 1100 times. Today it is a 2.7 K black body (explained in 1.3).
  • The oldest stars have about 25 % helium, exactly as Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts.
1.3

Spectra and Wien's law

Big ideaA hot, dense object gives a continuous spectrum set only by its temperature. A gas gives bright or dark lines that identify the element. Dark lines also sort stars into classes.

SpectrumWhat you seeWhere it comes from
Continuous spectrumevery wavelength, like a rainbowa hot, dense object, such as a lamp filament
Emission spectrumonly a few bright lines on a black backgroundhot, thin hydrogen gas
Absorption spectruma rainbow with a few dark linescooler hydrogen gas in front of a hot source

Hydrogen's lines are at 410.2, 434.1, 486.2 and 656.3 nm. Its dark lines are at exactly the same positions as its bright lines (Figure 1.1 in the booklet).

To see what the three kinds of spectrum look like, go to Animation 2 in M7 Topic 1.

Where the lines come from

An electron in an atom can only have certain energies, called energy levels. It is like a staircase: you can stand only on a step, not between two steps. Topic 3 explains this in detail.

Emission

Heat or a current lifts electrons. When an electron drops back, it gives out one photon: E = hf = the gap between the two levels.

Absorption

A photon with exactly the gap energy lifts an electron. This light is given out again, but in all directions, so less of it comes towards us, and the line is dark. Same gaps, same wavelengths.

Black body and Wien's law

  • A black body absorbs all light. The light it gives out depends only on its temperature.
  • Its particles jostle all the time, so it gives out every wavelength.
  • Hotter: more light at every wavelength, and a shorter peak wavelength. This is Wien's displacement law.

1.3

Classifying stars

Big ideaStarlight is an absorption spectrum. The pattern of dark lines is set mainly by temperature, so dark lines can sort stars from hot to cool as O B A F G K M.

  • Starlight is an absorption spectrum: the star's cooler outer gas absorbs some wavelengths.
  • Spectral classes, hottest to coolest: O B A F G K M. To remember them: "Oh Be A Fine Guy, Kiss Me".
  • Each class is split again from 0 to 9. These are subclasses, and 0 is the hottest. The Sun is G2.
  • Two words: ionised = an atom has lost electrons. "Metal" in astronomy means any element heavier than helium.

Why the dark lines differ between classes

  • Payne (1925): the classes differ because of temperature, not because of different elements.
  • Hydrogen's visible lines need electrons to be in n = 2 first. Class A has just the right temperature, so its hydrogen lines are the strongest. Cooler stars keep their electrons in n = 1. Class O is too hot: its hydrogen is already ionised.

What else spectral lines tell us

  • All lines shift together: the star is moving towards or away from us (the redshift / blueshift of 1.2).
  • Line width → gas density. Broad lines: a small, dense main sequence star. Narrow lines: a huge giant.
  • This is the luminosity class: I is supergiant, III is giant, V is main sequence. The Sun is written G2V.
  • Fast rotation also broadens lines: one edge comes towards us, the other moves away.
1.4

Reading the HR diagram

Big ideaAn HR diagram (Hertzsprung–Russell diagram) plots luminosity against surface temperature. A star's place on it shows its colour, luminosity, size and stage of life. Its mass decides how it lives and dies.

  • Luminosity L: the total power a star gives out (W). Distance does not change it. The Sun: L⊙ = 3.83 × 1026 W.
  • Apparent brightness: how bright it looks. It depends on distance: the further away the same star is, the dimmer it looks.
  • Absolute magnitude M: how bright a star would look from 10 parsecs. A smaller number is brighter, like places in a race. A difference of 5 magnitudes = a factor of 100.

Example: MA = 0, MB = 5: LA/LB = 100(5 − 0)/5 = 100. A is 100 times brighter.
Trap: a smaller M is brighter. The exponent is MB − MA: do not swap them. A difference of 5 magnitudes = 100 times, a difference of 10 magnitudes = 10 000 times.

How to read the HR diagram

  • Hot stars are on the left (blue, class O); bright stars are at the top. Note: the horizontal axis runs backwards. Temperature falls as you go to the right.
  • Main sequence: a diagonal band. These stars are fusing H into He. About 90 % of stars are here, because it is the longest stage of life.
  • Top right: red giants and supergiants. Cool but very luminous → they must be very big.
  • Bottom left: white dwarfs. Hot but very dim → they must be very small.
  • Why we can reason like this: stars with the same temperature give out the same power from each m². So a hot, dim star can only have a very small area.

1.4

The life of a star

Big ideaHow a star lives and dies depends on how heavy it is at the start: light stars end as a white dwarf, and heavy stars end by exploding as a supernova.

  1. A gas cloud (nebula) collapses into a hot protostar.
  2. At about 10 million K, hydrogen fusion starts. This is the main sequence. Inward gravity balances outward gas pressure: this is called stellar equilibrium. Analogy: in a tug of war, both sides pull equally hard and the rope does not move.
  3. More mass (M⊙ = the Sun's mass) means faster fusion and a shorter life. The Sun: 10 billion years. A 10 M⊙ star: only 30 million years. Analogy: a big truck has a big fuel tank, but it burns fuel much faster, so it runs out first.
  4. Later, it swells into a red giant or a red supergiant.
Starting mass below 8 M⊙ (like the Sun)

red giant → planetary nebula (the outer layers drift off) → white dwarf: the core left behind is below 1.4 M⊙. A white dwarf is about the size of Earth.

Starting mass above 8 M⊙

red supergiant → supernova → neutron star: core 1.4–3 M⊙, about 20 km across; or black hole: core above 3 M⊙, light cannot escape.

1.5

Fusion in stars: E = mc² and nucleosynthesis

Big ideaIn nuclear fusion, light nuclei join. The products have a little less mass. This mass defect Δm (mass before − mass after) becomes energy: E = Δm c².

  • 4 1
    1    H → 4
    2    He     turns 0.7 % of the mass into energy: 6.4 × 1014 J per kg, over 10 million times as much as petrol. So stars can shine for billions of years.
  • Fusion releases energy only up to iron.
  • Fusion needs over 10 million K. Nuclei are all positive and repel each other. Only very fast nuclei get close enough to join. Analogy: two magnets with the same poles facing each other need a big force to push them together.

1.5

After the main sequence: heavier elements

Big ideaAfter the main sequence, stars go on to fuse helium into carbon and oxygen. Massive stars fuse layer by layer, up to iron. Elements heavier than iron are made by neutron capture.

  • In a red giant (about 100 million K): the triple-alpha process 3 4
    2    He → 12
    6    C    . Then 12
    6    C + 4
    2    He → 16
    8    O + γ    .
  • Massive stars fuse carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon layer by layer (in shells), up to iron. It is like an onion, with one layer around another.
  • Fusing iron does not release energy. It takes in energy, so the core collapses.
  • Elements heavier than iron are made by neutron capture: a nucleus catches neutrons. Where: supernovae, neutron star collisions, old giant stars.
  • Supernovae and planetary nebulae spread these elements through space, and they become material for new stars.

Putting it together: where the elements come from

  1. H (which is just a proton, 10−6 s), He and a trace of Li (3–20 minutes) ← Big Bang.
  2. H → He ← main sequence stars (p–p chain or CNO cycle).
  3. He → C, O ← red giants (triple-alpha).
  4. Up to Fe ← the layered shells in massive stars.
  5. Heavier than Fe ← neutron capture.
1.6

Key experiments

Hubble's law from galaxy data

Aim
To show that v ∝ d.
Method
Hubble (1929) found speeds from redshifts and distances from Cepheids, then plotted v against d.
Results
A straight line through the origin, so v ∝ d. Hubble's own gradient was about 500 km s−1 Mpc−1, because his distances were too small. Today H0 ≈ 70 km s−1 Mpc−1, so t ≈ 1/H0 = 1.4 × 1010 years.
Conclusion
Space is expanding.
Significance
The first strong evidence for the Big Bang (Hubble–Lemaître law).

Observing continuous, emission and absorption spectra

Aim
To compare the three kinds of spectrum.
Method
Use a spectroscope (it splits light into its colours) to view three things: a filament lamp, gas discharge tubes, and the lamp through a sodium flame.
Results
Lamp: a rainbow. Discharge tubes: bright lines for each gas (hydrogen: 656, 486, 434, 410 nm). Through the sodium flame: a dark line at 589 nm.
Conclusion
Dark lines sit where the bright lines are.
Significance
So spectral lines show what stars are made of.

The hydrogen lines here are written as whole numbers of nm; Figure 1.1 gives one decimal place (410.2, 434.1, 486.2, 656.3 nm). They are the same lines.

Plotting an HR diagram from star data

Aim
To find the groups of stars.
Method
Plot real stars on a diagram: the horizontal axis is temperature (hot on the left), and the vertical axis is absolute magnitude (bright at the top).
Results
The Sun, Sirius A and Vega are on the main sequence; Betelgeuse and Rigel are supergiants, above it; Sirius B (a white dwarf) is below it on the left.
Conclusion
Stars group by size and stage of life.
Significance
The main sequence is the long, steady stage of life.

Common mistakes

Wrong: The Big Bang exploded from one point in empty space, and that point is the centre of the Universe.
Right: Space itself stretches, everywhere at once. There is no centre, and Earth is not the centre either.
Wrong: To make an electron and positron pair, a photon needs mec².
Right: A pair is two particles, so it needs 2mec².
Wrong: Using H0 = 70 directly to get t = 1/70.
Right: First change it to s−1: 70 km s−1 Mpc−1 = 2.27 × 10−18 s−1.
Wrong: Redshift shifts every line by the same number of nm.
Right: Every line gets longer by the same ratio: Δλ = zλ0, so long-wavelength lines shift more.
Wrong: z ≈ v/c and v = H0d can always be used.
Right: z ≈ v/c is only for light, with v below about 0.1c; v = H0d is only for distant galaxies.
Wrong: Using °C for T in Wien's law, or taking the wavelength of a spectral line as λmax.
Right: T is in kelvin. λmax is the peak of a continuous spectrum, and it is only for black bodies.
Wrong: Dark lines appear because atoms "eat" that light, and it is gone.
Right: The light is given out again, but in all directions, so less of it comes towards us.
Wrong: The dark lines differ between spectral classes because the stars are made of different elements.
Right: It is mainly because their temperatures differ (Payne).
Wrong: A larger absolute magnitude is brighter; hot stars are on the right of the HR diagram.
Right: A smaller M is brighter; on the HR diagram, hot is on the left and bright is at the top.
Wrong: A hot, dim star is very far away from us.
Right: The HR diagram uses luminosity, which does not depend on distance. Hot and dim means the star is small (a white dwarf).
Wrong: In one question, using u → MeV in one place and kg → J in another.
Right: Use one route only, and keep every decimal place.
Wrong: Stars can keep on fusing and make all the elements.
Right: Fusion only goes up to iron; elements heavier than iron need neutron capture.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. What is the least energy a photon needs to make an electron and positron pair? What about a proton pair? Which kind stops first?

Electron pair: 1.64 × 10−13 J; proton pair: 3.01 × 10−10 J; proton pairs stop first. A pair needs 2mc². Electron: 2 × 9.109 × 10−31 × (3.00 × 108)² ≈ 1.64 × 10−13 J. Proton: 2 × 1.673 × 10−27 × (3.00 × 108)² ≈ 3.01 × 10−10 J. As the Universe cools, photon energy falls, and it first becomes too small for the "expensive" proton pair.

2. A galaxy is 200 Mpc away, and H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1. What are its speed and z? Where does the hydrogen 656.3 nm line move to?

v = 14 000 km s−1, z ≈ 0.0467, and the line moves to about 686.9 nm. v = H0d = 70 × 200 = 14 000 km s−1 = 1.4 × 107 m s−1 (below 0.1c, so z ≈ v/c can be used). z = 1.4 × 107 ÷ 3.00 × 108 ≈ 0.0467. Δλ = zλ0 = 0.0467 × 656.3 ≈ 30.6 nm. λ = 656.3 + 30.6 ≈ 686.9 nm, a shift towards the red end.

3. Use H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1 (1 Mpc = 3.086 × 1022 m) to estimate the age of the Universe.

t ≈ 4.41 × 1017 s ≈ 1.4 × 1010 years. First change the units: H0 = 70 × 10³ m s−1 ÷ 3.086 × 1022 m ≈ 2.27 × 10−18 s−1. t ≈ 1/H0 = 1 ÷ 2.27 × 10−18 ≈ 4.41 × 1017 s. One year = 365 × 24 × 3600 ≈ 3.15 × 107 s, so t ≈ 1.4 × 1010 years.

4. The CMB today is a 2.7 K black body. What is its peak wavelength? Why is it called microwave?

λmax ≈ 1.07 × 10−3 m, about 1 mm. λmax = b/T = 2.898 × 10−3 ÷ 2.7 ≈ 1.07 × 10−3 m. Waves of about 1 mm are microwaves, so it is called the cosmic microwave background. It was originally light given out at 3000 K, and space has stretched it about 1100 times.

5. Star A has absolute magnitude −1, and star B has 9. Which is brighter? How many times brighter?

A is brighter, 10 000 times brighter. A smaller M is brighter, and −1 is smaller than 9. LA/LB = 100(MB − MA)/5 = 100(9 − (−1))/5 = 100² = 10 000. A difference of 10 magnitudes = 10 000 times.

6. A star is at the bottom left of the HR diagram. What kind of star is it? Why must it be very small?

White dwarf. Left = very hot, bottom = very low luminosity. Stars with the same temperature give out the same power from each m². Hot and dim means its surface area must be very small: the star is about the size of Earth.

7. The Sun and a 10 M⊙ star: which stays on the main sequence longer? What does each one finally become?

The Sun stays longer (10 billion years, against 30 million years). More mass means faster fusion and a shorter life. The Sun (below 8 M⊙): red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf. 10 M⊙ (above 8 M⊙): red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole (depending on how heavy the core left behind is).

8. The Sun's L⊙ = 3.83 × 1026 W. How much mass does it lose each second? Each helium-4 made gives out 26.7 MeV: how much mass (in u) is lost for each one?

About 4.26 × 109 kg each second; about 0.0287 u for each helium-4. Δm/Δt = P/c² = 3.83 × 1026 ÷ (3.00 × 108)² = 3.83 × 1026 ÷ 9.00 × 1016 ≈ 4.26 × 109 kg s−1. Δm = E ÷ 931.5 = 26.7 ÷ 931.5 ≈ 0.0287 u. This uses only the one route, MeV ↔ u.
