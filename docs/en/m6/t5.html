Motors, Generators & Braking

Explained from zero

This chapter has no new rules. It only uses the two rules from earlier, on four machines. First we make these two rules clear. After that, the rest is simple.

Warm-up

Know these 3 first

  1. Right-hand palm rule: hold your right hand flat. Fingers = field B, thumb = current I, the direction the palm pushes = force F.
  2. Magnetic flux Φ = BA cos θ: how many field lines pass through the coil. It is largest when the coil faces the field, and 0 when the coil is side-on (parallel to the field).
  3. Faraday's law and Lenz's law: the faster the flux changes, the bigger the emf. The induced current always acts against the "change". If the flux does not change, there is no emf, however big the flux is.
5.1

Torque on a coil & the simple DC motor

Big ideaThe two long sides of the coil carry opposite currents. So one side is pushed up and the other is pushed down, and the coil turns.

  • Put a coil (many loops of wire, called turns) between the N and S poles of a magnet.
  • Each long side is at 90° to the field. With n turns, the force on each long side is F = nlIB.
  • In one long side the current comes out of the page ⊙. In the other it goes into the page ⊗. The palm rule shows that one force is up and one is down.
  • These two forces are equal, opposite and not in line. They are called a couple. It is like turning a steering wheel with two hands: one hand pushes up and the other pulls down.
  • The turning effect is called torque (τ). Its unit is N m.
  • As the coil turns, the forces keep the same size. Only the lever arm (r⊥) changes: how far the line of the force is from the axle.
  • The forces on the two short sides (the ends of the coil) point along the axle. They do not turn the coil, so they give no torque.
  • If only one side is in the field: the force on that side is F = lIB (the side is at 90° to B), and the torque is τ = r⊥F.
plane ∥ B

The plane of the coil is parallel to the field. The lever arm is longest, so the torque is largest.

plane ⊥ B

The plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field. The two forces are in line, so torque = 0.

The four parts of a DC motor

PartWhat it does
StatorThe part that does not move. It makes the magnetic field (magnets).
Rotor / armatureThe part that turns: coil + soft-iron core + axle.
Split-ring commutatorTwo half-rings. They reverse the current in the coil every half turn.
BrushesFixed carbon blocks that carry electricity into the spinning coil.

Energy: a DC motor changes electrical energy into rotational kinetic energy.

Why must a motor have a commutator?

  • Without it: when the coil passes the vertical position (plane ⊥ B), the torque reverses and pulls the coil back. So the coil rocks a few times, then stops with its plane ⊥ B.
  • With it: each time the coil reaches plane ⊥ B, the current reverses. So the torque always turns the coil one way, and the coil keeps turning. At the moment of the swap the brushes touch the gaps, and rotational inertia carries the coil on.
  • Dead spot: if the coil stops exactly at plane ⊥ B, torque = 0 and it cannot start by itself. Real motors use many coils at different angles, so the torque is smooth.
  • Radial magnetic field: the ends of the magnets are curved (these are called pole pieces), and a soft-iron core sits in the middle. The field lines point straight out from the centre, like the spokes of a wheel. So B always lies along the plane of the coil, θ is always 90°, and the torque stays at its maximum nIAB for almost the whole turn. Note: the maximum is not bigger. The average is.
5.2

Back emf, maximum speed and energy

Big ideaOnce a motor is spinning, it also acts as a small generator. It makes a voltage that "pushes back". This is called back emf.

  • Why it exists: a coil spinning in a field has a changing flux, so an emf is induced (Faraday). By Lenz's law, this emf opposes the supply.
  • The faster the motor spins, the bigger the back emf (back emf ∝ speed). AC and DC motors both have it.
  • What really pushes the current is the difference between the two: V − εback. This is called the net emf.

The three stages of motor current

  1. Switch-on: the coil is not turning yet, so back emf = 0. So I = V/R: the current is largest, and the torque is largest too.
  2. Speeding up: the back emf grows, so the current and torque fall.
  3. Maximum speed: the torque just balances friction and the load. The back emf is a little less than V, so a small current still flows. In an ideal motor (no friction, no load): back emf ≈ V, current ≈ 0.
  • Adding a load: the motor slows, the back emf falls, the current rises and the torque rises. The motor "pushes harder" by itself.
  • A motor that stalls: it is switched on but cannot turn. The back emf = 0, so the current stays large. The I²R heating can melt the insulation (burn out). So large motors have a starting resistor in series, which limits the start-up current.

When to use: a running DC motor. At switch-on or when stalled, εback = 0, so I = V/R.
You can also derive it: use V = IR from the sheet, and replace V with the net emf: V − εback = IR. Multiply both sides by I: VI = εbackI + I²R.
Example (illustrative numbers): V = 12 V, R = 2 Ω. At switch-on: I = 12/2 = 6 A. Once it is running with back emf = 10 V: I = (12 − 10)/2 = 1 A.
Trap: the back emf is 0 at switch-on and largest at maximum speed. Do not mix them up.

Energy in a motorNot on the sheet: learn it
VI = εbackI + I²R

Power in = mechanical power (useful) + heat in the coil. When stalled, back emf = 0, so all of the input energy becomes heat. So back emf is how the motor "does work".

Lenz's law is really conservation of energy

Imagine the induced emf helped the supply push. The faster the motor spins, the bigger the current and torque, so it spins even faster, and it speeds up without limit. Energy would be made from nothing, and this is impossible. So the induced emf must "push back".

5.3

Generators: AC and DC

Big ideaAn outside force turns the coil. The flux keeps changing, so an emf is produced. Kinetic energy becomes electrical energy.

  • As the coil turns, θ changes, so Φ = BA cos θ changes and an emf is produced (Faraday: ε = −NΔΦ/Δt).
  • The emf depends on how fast Φ changes (the gradient of the Φ–t graph), not on how big Φ is. This is the easiest place in the chapter to make a mistake.
Coil positionΦ (flux)ε (emf)
plane ⊥ Blargest (BA)0: Φ is not changing at this instant. The wires move along the field lines and do not "cut" them.
plane ∥ B0largest: Φ is changing fastest. The wires "cut" straight across the field lines.
AC generator

Uses slip rings (two full rings, each always joined to the same end of the coil). The coil's emf reverses every half turn, and the output reverses with it. So the output is alternating current (AC).

DC generator

Uses a split-ring commutator. It swaps the connections every half turn (just when ε = 0). The output goes one way only, but in pulses. It is direct current (DC).

What changes the output?

  • Turning twice as fast: peak emf × 2, frequency × 2 (the period halves).
  • More turns, a stronger field, a bigger coil: only the peak gets bigger. The frequency does not change.
  • Turning the other way: the graph flips upside down.

Energy: when a device is connected, a current flows in the coil. This current is in a field, so it feels a force (motor effect). By Lenz's law this force opposes the turning. The bigger the current in the coil, the harder the coil is to turn. It is like a bike light run by a generator: when the light comes on, your legs get more tired.

5.4

AC induction motors

Big ideaThe stator makes a rotating magnetic field. The field drags the rotor round, and the rotor is always a little slower.

The current in the rotor does not come in through wires. It is induced. So an induction motor has no brushes and no commutator.

  1. Rotating field: the stator has 3 pairs of coils connected to three-phase AC (three alternating currents, a third of a cycle apart, that reach their maximum one after another). Together they make a field whose strength stays fixed and whose direction keeps turning. This is called a rotating magnetic field. Note: the coils themselves do not move at all.
  2. Induced current: the rotor is a squirrel cage: metal bars joined at both ends by metal rings. As the field turns past, the flux through each loop changes, so currents are induced (Faraday).
  3. Force: the bars carry current in a field, so they feel a force (F = lIB). By Lenz's law the force opposes the "relative motion", so the rotor is dragged round after the field.
  • Slip: the rotor always turns slower than the field. If it caught up, there would be no relative motion between them, no flux change, no current and no force, so the rotor would slow down again.
  • A bigger load gives more slip, so more induced current and more torque.
  • Advantages: no brushes or commutator, so little wear and no sparking. Disadvantage: the supply frequency sets the speed, so it is hard to change.
  • Analogy: walking a dog. The lead (the field) goes ahead, and the dog (the rotor) is pulled along, but it is always a little behind. If the dog walks beside you, the lead goes loose and there is no pull.
5.4

Magnetic braking

Big ideaWhen metal and a magnetic field move past each other, eddy currents are produced in the metal. The force from the eddy currents opposes the relative motion. So the object slows, and its kinetic energy becomes heat.

  • Eddy currents: induced currents that flow in loops inside a piece of metal.
  • Use Lenz's law for the direction: when metal enters the field the flux increases, and when it leaves the flux decreases. So the eddy currents on the two sides go opposite ways. But the forces on both sides oppose the motion.
  • Example: a metal disc turns through a field into the page (×). Where metal enters the field the flux increases, and the eddy current is anticlockwise. Where metal leaves the field the eddy current is clockwise. In the field these two currents flow the same way, and the force on them is opposite to v. So the disc slows.
  • Energy: kinetic → electrical → heat in the metal.
  • The higher the speed, the bigger the braking force. At zero speed the flux does not change and there is no force. So a magnetic brake cannot hold an object still. Friction brakes finish the stop.
  • A stronger field or a better conductor brakes more strongly. Slots in the metal break up the eddy-current loops and weaken the braking.
  • No contact, so no wear. Roller coasters, trains and drop towers all use it.
  • Regenerative braking: when an electric car brakes, its motor acts as a generator and charges the battery. By Lenz's law this also slows the car, and the energy is not wasted.

5.5

Key experiments

Build a simple DC motor yourself

Aim
To find the job of each part of a DC motor, and the effect of back emf.
Method
Wind a coil of enamelled wire (wire with a thin insulating coat) and rest it on two paper clips, with a magnet below. Scrape the coat off all round one end, but off only the top half of the other end.
Results
The coil keeps spinning only when one end is half scraped.
Why
The half-scraped end switches the current off for the half turn when the torque would reverse. So the torque always acts one way, just like a commutator. The paper clips act as brushes.
back emf
Lab motor: when it is held still by hand for 1–2 seconds, the current is 1.5 A. When it runs, the current is only 0.30 A. The current is smaller when the motor runs, and this is the evidence for back emf.
Significance
It shows every part of a DC motor, and back emf, in a few minutes.

Falling magnets and swinging plates

Method
A strong magnet falls through a PVC tube, a slotted copper tube and a complete copper tube. An aluminium or copper plate swings between magnet poles, and then a slotted plate is used instead.
Results
Fall times: PVC 0.41 s, slotted copper tube 0.56 s, copper tube 3.57 s. The solid plate stops quickly, and the slotted plate swings longer.
Conclusion
Eddy currents oppose the motion (Lenz), and the lost energy becomes heat. Slots cut the eddy currents, so the braking is weaker.

Memorise these 7 before the exam

  1. τ = nIAB sin θ, with θ from the normal. The torque is largest when the plane of the coil ∥ B.
  2. The split-ring commutator reverses the current every half turn, so the torque always turns the coil one way.
  3. Back emf opposes the supply and grows with speed: I = (V − εback)/R (not on the sheet).
  4. Generator: the emf is largest when the plane of the coil ∥ B. Slip rings: AC. Split ring: DC.
  5. Induction motor: a rotating field drags the cage rotor round, a little slower (slip).
  6. Magnetic brake: eddy currents oppose the motion. There is no force at zero speed.
  7. Lenz's law is conservation of energy: if induced currents helped the change, energy would be made from nothing.

Common mistakes

Wrong: θ is the angle between B and the plane of the coil.
Right: θ is the angle between B and the normal.
Wrong: the back emf is largest when a motor is switched on.
Right: at switch-on the back emf = 0 and the current is largest.
Wrong: when a generator's Φ is largest, the emf is also largest.
Right: when Φ is largest, emf = 0. When Φ = 0, the emf is largest.
Wrong: a DC motor uses slip rings.
Right: DC motors and DC generators use a split ring. AC generators use slip rings.
Wrong: a magnetic brake can stop a car completely.
Right: the lower the speed, the smaller the force. Friction brakes finish the stop.
Wrong: explaining Lenz's law by saying only "it opposes".
Right: also say why: conservation of energy.

Check

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. When the plane of the coil is parallel to the field, is the torque largest or smallest?

Largest. Here θ (between B and the normal) = 90°, sin 90° = 1, and the lever arm is longest.

2. What happens to a DC motor with no split-ring commutator?

The coil rocks a few times, then stops with its plane ⊥ B. Once the coil passes plane ⊥ B, the torque reverses and pulls the coil back.

3. Why is the current largest when a motor is first switched on?

It is not turning yet, so back emf = 0. So I = V/R, and only the small resistance of the coil limits the current.

4. V = 12 V, R = 2 Ω, back emf = 10 V. What is the current? What about when it is stalled?

Running: I = (12 − 10)/2 = 1 A. Stalled: back emf = 0, I = 12/2 = 6 A.

5. When the plane of a generator's coil is ⊥ B, what is the emf? Why?

0. Φ is largest here, but at this instant Φ is not changing (its rate of change is 0).

6. Can the rotor of an induction motor turn as fast as the field?

No. At the same speed there is no relative motion, the flux does not change, there is no induced current, and so there is no force.

7. Why does a magnet fall slowly in a copper tube? Where does the lost energy go?

Eddy currents are produced in the tube. By Lenz's law they oppose the motion of the magnet. The lost energy becomes heat in the tube.
