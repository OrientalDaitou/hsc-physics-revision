Electromagnetic Induction: Flux, Faraday and Lenz

Explained from zero

This chapter shows how to make electricity from a magnetic field. The most important idea: when the flux through a coil changes, an emf is produced. You will learn three things: how much flux there is, how big the emf is, and which way the current flows. The moving wire, the magnet and the two coils later in the chapter all follow the same rules.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Field lines and B: field lines come out of the N pole and go into the S pole. The closer together the lines are, the stronger the field is. The unit of B is T (tesla).
  2. Right-hand grip rule: hold the coil in your right hand, with your fingers curling along the current. Your thumb points the way of the magnetic field inside the coil.
  3. Right-hand palm rule: hold your right hand flat. Fingers = field B, thumb = velocity v of a positive charge (or current I), the way the palm pushes = force F.
  4. Three cos values: cos 0° = 1, cos 90° = 0, cos 180° = −1.
3.1

Magnetic flux

Big ideaMagnetic flux tells you how much magnetic field passes through a loop. It depends on three things: the field strength, the area and the angle.

  • Magnetic flux (Φ): how much magnetic field passes through an area. You can think of it as "how many field lines pass through the loop". Unit: weber (Wb).
  • Plane: the flat surface inside a loop, like the skin of a drum.
  • Normal: an imaginary arrow at 90° to the plane of the loop.
  • θ is the angle between B and the normal, not the angle between B and the plane. If a question gives "the angle to the plane", use θ = 90° − that angle.
  • Only the part of the field along the normal passes through: B∥ = B cos θ.
  • Analogy: catching rain in a bucket. With the open top facing up, you catch the most rain. With the bucket tilted, you catch less. With the open top facing sideways, you catch none.
θ = 0°

plane ⊥ B. All the field lines pass through. Φ = BA, the largest.

Tilted

Only some of them pass through. Φ = BA cos θ.

θ = 90°

plane ∥ B. The field lines slide along the loop and none pass through. Φ = 0.

  • 1 Wb = 1 T m². With B perpendicular to the plane, B = Φ/A, the flux per square metre. So B is also called the magnetic flux density.
  • Reversing the field (or turning the loop over) takes the flux from +BA to −BA. So |ΔΦ| = 2BA, not 0.

3.2

Faraday's law

Big ideaOnly a changing flux induces an emf. A steady flux induces nothing, however big it is.

  • Electromagnetic induction: making an emf by changing flux. To induce an emf means to produce it.
  • emf (ε, electromotive force): the name has "force" in it, but it is not a force. It is the energy given to each coulomb of charge. Unit: volt (V).
  • A faster magnet, a stronger magnet or more turns give a bigger emf.
  • Faraday's law: the induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the circuit.
  • In a coil with N turns, the emfs of the turns add, like cells in a battery.
  • Closed circuit: an induced current flows, I = ε/R. Open circuit: an emf, but no current.
  • Analogy: flux is like the balance in a bank account, and emf is like the money going in or out each day. However big the balance is, if no money moves, the money going in or out is 0.

Reading the emf from a Φ–t graph

  • |ε| = N × gradient. Gradient is how steep the line is.
  • Steep line: big emf. Flat line: emf = 0.
  • The rising and falling parts of the graph give emfs of opposite signs.
  • In Animation 4 in 3.5, the Φ–t graph and the ε–t graph are lined up one above the other, so you can see this directly.

3.3

Lenz's law: the direction of the current

Big ideaAn induced current always fights the change that made it. Lenz's law (learn the exact words): the induced current flows in the direction that makes a magnetic field opposing the change in flux that produced it.

In the exam you must write this sentence (the booklet's own words, learn it):
"The induced current flows in the direction that makes a magnetic field opposing the change in flux that produced it."
Key words: induced current, magnetic field, opposing the change in flux.

  • Lenz's law is the minus sign in Faraday's formula. It comes from conservation of energy (covered at the end of this section).
  • In an open circuit there is no current. The direction of the emf is then the direction of the current it "wants" to drive.
  • The induced current also makes its own magnetic field. Below we call it induced B.
  • It opposes the change, not the field itself. Analogy: a person who hates change. If you push something in, they push it out. If you take something out, they pull it back.
Flux increasing

Induced B points opposite to the original B, trying to push the flux back down.

Flux decreasing

Induced B points the same way as the original B, trying to bring the flux back up.

  • Right-hand grip rule: fingers curl with the current, thumb = the field inside the coil. It also works the other way: if the current looks anticlockwise to you, that face is an N face. If it looks clockwise, it is an S face.

Three steps to find the current direction

  1. Which way does the original B point? Is the flux growing or shrinking?
  2. Use Lenz's law to find which way induced B points.
  3. Use the grip rule to find the current direction from induced B.

Note: when you say "clockwise" or "anticlockwise", you must say which side it is seen from (for example, "seen from the magnet"). If you do not say which side, the answer has no meaning.

A faster way: the pole method

  • Pole method: when a pole approaches, the near face of the coil becomes the same pole and repels it. When a pole leaves, the near face becomes the opposite pole and attracts it.
  • In both cases, the coil opposes the motion of the magnet.
N pole approaching

The near face becomes N and repels the magnet. Induced B points towards the magnet. Seen from the magnet: anticlockwise.

N pole leaving

The near face becomes S and attracts the magnet. Induced B points away from the magnet. Seen from the magnet: clockwise.

Lenz's law and conservation of energy

  • Suppose the coil attracted an approaching magnet. The magnet would speed up and gain energy from nothing. That breaks the law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed.
  • So the coil must push back. The work you do against this push → electrical energy → heat.
  • Open circuit: no current, so no push back.
3.4

Motional emf: a straight wire moving in a field

Big ideaA metal rod moving across a magnetic field has an emf between its ends.

A rod alone

  • Use the palm rule: fingers = B, thumb = v of a positive charge, the way the palm pushes = force.
  • Rod moving right, B into the page (×): positive charge is pushed up. Free electrons (electrons that can move in the metal) are pushed down.
  • So the top end becomes + and the bottom end becomes −, like a battery. This is called motional emf.
  • As more charge builds up, it makes an electric field that pushes back. When the electric and magnetic forces are the same size, the charge stops building up.

On rails

  • Put the rod on two rails, and join a resistor R to the left end of the rails. This makes a closed circuit (as in Animation 3). When the rod moves right, away from R, the current flows up the rod, and it goes anticlockwise around the whole loop (seen from the front of the page).
  • The rod carries a current and is in a field, so it feels a force F = lIB. This force opposes the motion (Lenz).
  • So you must keep pushing for the rod to move at constant speed. The work done by the push ends up as heat in R.
  • Faraday's view: as the rod moves forward, it sweeps out area, so the flux through the loop grows, and there is an emf. Both views give the same answer.
  • Rod moving along the field lines, or sliding along its own length: it does not cut across the field lines, so ε = 0.
Induction (this chapter)

Motion → current. A wire moving in a field produces a current.

Motor effect

Current → motion. Motor effect: a wire carrying a current in a magnetic field is pushed.

3.5

A magnet moving through a solenoid · Metal plates: eddy currents

Big ideaAny change in flux induces an emf, whatever causes it. Magnets in a solenoid, metal plates and nearby coils all follow the same rules.

Solenoid: a long coil with many turns.

A magnet moving through a solenoid

  1. Approaching: the flux increases, so an emf is induced one way. This is pulse 1.
  2. Centre: the flux is largest, but it is not changing at this moment, so ε = 0.
  3. Leaving: the flux decreases, so the emf is the other way. This is pulse 2.
  • The faster the magnet, the taller and narrower the pulse.
  • A falling magnet leaves faster than it enters. So pulse 2 is taller and narrower.
  • The two pulses have the same area, N|ΔΦ|. Faster means taller, but the time is also shorter.
  • Closed circuit: the coil repels the magnet above it and attracts it below. Both forces point up, and both oppose the fall. Some gravitational potential energy becomes heat, so the magnet falls slower.

Metal plates: eddy currents

  • Eddy currents: loops of induced current inside solid metal. They appear only where the flux changes. Lenz's law gives their direction.
  • Below, a metal plate moves right through a field into the page (×), seen from the front of the page:
The part entering the field

Flux increases → anticlockwise eddy current.

The part leaving the field

Flux decreases → clockwise eddy current.

The part fully inside the field

Flux does not change → no eddy current.

  • The force on each eddy loop opposes the motion. The plate slows, and its kinetic energy becomes heat. This is called magnetic braking.
  • Uses: brakes, induction cooktops, metal detectors.
  • Problem: there are also eddy currents in the core of a transformer, and they waste energy (next chapter).
  • To see a magnet falling into a copper tube and being slowed by eddy currents, go to Animation 5 of Topic 5.
3.5

One solenoid near another

Big ideaWhen the current in one coil changes, there is an emf in a nearby coil. If the current does not change, there is no emf.

  • Mutual induction: a changing current in the primary coil (joined to the supply) induces an emf in a nearby secondary coil.
  • A coil with a steady current, moved in or out of another coil, acts like a bar magnet.

Closing and opening the switch

  1. Switch closed: the primary current grows from 0, and there is a brief emf in the secondary. The secondary's induced B opposes the growing field.
  2. Kept closed: the current is steady, the flux is steady, and there is no emf.
  3. Switch opened: the current falls, and there is another brief emf, the other way.
  • Analogy: riding in a lift. You feel it only when it starts and when it stops. You feel nothing while it moves at constant speed.
  • A soft iron core is easily magnetised. It guides more flux into the secondary, so the emf is bigger.
  • AC (current keeps changing size and direction) supply: the current keeps changing, so there is an emf all the time. This is a transformer (next chapter). A steady DC supply: an emf only at the moment of switching on or off.
  • Where does the secondary's energy come from? From the supply. If the emf is made by moving a coil, it comes from the work you do.

A coil wound on a long solenoid

  • A long air-core solenoid is the primary, and a coil wound around the outside of it is the secondary. All the primary's flux passes through the secondary.
  • Flux through each turn of the secondary: Φ = BA, with B = μ0NpIp/L.
  • Faraday's law then gives the formula below. The faster Ip changes, the bigger the emf.

3.6

Key experiments

Faraday's induction experiments (1831)

Aim
To show that a changing flux induces an emf.
Method
① A magnet was pushed into and pulled out of a coil joined to a galvanometer (a meter for very small currents). ② A wire was moved across a magnet's field. ③ On two coils sharing an iron ring, a switch was closed and opened.
Results
The needle flicked only while something changed. Opposite changes gave opposite flicks. Faster changes gave bigger flicks.
Conclusion
An emf is induced only while the flux changes. Only relative motion matters.
Significance
With Ørsted's work (1820), it linked electricity and magnetism.

Eddy currents: falling magnets and swinging plates

Aim
To show that eddy currents oppose the motion that causes them.
Method
A magnet fell through PVC, copper and slotted copper tubes. Solid and slotted metal plates swung between magnet poles.
Results
Fall times: PVC 0.41 s (free fall), slotted copper 0.56 s, copper 3.57 s. The solid plate stopped fast; the slotted one kept swinging.
Conclusion
Eddy currents in the copper oppose the fall (Lenz). The lost gravitational potential energy becomes heat. Slots break the current loops, so braking is weaker.
Significance
Evidence for Lenz's law and energy conservation. Magnetic brakes use this idea.

You can see these fall times in Animation 5 of Topic 5.

Check

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. The plane of a loop is parallel to the field. What is θ? What is Φ?

θ = 90°, Φ = 0. θ is the angle between B and the normal. When plane ∥ B, normal ⊥ B and cos 90° = 0, so no field lines pass through.

2. A loop has an area of 200 cm². B = 0.5 T, and the plane of the loop is at 30° to B. What is Φ?

Φ = 0.005 Wb (5 × 10−3 Wb). First change the unit: A = 200 × 10−4 = 0.02 m². Then change the angle: θ = 90° − 30° = 60°. Φ = 0.5 × 0.02 × cos 60° = 0.005 Wb.

3. A very strong magnet sits inside a coil and does not move. Does the galvanometer show a reading?

No. The flux is large but does not change, so ΔΦ = 0 and ε = 0. Only a changing flux induces an emf.

4. A coil of 100 turns with an area of 0.02 m² faces directly into a field of B = 0.5 T. The field reverses in 0.1 s. What is the average emf? The circuit resistance is 4 Ω. What is the current?

ε = 20 V, I = 5 A. The flux goes from +BA to −BA: |ΔΦ| = 2BA = 2 × 0.5 × 0.02 = 0.02 Wb (not 0). |ε| = N|ΔΦ|/Δt = 100 × 0.02 / 0.1 = 20 V. I = ε/R = 20/4 = 5 A.

5. The S pole of a bar magnet moves away from a coil. What pole does the near face of the coil become? Does it push or pull?

The near face becomes N, and it pulls the magnet. Pole method: when a pole leaves, the near face becomes the opposite pole. They attract, which opposes the magnet leaving.

6. A rod on rails: l = 0.50 m, B = 0.80 T, v = 2.0 m s−1, R = 2.0 Ω. Find ε, I and the force on the rod.

ε = 0.80 V, I = 0.40 A, F = 0.16 N, opposite to the motion. ε = lvB = 0.50 × 2.0 × 0.80 = 0.80 V. I = ε/R = 0.80/2.0 = 0.40 A. F = lIB = 0.50 × 0.40 × 0.80 = 0.16 N. By Lenz, this force opposes the motion, so you must keep pushing.

7. A magnet falls down through a coil. Why is the second pulse taller and narrower? What about the areas of the two pulses?

The magnet is faster when leaving; the areas are the same. The magnet keeps speeding up as it falls, so it leaves faster than it enters. The flux changes faster, so the emf is bigger and the time is shorter. Both pulses have an area equal to N|ΔΦ|.

8. A long air-core solenoid: Np = 1000, L = 0.50 m, A = 4.0 × 10−4 m², with Ns = 50 turns wound around the outside. Ip rises from 0 to 2.0 A in 0.010 s. What is εs? What is it after that, if Ip stays at 2.0 A?

About 0.010 V; then 0. At 2.0 A, B = μ0NpIp/L = 4π × 10−7 × 1000 × 2.0 / 0.50 = 5.03 × 10−3 T. For each turn, Φ = BA = 5.03 × 10−3 × 4.0 × 10−4 = 2.01 × 10−6 Wb. |εs| = NsΔΦ/Δt = 50 × 2.01 × 10−6 / 0.010 = 1.0 × 10−2 V. Once the current is steady, the flux does not change, so emf = 0.
