Charged Particles in Electric and Magnetic Fields

Explained from zero

This chapter is about how charged particles move in electric and magnetic fields. The force from an electric field keeps the same direction, so the path is the same as a projectile's. The force from a magnetic field is always perpendicular to the velocity, so a charge that enters at 90° moves in a circle. Remember the three rules below, and everything after that is simple.

Rule ①
Electric force

The force from an electric field on a charge has a fixed direction: a positive charge is pushed along E, and an electron is pushed against E. The force is constant, so the acceleration is constant too.

F⃗ = qE⃗
Rule ②
Work done by the field

The field pushes the charge along, and all the work it does becomes kinetic energy. When the charge goes from one plate to the other (across the whole plate gap), the work does not depend on the plate gap d.

W = qV
Rule ③
Magnetic force

A magnetic force pushes only on a moving charge. The force is always perpendicular to v and B. It does no work. It changes only the direction, not the speed. So a charge that enters a uniform magnetic field at 90° moves in a circle.

Order for revising with students

  1. First spend 5 minutes on "Know these 4 first" below, especially projectile motion and uniform circular motion. This chapter uses them all the time.
  2. Teach in the order 1.1 → 1.4. Compare the electric field part with projectiles all the way through, and the magnetic field part with circular motion. For each section, read the "Big idea" first, then open the animation and let students say what they see.
  3. Afterwards, have students close the book and fill in the comparison table in 1.3 by themselves. Finish with the "Quick quiz", then look at "Common mistakes".
Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Projectile motion (Module 5): for a ball thrown horizontally, the horizontal velocity stays the same. In the vertical direction the acceleration g is constant, so the path is a parabola. It uses the constant acceleration equations: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as.
  2. Splitting and joining vectors: when a velocity u is at an angle θ to the horizontal, ux = u cos θ, uy = u sin θ. Joining the two components: v = √(vx² + vy²).
  3. Force, work and kinetic energy: F⃗net = ma⃗, so the acceleration points the same way as the force. Work W = Fs cos θ: when the force is perpendicular to the displacement, W = 0. Kinetic energy K = ½mv².
  4. Uniform circular motion: moving in a circle at a constant speed. The velocity is along the tangent, and the net force points to the centre: Fc = mv²/r, v = 2πr/T.
1.1

Charges in a uniform electric field

Big ideaTwo charged parallel plates make a uniform electric field. A charge between them feels a constant force, so it has a constant acceleration.

  • Join two parallel metal plates to a battery, with voltage V. The top plate is +, the bottom plate is −, and the plates are a distance d apart.
  • Between the plates there is an electric field: a region where a charge feels a force. Its direction is the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Electric field strength E: the force per coulomb. Its unit is V m−1, which is the same as N C−1.
  • Field lines go from + to −. Between the plates they are straight, parallel and evenly spaced. This is a uniform field: a field with the same strength and the same direction everywhere.
  • At the ends of the plates, the field lines bulge out a little. This is called the edge effect. Calculations ignore it.
+ charge

Pushed along E: from the + plate towards the − plate.

Electron (−)

Pushed against E: from the − plate towards the + plate.

  • Elementary charge: e = 1.602 × 10−19 C, the size of the charge on one proton or one electron. The formula sheet prints qe as negative. In calculations, use the size and find the direction separately.
  • Released from rest, a charge moves in a straight line, faster and faster.
  • The force is constant, so the acceleration is constant too: a = qE/m = qV/(md). This combines the three equations F⃗ = qE⃗, F⃗net = ma⃗, E = V/d.
  • A bigger charge-to-mass ratio q/m gives a bigger a.
  • Ignore gravity for electrons, protons and ions (charged atoms). In a field of only 100 V m−1, the electric force on an electron is about 2 × 10¹² times its weight. Gravity is too small to notice.
  • The field also does work on the charge: W = qV. It is listed in the card below first, and the second half of 1.1 explains it in detail.

Oil drops: when gravity counts

An oil drop is much heavier than an electron, so gravity cannot be ignored. A charged oil drop is held still between the plates: electric force up = weight down, qE = mg (not on the sheet). Its charge is q = ne, where n is a whole number. This is Millikan's oil drop experiment, which Module 8 explains.

1.1

Work, energy and the electron gun

Big ideaThe field pushes the charge along, so it does work W = qV on the charge. The charge loses electric potential energy and gains kinetic energy.

  • The field does work on the charge, which means it transfers energy. The charge loses electric potential energy and gains kinetic energy.
  • It is like a ball rolling downhill: its height (potential energy) goes down, and its speed (kinetic energy) goes up.
  • Voltage means "the change in energy per coulomb of charge": V = ΔU/q (on the sheet). So the work done by the field is W = qV.
  • Across the whole plate gap, the plate gap does not matter. With closer plates, E = V/d is bigger, so they push harder, but for a shorter distance. The two effects cancel, so W = qEd = qV stays the same.
    Over only part of the gap, it is different. If the charge moves only a fixed distance s along E, the work is W = qEs. With the voltage unchanged and the plates further apart, E is smaller, so W is also smaller.

Electron volt: an energy unit for tiny particles

  • Electron volt (eV): the energy one electron (or one proton) gains through 1 V. 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J.
  • Working out eV is simple: energy in eV = (charge in units of e) × (voltage in V).
  • Example: an electron through 100 V gains 100 eV. A particle with charge +2e through 100 V gains 200 eV.

Electron gun

  1. A hot cathode (the negative plate) gives off electrons.
  2. The field speeds the electrons up from rest, all the way across.
  3. The electrons leave through a small hole in the positive plate as a beam.

An electron gun uses the card below: all the work done by the field becomes the electrons' kinetic energy.

1.2

Curved paths in an electric field

Big ideaA charge that enters moving parallel to the plates follows a parabola, like a ball thrown horizontally. Its velocity along the plates stays the same; across the plates it has a constant acceleration.

Imagine an electron that enters between the two plates from the left at speed u, moving parallel to the plates. The positive plate is on top and the negative plate is below. The plate length is L and the plate gap is d.

  • The electron's trajectory (the path it follows): along the plates there is no force, so vx = u is constant and x = ut.
  • Across the plates: a = qE/m is constant. At the start uy = 0, so y = ½at².
  • The path bends towards the plate that attracts the charge. The electron is negative, so it bends towards the positive plate.
  • After the plates there is no force, so the path is a straight line.

Why a parabola

Put t = x/u into y = ½at²: y = [qE/(2mu²)] x². So y ∝ x², which is a parabola. A ball thrown horizontally has the same shape, with qE/m in place of g.

How far it is deflected

  • Deflection: how far the particle is pushed sideways.
  • For plates of length L: the time between the plates is t = L/u, and at the exit y = qEL²/(2mu²).
  • L is the plate length, not the plate gap d.
  • A faster particle spends less time between the plates, so it is deflected less.
  • Exit velocity: vy = at, v = √(vx² + vy²), direction θ = tan−1(vy/vx).
  • Entering at an angle: first split u into u cos θ and u sin θ, then work out each direction separately.
Like a projectile

Both are parabolas: a constant force in one direction, and no force at right angles to it. The same equations are used in each direction.

Unlike a projectile

a = qE/m depends on charge and mass, whereas g is the same for every mass. The force can point either way (sign of q). For electrons, a is huge.

1.3

The magnetic force

Big ideaA magnetic field pushes only on a moving charge. The force is at right angles to both the velocity v and the field B.

  • Magnetic field: a region where a magnet or a moving charge feels a magnetic force.
  • B shows how strong the field is. It is called the magnetic field strength. Its unit is the tesla (T).
  • A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
  • Only the part of v that is perpendicular (at 90°) to B counts: v⟂ = v sin θ. Moving along B: no force.
  • The force is perpendicular to both v and B.
  • How it is drawn on paper: • • • = B out of the page (like looking at the tip of an arrow); × × × = B into the page (like looking at the tail of an arrow).

Direction: the right-hand palm rule

  • Right-hand palm rule (gives the same result as the left-hand rule): hold your right hand flat. Fingers = B, thumb = v of a positive charge, and the palm faces F.
  • For a negative charge (such as an electron): work it out as a positive charge first, then reverse the force.
  • Example 1: fingers up (B up), thumb to the right (v to the right), palm faces you: F is out of the page.
  • Example 2: B into the page, v to the right: the force on a positive charge is up (towards the top of the page), and the force on an electron is down.
1.3

Circular paths

Big ideaThe magnetic force changes only the direction, not the speed. So a charge that enters a uniform magnetic field at 90° moves in a circle.

The acceleration a = F/m is along F, so it is perpendicular to the field. Why is the path a circle? There are three steps:

  1. F is always at 90° to v, so W = Fs cos 90° = 0: the magnetic force does no work.
  2. No work, so the speed is constant. With constant speed, F = qvB keeps the same size.
  3. A force of constant size, always at 90° to v, is a centripetal force (the net force towards the centre). So the charge moves in uniform circular motion.
+ charge, B into the page

Goes anticlockwise. v is along the tangent, and F points to the centre.

Electron, B into the page

Goes clockwise. The force is reversed, so the direction of turning is also reversed.

How big the circle is and how fast it turns

  • Heavier or faster: a bigger circle (r ∝ v). More charge or a stronger B: a smaller circle.
  • T does not depend on the speed. At twice the speed, the circle is twice as big: the path is twice as long and the charge moves twice as fast, so the time for one turn stays the same.

Half a circle and the helix

  • Crossing the edge of a large field at 90°: half a circle, out through the same edge, with the exit 2r from the entry.
  • A mass spectrometer uses this to sort ions: with the same charge and speed, heavier ions land further away.
  • v at another angle to B: v⟂ makes it go in a circle, and v‖ (no force) carries it along B. The path is a helix, like a stretched spring.
1.3

Comparing electric, magnetic and gravitational fields

Big ideaIn a gravitational field and in an electric field the force has a fixed direction, so the path is a parabola. In a magnetic field the force turns with the velocity, so the path is a circle.

Below is Table 1.1 from the booklet: the same charge, compared in a uniform electric field and in a uniform magnetic field.

  • Like all uniform circular motion: a ball on a string (tension), a car going round a bend (friction), a satellite around the Earth (gravity). F is at 90° to v, so there is no work and the speed is constant.
  • Differences: qvB exists only because the charge moves.
  • For a charge, r ∝ v and T does not depend on the speed. This is why a cyclotron (a machine that speeds up particles in circles) works.
  • A satellite is different: in a bigger orbit it moves more slowly, and one turn takes longer.
1.3

Crossed fields: the velocity selector

Big ideaWhen the electric and magnetic fields are at right angles, the electric force and the magnetic force can cancel. Only a particle with exactly the speed v = E/B goes straight through.

  • Crossed fields: E and B in the same place, at 90° to each other.
  • Example: a positive charge moves to the right, E is down, and B is into the page. The electric force qE is down; the magnetic force qvB is up (palm rule, the same as Example 2 in 1.3).
  • When qE = qvB, the two forces cancel and the particle moves in a straight line. Cancel q on both sides: v = E/B (not on the sheet).
  • Only this one speed goes straight through, so this is called a velocity selector. Too fast: qvB is bigger, so the particle bends towards the side the magnetic force points to. Too slow: qE is bigger, so it bends towards the side the electric force points to.
  • v = E/B has no q or m in it: with an electron, both forces reverse and they still cancel.
  • Thomson used this when he measured the electron (Module 8 explains this).

1.4

Key experiments

Simulating charges between parallel plates

Aim
To test the path of a charge between the plates. Also to test the speed it gains from rest.
Method
A computer simulation.
(1) Deflection: an electron enters between the plates, moving parallel to them (this is the situation in 1.2). Change only V and record y; repeat with half the speed; repeat with a proton; record y at several x.
(2) Electron gun: record the exit speed v for several V, then double the plate gap.
Results
(1) A graph of y against V is a straight line through the origin. Half the speed gives 4 times the deflection. At the same entry speed, a proton is deflected the other way, and about 1840 times less (its mass is about 1840 times bigger). y/x² is the same at every x.
(2) v² against V is a straight line through the origin. Its gradient agrees with 2e/me = 3.52 × 1011 C kg−1. The plate gap makes no difference.
Conclusion
(1) So y ∝ V and y ∝ x²: a parabola, like a projectile, because the force is constant.
(2) v² = (2q/m)V is confirmed. Across the whole plate gap, W = qV does not depend on d.
Significance
The same physics steered the electron beam in cathode-ray oscilloscopes (old screens that drew voltage graphs). Electron guns, X-ray tubes and accelerators set particle energy with the voltage.
Safety
A real deflection tube uses a kV supply and can implode (break inwards). Teacher demonstration only.

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. Between plates, E = V/d, F = qE and a = qE/m are constant.
  2. W = qV = qEd. From rest: qV = ½mv².
  3. Between the plates: a projectile path (parabola), with qE/m in place of g. After the plates: a straight line.
  4. F = qvB sin θ acts only on moving charges, at 90° to both v and B (palm rule; reverse it for negative charges).
  5. At 90° to B: a circle. From qvB = mv²/r: r = mv/(qB), T = 2πm/(qB) (not on the sheet).
  6. An electric field can change the speed; a magnetic field does no work and changes only the direction.

Common mistakes

Wrong: Putting mm, cm or kV straight into a formula.
Right: Change to m and V first: 5 mm = 0.005 m, 2 kV = 2000 V.
Wrong: The force on an electron is in the same direction as E.
Right: The force on a negative charge is against E. On the sheet, qe is negative: use e for the size, and find the direction separately.
Wrong: v = √(qV/m), or forgetting the square root.
Right: ½mv² = qV, so v = √(2qV/m).
Wrong: The closer the plates, the more energy the charge gains.
Right: Across the whole plate gap, W = qV, which does not depend on d. Closer plates push harder, but for a shorter distance.
Wrong: W = qV never depends on d.
Right: Over only a fixed distance s, W = qEs (here the V in qV is the voltage Es across that distance, not the voltage between the plates). With V unchanged and the plate gap doubled, E = V/d halves, so W also halves.
Wrong: Using the plate gap for L in the deflection formula.
Right: L is the plate length; d is the plate gap.
Wrong: θ is the angle between v and the page (or the plate).
Right: In F = qvB sin θ, θ is measured from the field. With v in the page and B into or out of the page, θ = 90°.
Wrong: A magnetic force can make a charge go faster.
Right: F is always at 90° to v, so it does no work. The speed does not change; only the direction changes.
Wrong: r = mv/(qB) and T = 2πm/(qB) are on the formula sheet.
Right: Neither is. Start from qvB = mv²/r.
Wrong: The faster the charge, the longer one turn takes.
Right: T = 2πm/(qB), which does not depend on the speed. When it goes faster, the circle is also bigger.

1. Two plates are 5 mm apart, with a voltage of 50 V. What is E between the plates? How big is the force on an electron, and which way does it point?

E = 1.0 × 104 V m−1; F = 1.602 × 10−15 N, towards the positive plate. Change the units first: 5 mm = 0.005 m. E = V/d = 50/0.005 = 10 000 V m−1. F = qE = 1.602 × 10−19 × 10 000 = 1.602 × 10−15 N. The electron is negative, so the force is against E, which means towards the positive plate.

2. An alpha particle (charge +2e) is sped up from rest through 1000 V. How many eV does it gain? How many J?

2000 eV, which is 3.204 × 10−16 J. Energy in eV = (charge in units of e) × (voltage) = 2 × 1000 = 2000 eV. In J: W = qV = 2 × 1.602 × 10−19 × 1000 = 3.204 × 10−16 J.

3. An electron is sped up from rest through 100 V. What are its kinetic energy and speed? (electron mass 9.109 × 10−31 kg)

K = 1.602 × 10−17 J (100 eV), v ≈ 5.93 × 106 m s−1. ½mv² = qV = 1.602 × 10−19 × 100 = 1.602 × 10−17 J. v = √(2qV/m) = √(2 × 1.602 × 10−19 × 100 ÷ (9.109 × 10−31)) = √(3.52 × 1013) ≈ 5.93 × 106 m s−1. This is much less than the speed of light, so this formula can be used.

4. The voltage of an electron gun stays the same, and the plate gap is doubled. What happens to the exit speed of the electrons?

It does not change. E = V/d becomes half, so the force also becomes half, but the electron moves 2 times as far. W = qEd = qV stays the same, so ½mv² stays the same. Note: this is because the electron crosses the whole plate gap. If it moves only a fixed distance s, W = qEs. When the plates move apart, E gets smaller, so W also gets smaller.

5. An electron enters parallel to the plates, and its deflection at the exit is y. If the entry speed is halved, what does the deflection become? What about a proton at the same speed?

It becomes 4y. The proton is deflected the other way, about 1840 times less. y = qEL²/(2mu²): when u is halved, the time between the plates × 2 and u² becomes ¼, so y × 4. The proton is positive, so it is deflected towards the negative plate. Its mass is about 1840 times bigger, so a is about 1840 times smaller.

6. B is into the page, and a positive charge moves to the right. Which way is the magnetic force on it? What about an electron?

Positive charge: up (towards the top of the page). Electron: down. Palm rule: fingers into the page, thumb to the right, palm faces up. The electron is negative, so the force is reversed.

7. A charge moves in a circle in a uniform magnetic field. Its speed becomes 2 times as big. What happens to r and T? Will the field make it go faster?

r × 2, T does not change; the field does not change the speed. r = mv/(qB), so r ∝ v. T = 2πm/(qB) has no v in it: the path is twice as long and the charge moves twice as fast. The magnetic force is always at 90° to v and does no work, so the speed does not change.

8. In a velocity selector, E = 6000 V m−1 and B = 0.02 T. What speed of particle goes in a straight line? Which way does a faster positive charge bend? (particle moving right, E down, B into the page)

v = 3 × 105 m s−1; a faster one bends up. qE = qvB, v = E/B = 6000 ÷ 0.02 = 300 000 m s−1 = 3 × 105 m s−1. When it is faster, qvB is bigger than qE. The upward magnetic force wins, so it bends up.
