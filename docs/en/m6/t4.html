Transformers and Power Transmission

Explained from zero

This chapter covers three things: how a transformer makes an AC voltage bigger or smaller, why a real transformer gets hot, and why the grid sends electricity at a very high voltage. It uses three rules you have already learned. Once you know these three, the rest is just simple ratios.

Order for revising with students

  1. First spend 3 minutes on "Know these 4 first" below, especially number 1: if the flux does not change, there is no emf.
  2. Teach 4.1 → 4.3 in order. For each section, read the "Big idea" first, then open the animation and let the student say: does the voltage get bigger or smaller? What about the current? Where does the energy go?
  3. Finish with the "Quick quiz", then look at "Common mistakes".
Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. Magnetic flux and Faraday's law: the faster the flux changes, the bigger the emf. However big the flux is, if it does not change, the emf is 0.
  2. Lenz's law: an induced current always opposes the change in flux.
  3. Right-hand grip rule: point your thumb along the current, and your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field. The direction of the current in a coil decides the direction of the flux in the core.
  4. Electric power P = VI and Ohm's law V = IR: both are on the sheet.
4.1

The ideal transformer: how a transformer works

Big ideaA transformer can make an AC voltage bigger or smaller. A changing current in one coil makes a changing flux in an iron core, and this induces an emf in a second coil.

  • Transformer: a device that changes the size of an AC voltage.
  • AC (alternating current): current that keeps reversing. DC (direct current) flows one way only.
  • Primary coil: the input coil, joined to the supply. Secondary coil: the output coil, joined to the load (the device that uses the energy).
  • Mutual induction: an emf made in one coil by a changing current in a nearby coil.
  • Step-up: more secondary turns, so a bigger voltage. Step-down: fewer secondary turns, so a smaller voltage.
  • Both coils are wrapped around one closed core. The core is a ring of soft iron (iron that is easy to magnetise and demagnetise).

How the output voltage is made

  1. AC in the primary makes a magnetic field that keeps changing size and direction.
  2. The iron core carries this changing flux to the secondary.
  3. The flux through each secondary turn changes, so an emf is induced in every turn (Faraday's law, ε = −N ΔΦ/⁠Δt).
  4. The turns are in series, so their emfs add up. More turns, bigger voltage.
  5. The flux reverses with the supply, so the output is also AC, with the same frequency.

Note: no wire joins the two coils, and no charge crosses between them. The changing magnetic field carries the energy. The direction of the flux in the core reverses every half-cycle (in Animation 1 you can see the flux arrows reverse).

Why AC is needed

Steady DC makes a magnetic field that does not change, so ΔΦ/⁠Δt = 0: no emf. DC gives only a short pulse at the moment it is switched on or off (see Animation 5 of Topic 3).

Why V ∝ N

The same flux passes through every turn of both coils, so each turn has the same emf. So the voltage ratio = the turns ratio Np : Ns.

What the output looks like

  • The frequency stays the same. The peak (the largest voltage in each cycle) is multiplied by Ns/⁠Np.
  • Mains (the electricity from a power point at home): 230 V AC, 50 Hz (T = 1/⁠f = 20 ms).
  • 230 V is an effective value: a steady voltage (DC) of 230 V heats a heater by the same amount as this AC. The peak is about 325 V.
  • Never mix peak and effective values.

From the primary current graph to the secondary emf

The flux follows the current. So the secondary emf follows the gradient (how steep the graph is) of the current graph, not how big the current is. It is like a car: the steeper the distance graph, the faster the car. If the distance graph is flat, the car is stopped.

4.1

The ideal transformer: power and current

Big ideaAn ideal transformer wastes no energy, so power in equals power out. So voltage up means current down.

  • Ideal transformer: a transformer that wastes no energy at all. So power in = power out.
  • Power: the energy transferred each second, in W. The power of each coil is P = VI (on the sheet).
Electrical powerOn the formula sheet

Also often used the other way round: the current sent is I = P/⁠V. For the same power, the higher the voltage, the smaller the current. Section 4.3 uses this.

Put this together with the turns equation to get the ratio of the currents. The current ratio is upside down: step-down gives a bigger secondary current.

How to combine: divide both sides of VpIp = VsIs by VsIp to get Is/⁠Ip = Vp/⁠Vs. Then use the turns equation to change it to Np/⁠Ns.
Example: in the 600 turns → 1200 turns example above, voltage × 2, so current ÷ 2. When Is = 0.5 A, Ip = 12 × 0.5 /⁠ 6.0 = 1 A.

Step-up

Ns > Np. Voltage up, current down.

Step-down

Ns < Np. Voltage down, current up.

The load decides the current

  • The load takes the current it needs. The primary then automatically draws just enough current to supply that power.
  • It is like a restaurant: the kitchen cooks as much food as the customers order. If the customers order nothing, the kitchen cooks nothing. If the load is switched off, Is = 0, and the ideal transformer's Ip is also 0.
4.2

Real transformers: energy losses and efficiency

Big ideaReal transformers waste a little energy, mostly as heat. So the power out is a bit less than the power in. A closed iron core, thick wire and a laminated core keep the losses small.

  • Incomplete flux linkage: some flux from the primary misses the secondary.
  • Eddy currents: loops of current induced inside a solid piece of metal by a changing flux.
  • Laminated core: a core made of many thin iron sheets, with insulation between them.
  • Real transformer: VsIs < VpIp. The lost energy becomes heat (and a little sound).
  • The table below gives the three limitations in the syllabus.
LimitationCause and effectHow to reduce it
Incomplete flux linkage, also called flux leakageSome primary flux leaks into the air and misses the secondary, so the secondary emf is smaller than predicted.One closed loop of soft iron for both coils. Wind the secondary directly over the primary.
Resistive heating in the coilsThe current heats the long copper coils (I²R). Resistive heating is the heat made when a current flows through a resistance.Thick copper wire (thickest on the coil with the larger current). Cooling (vents, fans or oil) keeps the wire cool, and a cool wire has less resistance.
Eddy currents in the coreThe changing flux induces loops of current in the iron core, which heat it.A laminated core, or a ferrite core.

How good is the ideal model?

  • Big transformers in power stations and substations are about 99% efficient. Small ones often waste 10–20%.
  • So the ideal model is a very good approximation, but never exact.
  • Losses can be reduced, never removed.

4.2

Eddy currents and the laminated core

Big ideaThe core itself is a conductor. When the flux changes, eddy currents flow inside the core and heat it. If the core is made of many insulated thin sheets, the eddy currents are much smaller.

  • The core is a conductor, and the flux in it keeps changing. So eddy currents flow in it, in loops at right angles to the flux.
  • By Lenz's law they oppose the change in flux. They heat the core.
  • Laminated core: many thin iron sheets, each with a thin insulating layer.
  • The sheets lie along the flux, so the flux still passes easily.
  • The insulation cuts the eddy-current paths: each loop stays inside one sheet.

Cut a slice from the core and look at the cut face

The flux comes out of the page (⊙) and is growing. By Lenz's law, the magnetic field of the eddy currents must point into the page, to oppose this growth. So the eddy currents flow clockwise. AC reverses every half-cycle, so the eddy currents reverse every half-cycle too.

Solid core

One big eddy-current loop goes around the whole slice. Large current, a lot of heat.

Laminated core

Each sheet has only one small, long, thin loop. Small current, much less heat.

Why laminating works

  • Each thin loop surrounds only a thin strip of flux, so its emf is small.
  • Its path is narrow and long, so its resistance is high.
  • So the eddy currents and their heat are much smaller. It is like a big sports field divided by fences into many narrow tracks: each person can run only in their own narrow track and cannot run a big loop.
  • Small chargers use ferrite cores. Ferrite is a magnetic ceramic that conducts very poorly.
  • Note: cooling does not stop eddy currents. It only keeps the core a bit cooler.
4.3

Sending electricity at high voltage

Big ideaTransmission lines have resistance, so the current in them makes heat (I²R). Stepping the voltage up makes the current smaller, so much less energy is wasted.

  • Transmission line: a long, thick wire on tall towers that carries electricity far.
  • Voltage drop: the voltage used up pushing current through a resistance, equal to IR.
  • Substation: a place with transformers that change the voltage.

A simple model

  • The power station sends voltage V. The long line has several ohms of resistance R (both wires together).
  • The current I is the same all the way around the loop.
  • The voltage used up on the line is Vdrop = IR (Ohm's law). So the town gets only V − IR.
  • The power turned into heat in the line is Ploss = I²R.

Why high voltage

  1. A power station sends a fixed power. Since P = VI, a higher voltage needs a smaller current.
  2. The loss I²R depends on the square of the current: voltage × 10 → current becomes 1/10 → loss becomes 1/100. R stays the same.

Thick wires have less resistance, so they also reduce the loss. But they are heavy and expensive.

4.3

The grid

Big ideaOn the way from the power station to your home, electricity is first stepped up to a very high voltage at the power station. Then near towns it is stepped down in stages to 230 V.

Electricity grid: the network of power stations, lines and transformers. Typical NSW voltages are:

WhereVoltageTransformer
Power stationabout 20 kVstep-up
Transmission lines330 kV or 500 kV—
Substations132 kV or 66 kV → 11 kVstep-down
Power pole230 Vstep-down
Homes230 V—
  • Step up at the power station: a low current in the long lines, so little energy is wasted.
  • Step down near towns: high voltages are dangerous. They can spark (arc) across air and need tall towers and big insulators. Appliances at home are built for 230 V.
  • Why AC: transformers need a changing current: AC, not steady DC. (A few undersea links, such as Basslink, use DC.)

Other uses of transformers

DeviceTypeVoltage
Microwave ovenstep-up230 V → about 2 kV
Welderstep-down230 V → 50 V
Phone chargerstep-down230 V → 5 V

A welder has a very large current, which can melt metal. A phone charger uses a small ferrite core, then electronics make DC.

4.4

Key experiments

Testing the transformer equations

Aim
To test whether a real transformer follows Vp/⁠Vs = Np/⁠Ns, and to find its efficiency.
Method
Use a demountable transformer (one you can take apart): a laminated U-shaped iron core, an iron I-bar to close the top, and coils that slide on and off. Use low-voltage AC and AC meters. Primary 600 turns at 6.0 V. Change the secondary from 150 to 1200 turns, and measure Vs each time. With a load, read all four meters (Vp, Ip, Vs, Is). Finally, lift off the I-bar.
Results
The Vs–Ns graph is a straight line through the origin, about 5% below the ideal line. With the load, η ≈ 90%. Lifting off the I-bar (opening the core) cut Vs (300 turns) from 2.86 V to about 0.9 V.
Conclusion
Vs ∝ Ns, as predicted. The small gap is mainly flux leakage. With the I-bar off, the core is no longer a closed loop. A lot of flux leaks into the air and misses the secondary, so Vs is much smaller (this is flux leakage too). Pout < Pin (the lost power becomes heat). The ideal model is close, not exact.
Significance
It supports the model behind every grid transformer. It also shows why cores are closed loops.

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. A transformer needs AC: changing primary current → changing flux in the iron core → induced emf in the secondary (Faraday's law). Steady DC gives no output.
  2. Vp/⁠Vs = Np/⁠Ns: more secondary turns, bigger voltage. The frequency stays the same.
  3. Ideal: VpIp = VsIs, so voltage up means current down: Is/⁠Ip = Np/⁠Ns.
  4. Real losses: flux leakage, I²R heating in the coils, eddy currents in the core. Fixes: closed soft-iron core, thick copper wire, laminated core.
  5. Not on the sheet, so learn them: η = Pout/⁠Pin × 100% and Ploss = I²R.
  6. Fixed power: higher voltage → smaller current → much less I²R loss. Step up at the power station. For safety, step down in stages to 230 V.

Check

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. A transformer's primary is joined to a steady DC supply. Is there an output from the secondary?

No (only a short pulse at the moment it is switched on or off). Steady DC makes a magnetic field that does not change. The flux does not change, ΔΦ/Δt = 0, so there is no emf.

2. The primary carries sine-shaped AC. When the current is exactly at its highest point, what is the secondary emf?

0. The emf follows the gradient of the current graph. At the highest point the graph is flat, so the gradient = 0 and the flux is not changing at this moment. When the current crosses 0, the graph is steepest and the emf is biggest.

3. Experiment: primary 600 turns, 6.0 V, secondary 300 turns. What is the ideal Vs? If 2.86 V is measured, by what percentage is it too low?

Ideal 3.0 V; about 5% too low. Vs = Vp × Ns/Np = 6.0 × 300/600 = 3.0 V. The gap: (3.0 − 2.86)/3.0 = 0.047, about 5%. The main cause is flux leakage.

4. Ideal phone charger: 230 V → 5 V, output current 2.0 A. What is the turns ratio Np : Ns? What is the primary current?

46 : 1; Ip ≈ 0.043 A. Np/Ns = Vp/Vs = 230/5 = 46. Power out = 5 × 2.0 = 10 W. Ideal: power in is also 10 W, so Ip = 10/230 = 0.043 A. Step-down: voltage down, current up.

5. A transformer has 200 W in and 12 V, 15 A out. What is its efficiency? How much energy is wasted in 1 minute?

η = 90%; 1200 J wasted. Pout = 12 × 15 = 180 W. η = 180/200 × 100% = 90%. Power wasted = 200 − 180 = 20 W, which means 20 J is wasted each second. ΔE = 20 × 60 = 1200 J, which becomes heat.

6. A power station sends 10 MW. The total resistance of the line is 5 Ω. How much power is lost in the line when sending at 50 kV, and at 500 kV?

50 kV: 200 kW; 500 kV: 2 kW. 50 kV: I = P/V = 10 000 000/50 000 = 200 A, Ploss = 200² × 5 = 200 000 W = 200 kW. 500 kV: I = 20 A, Ploss = 20² × 5 = 2000 W = 2 kW. Voltage × 10, current ÷ 10, loss ÷ 100.

7. A power station sends 10 MW at 330 kV. The total resistance of the line is 5 Ω. A student calculates: Ploss = V²/R = (330 kV)² / 5 Ω. What is wrong?

330 kV is the voltage sent, not the voltage drop on the line. This calculation gives about 2.2 × 1010 W, which is more than two thousand times the 10 MW sent (2.18 × 1010 / 107 ≈ 2200). This is impossible. The V in V²/R can only be the voltage drop IR. The correct way: first find I = P/V, then find I²R.

8. Why does a laminated core reduce eddy currents? Does adding a fan to cool the core help?

The insulation cuts the big eddy-current loops; cooling does not stop eddy currents. Each loop must stay inside one thin iron sheet. It surrounds only a thin strip of flux (small emf), and its path is narrow with a high resistance. So the current and the heat are much smaller. Cooling only keeps the core a bit cooler, and the eddy currents are still there.
