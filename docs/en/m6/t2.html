The Motor Effect

Explained from zero

This chapter covers two things: a current makes a magnetic field, and a wire with a current in another magnetic field gets pushed. You find every direction with your right hand: grip to find the field, hold it flat to find the force. Once you have practised these two hand positions, this chapter is easy.

Warm-up

Know these 4 first

  1. ⊙ = out of the page, like an arrow flying towards you: you see the tip of the arrow. ⊗ = into the page, like an arrow flying away from you: you see the feathers at its tail.
  2. Conventional current: the way positive charge would flow. Out of the + terminal, round the circuit, back to the − terminal. The way the electrons really move is called electron flow. It is exactly the opposite way.
  3. You learned this in Chapter 1: a moving charge in a magnetic field feels a force F = qv⊥B. The force in this chapter is many charges in a wire all feeling this force together.
  4. sin 0° = 0, sin 30° = 0.5, sin 90° = 1. The sin θ in the formula is like a discount: at 90° nothing is taken off, and at 0° the value is cut to 0.
2.1

Magnetic fields made by currents (Year 11 refresher)

Big ideaEvery current makes a magnetic field. Around a straight wire the field lines are circles. A loop or a solenoid makes a field like a bar magnet's.

  • Magnetic field (symbol B): where a magnet or moving charge feels a force. Unit: tesla (T).
  • Field lines: the arrow on a line = the way the N end of a small compass points. Closer lines = stronger field.
  • Uniform field: the same strength and direction everywhere. It is drawn as parallel lines with equal gaps.
  • Solenoid: a long coil of wire with many turns.
  • Permeability constant μ0: a constant that links a current to the field it makes in a vacuum. μ0 = 4π × 10−7 N A−2.

Three shapes, three fields

  • Straight wire: use the grip rule. Grip the wire with your right hand, like holding a stick: thumb along the conventional current, and the curled fingers show the direction of the field. The field lines are circles. They are closer together (stronger) nearer the wire.
  • Wire seen end-on (looking from one end): with the current out of the page ⊙, the field lines are anticlockwise circles. With the current into the page ⊗, they are clockwise.
  • Wire seen from the side: with the current up the page, the field to the left of the wire is out of the page ⊙, and the field to the right is into the page ⊗.
  • One loop (current loop): curl your right fingers with the current. The thumb points out of the N face. Face-on: anticlockwise current = N face, clockwise = S face.
  • Solenoid: the fields of all the turns add. It is like many flat magnets in a row, which become one bar magnet. Inside, the field is strong and uniform, from S to N. Outside, it is like a bar magnet. Curl your fingers with the current, and the thumb points to the N end.
Anticlockwise = N face

Face-on, the current in the loop goes anticlockwise. The field inside the loop is out of the page ⊙. The face towards you is N.

Clockwise = S face

Face-on, the current in the loop goes clockwise. The field inside the loop is into the page ⊗. The face towards you is S.

A solenoid cut in half, seen from the side: in the top row the current is out of the page ⊙, and in the bottom row it is into the page ⊗. The field inside points right, so the right end is N and the left end is S.

2.2

The motor effect: force on a wire

Big ideaA wire carrying a current in an external magnetic field feels a force. The force is largest when the wire is at 90° to the field. There is no force when the wire is parallel to the field.

  • Motor effect: the force on a current-carrying wire in an external magnetic field.
  • External field: a field from something else (such as a magnet), not the wire's own field.
  • Why there is a force: a current is a stream of moving charges. A moving charge in a magnetic field feels a force (F = qv⊥B). The charges cannot leave the wire, so the whole wire is pushed. It is like a bag of marbles: every marble is pushed the same way, the marbles cannot leave the bag, so the whole bag is pushed along.
  • F grows with three things: l (the length of wire in the field), the current I, and the field B.
  • Only the part of the current at 90° to B counts: I⊥ = I sin θ. θ is the angle between the wire and B. The part along B (I∥ = I cos θ) makes no force.

2.2

The motor effect: force on a wire · Direction

Big ideaThe force is at 90° to both the current and the field. Use the right-hand palm rule to find the direction: fingers along B, thumb along I, and the way the palm pushes is F.

  1. Hold your right hand flat.
  2. Fingers along the field B (from N to S).
  3. Thumb along the conventional current I (not the electron flow).
  4. The palm faces the force F. It is like pushing a door with your palm: the door moves the way your palm faces.
  • Example (figure in the booklet): B up, current to the right. Fingers up, thumb right, palm faces you, so F is out of the page ⊙.
  • Reversing: reverse only I, or only B: the force reverses. Reverse both: the force does not change.
  • If the question gives the electron flow: first reverse the direction to get the conventional current, then point your thumb along it.
  • Special case: B into or out of the page. Every wire in the page is then at 90° to B. So the force is always at its largest value lIB. It lies in the page, at 90° to the wire.
  • Example: B into the page ⊗, current to the right. Fingers point into the page, thumb points right, palm faces up, so F is up.
2.3

Forces between parallel wires

Big ideaEach of two parallel wires sits in the field of the other, so each feels a motor-effect force. Same-direction currents attract; opposite currents repel.

  • Force per unit length F/l: the force on each metre of wire. Unit: N m−1.
  • Newton's third law: if wire 1 pushes wire 2, wire 2 pushes back equally hard.

Three steps to find the direction

  1. Grip rule: find the field B1 of wire 1 at wire 2.
  2. Palm rule: find the force on wire 2.
  3. Newton's third law: the force on wire 1 is the same size, in the opposite direction.
Same-direction currents · attract

Both are out of the page ⊙ (wire 1 is 2.0 A, wire 2 is 6.0 A). B1 points up at wire 2, and B2 points down at wire 1. The two forces point towards each other: they attract. The currents are different, but the two forces are the same size.

Opposite currents · repel

Wire 1 is out of the page ⊙, and wire 2 is into the page ⊗. B1 and B2 both point up at the other wire. The two forces point away from each other: they repel.

  • The reverse of charges: like charges repel, but same-direction currents attract.
  • Why: wire 2 is at 90° to the field of wire 1. B1 = μ0I1/(2πr), so F = lI2B1. F grows with I1 and I2, and falls as the distance r grows.
  • Put B1 into this and divide both sides by l. This gives the formula below.

Trap: F/l is in N m−1: multiply by l to get the force. The bottom of the fraction is r, not r2.

2.3

Newton's third law and the SI ampere

Big ideaThe forces on the two wires are always the same size and in opposite directions. This force was once used to define the unit of current: the ampere.

  • Both wires feel the same size of force, even when the currents differ. This is because I1I2 is the same number for both wires (2.0 × 6.0 = 6.0 × 2.0).
  • They form an action–reaction pair: equal and opposite forces that two objects put on each other.
  • The ampere (A) is the SI unit (the international unit, used all over the world) of current. 1 A = 1 C of charge passing each second.
  • From 1948 to 2019, the ampere was defined by this force between parallel wires. Since 2019, it has been defined by the size of the electron's charge.
  • Link to Newton's third law: the definition names one force "between" the two wires, not which wire feels it. Newton's third law makes the two forces equal and opposite, so one value is true for both wires.
Learn the wording

one ampere is the constant current that, flowing in each of two straight, parallel wires of infinite length and negligible circular cross-section, placed 1 m apart in a vacuum, produces between these wires a force of 2 × 10−7 newton per metre of length.

Check: put the values into the formula: F/l = 2 × 10−7 × 1 × 1 / 1 = 2 × 10−7 N m−1. It matches exactly.

2.4

Key experiments

Both investigations use an electronic balance (a digital scale). The balance really measures the force pressing on it, but it shows this as a mass. If the reading rises by Δm, the force has risen by Δm g.

Trap: the balance shows g (grams). First change to kg (÷ 1000), then put the value into the formula. Example: the reading rises by 1.00 g = 0.00100 kg, so F = 0.00100 × 9.8 = 9.8 × 10−3 N.

The force on a wire (current balance)

Aim
To find how the force on a wire depends on the current and the angle.
Method
A magnet sits on the electronic balance. A wire is held by a clamp and passes through the gap in the magnet, at 90° to B, without touching the magnet. A DC supply, ammeter, rheostat (a variable resistor) and switch are in series with the wire. First tare the balance (set the reading to 0). Change I (keep l, B, θ the same). Then reverse I, then reverse B. Finally change θ.
Why
The figure in the booklet: N on the left, S on the right, B points right; the current in the wire is out of the page ⊙. Palm rule: the wire is pushed up. Newton's third law: the magnet is pushed down. So the reading rises by Δm, and F = Δm g (Δm in kg).
Results
F against I is a straight line through the origin: F ∝ I, gradient = lB. F is largest at 90° and 0 when the wire is parallel to B (it follows sin θ). Reversing only I or only B reverses F; reversing both does not change it.
Conclusion
This agrees with F = lIB sin θ and the palm rule. You can also work out B = gradient ÷ l.
Significance
Electric motors, loudspeakers and analogue meters all use this effect.

Two parallel wires (required investigation)

Aim
To show that parallel currents attract or repel, and to test F/l = (μ0/2π)(I1I2/r).
Method
The lower rod rests on insulating blocks on the electronic balance. The upper rod is held above it by an insulated clamp. The two rods are l = 0.50 m long. A flexible link (a soft wire) joins the two rods, so the currents are the same size and opposite: I1I2 = I2. First tare the balance, to set the reading to 0. A low-voltage, high-current DC supply, ammeter, rheostat and switch are in series with the two rods. Use short bursts of current.
Measuring r
r (centre to centre) = the gap, measured with vernier calipers (a tool for small lengths), + one rod diameter.
What to change
Change I; keep l and r the same. Plot F = Δm g against I2. Then change r with spacers to test F ∝ 1/r. Without the link, both rods can carry current the same way.
Results
Opposite currents: the reading rises (they repel, and the lower rod is pushed down). Same direction: the reading falls below 0 (they attract, and the lower rod is pulled up). Hanging foil strips show the same result. F against I2 is a straight line through the origin: F ∝ I1I2, gradient = μ0l/(2πr). The value of μ0 worked out from the gradient is close to 4π × 10−7 N A−2.
Conclusion
Same-direction currents attract, opposite currents repel, and F ∝ I1I2, as predicted.
Significance
Ampère found this force in 1820. It was used to define the ampere as a unit (1948–2019).

Memorise these 6 before the exam

  1. Wire: field lines are circles, B = μ0I/(2πr). Solenoid: uniform inside, B = μ0NI/L.
  2. Grip rule. Straight wire: thumb = conventional I, curled fingers = B. Coil: curled fingers = current, thumb points to the N end.
  3. Motor effect: F = lIB sin θ, only in an external field. Largest at 90°, 0 when parallel.
  4. Palm rule: fingers B, thumb conventional I, palm F (at 90° to both).
  5. Parallel wires: same direction attract, opposite repel; F/l = (μ0/2π)(I1I2/r).
  6. The two forces are equal and opposite (Newton's third law). This force was once used to define the ampere.

Common mistakes

Wrong: for a straight wire, B ∝ 1/r².
Right: B ∝ 1/r. Twice as far away, B is half, not a quarter.
Wrong: L in the solenoid formula is how long the wire is.
Right: L is the length of the solenoid itself.
Wrong: the thumb points along the electron flow.
Right: the thumb points along the conventional current. The electron flow is exactly the opposite way, so reverse it first.
Wrong: the force is largest when the wire is parallel to B.
Right: when parallel, F = 0. The force is largest when the wire is at 90° to B.
Wrong: using the angle in the question directly as θ.
Right: θ is the angle between the wire and B. For an angle measured from "a line at 90° to B", first work out θ = 90° − that angle.
Wrong: the wire's own field also pushes it.
Right: the motor effect only counts the external field (such as a magnet's field).
Wrong: same-direction currents repel, just as like charges do.
Right: same-direction currents attract and opposite currents repel, the reverse of charges.
Wrong: the wire with the bigger current feels the bigger force.
Right: both wires feel the same size of force (Newton's third law). I1I2 is the same number for both.
Wrong: the value you get for F/l is the force.
Right: F/l is in N m−1. Multiply by l to get the force F.
Wrong: putting the balance reading in grams straight into F = Δm g.
Right: first change Δm to kg.

Quick quizClick a question to see the answer

1. A vertical wire has a current going up. Is the field to the right of the wire out of the page or into the page?

Into the page ⊗. Grip the wire with your right hand, thumb up, and your fingers curl round the wire: to the right of the wire, the fingers go into the page; to the left, they come out of the page.

2. A long straight wire has a current of 5.0 A. How big is B at 1.0 cm from the wire? And at 2.0 cm?

1.0 × 10−4 T; 5.0 × 10−5 T. First change cm to m: r = 0.010 m. B = μ0I/(2πr) = 2 × 10−7 × 5.0 / 0.010 = 1.0 × 10−4 T. When r doubles, B halves (B ∝ 1/r): 5.0 × 10−5 T.

3. A wire 0.50 m long has a current of 4.0 A in a 0.20 T field, at 30° to B. How big is the force? What if the wire is turned until it is parallel to B?

0.20 N; 0. F = lIB sin θ = 0.50 × 4.0 × 0.20 × sin 30° = 0.40 × 0.5 = 0.20 N. When parallel, sin 0° = 0, so there is no force.

4. B is into the page ⊗, and the current in the wire goes right. Which way is the force? What if you reverse both the current and B?

Up; still up. Fingers point into the page, thumb points right, palm faces up. When I and B are both reversed, the direction of the force does not change.

5. Two long parallel straight wires carry 2.0 A and 6.0 A in the same direction, 0.10 m apart. Do they attract or repel? How big is the force per metre? Which wire feels the bigger force?

Attract; 2.4 × 10−5 N m−1; they are the same size. F/l = 2 × 10−7 × 2.0 × 6.0 / 0.10 = 2.4 × 10−5 N m−1. I1I2 = 12 is the same number for both wires, and the two forces are an action–reaction pair, so they are the same size.

6. Why does the definition of the ampere only say there is one force "between" the two wires, and not which wire feels it?

Because of Newton's third law: the forces on the two wires are the same size and in opposite directions. So one value (2 × 10−7 N per metre) is true for both wires. Check: F/l = 2 × 10−7 × 1 × 1 / 1 = 2 × 10−7 N m−1.

7. In the current balance experiment, the reading rises by 0.50 g when the current is on. How big is the force on the wire? Which way does it point?

4.9 × 10−3 N, up. Δm = 0.50 g = 0.00050 kg. F = Δm g = 0.00050 × 9.8 = 4.9 × 10−3 N. The reading rises, which shows the magnet is pushed down. By Newton's third law, the wire is pushed up.

8. In the parallel rods experiment, why do you plot F against I²? What does a reading below 0 show?

Because the two rods are in series, so I1I2 = I²; a negative reading shows that the rods attract (same-direction currents). F = (μ0l/(2πr)) × I², so F against I² is a straight line through the origin, with gradient μ0l/(2πr). When the rods attract, the lower rod is pulled up. The force pressing on the balance gets smaller, so the reading falls below 0.
